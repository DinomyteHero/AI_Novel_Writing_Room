They sat around the common room table like strangers at a waystation, which was close enough to the truth. Forty-eight hours into the Unknown Regions, and the crew of the Wandering Star had settled into the careful politeness of people who hadn't yet decided whether to trust each other.

Yara had spread her research across the table's surface: data slates, printed schematics, a worn notebook filled with handwriting so small it could have been code. She was explaining the Celestial construction patterns to anyone who would listen, and Ben was doing his best to follow.

"The structures aren't built," she said, tapping a schematic that showed interlocking hexagonal forms. "They're grown. The Celestials didn't use materials the way we understand them. They shaped spacetime itself into stable configurations."

"Like ice crystals," Ben offered.

"More like coral." She caught his expression. "Coral builds itself according to internal rules, responding to its environment. Each polyp doesn't know what the whole reef looks like. But the reef has a shape. A function." She leaned forward, and the dim light from the overhead panels caught the enthusiasm in her eyes. "That's what the Celestials did. They set initial conditions and let the structures emerge."

Dex spoke from his corner of the room without looking up from the blaster he was field-stripping. "Coral doesn't rearrange hyperspace."

"No. But the principle scales."

"Does it?" Kaia had been silent for twenty minutes, reviewing data from the probe returns on her personal slate. Now she set it down with exaggerated care, the way people handled fragile things. "The Celestial engineering we've studied was always purposeful. Containment structures. Barriers. Things designed to keep other things in or out." Her gaze settled on Yara. "What were they keeping out?"

Yara's enthusiasm dimmed. "That's the question nobody's been able to answer."

"Nobody outside the Archives, maybe."

The temperature in the room dropped by several degrees. Not literally, though the environmental systems had been unreliable since they'd crossed the boundary layer. Figuratively. Ben watched the exchange from his seat at the head of the table, a position he'd taken because it gave him sightlines to both the corridor and the cockpit, not because he wanted to signal authority. Although it probably signaled authority anyway.

"What do you mean by that?" Yara's voice had lost its lecturing warmth. She sounded young and defensive, which was what she was.

"I mean that the Archives contain material on Celestial containment theory that isn't available to university researchers." Kaia's tone was academic. Precise. The kind of precision that was itself a weapon. "I'm curious whether your published work incorporates any of those restricted sources."

"My work is based entirely on publicly available data."

"Then it's incomplete."

"All research is incomplete. That's why we do more of it."

Dex reassembled his blaster with three economical clicks and set it on the table. The sound punctuated the silence like a period at the end of a sentence. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

Ben cleared his throat. "Kaia, if you have restricted material that's relevant to the mission, now would be a good time to share it."

She regarded him for a long moment. He'd noticed that about her: she always paused before answering questions, even simple ones. It wasn't hesitation. It was calculation. She was choosing her words the way a surgeon chose instruments.

"I have theoretical models," she said. "Based on my time in the Archives. They suggest the Celestial containment network wasn't designed to keep something out of our space. It was designed to keep something in."

"In where?"

"Here." She gestured at the viewport, where the shifted stars rippled their slow distortions. "In the wound regions. These aren't scars from the Abeloth crisis. They're gaps in a cage. And we're inside it."

The ship creaked. Environmental systems cycling, probably. Or the hull adjusting to whatever subtle forces played through the local spacetime. Ben chose to believe it was the environmental systems.

"You've had this theory for how long?" he asked.

"Three years."

"Since your Padawan died at Sinkhole Station."

The words landed harder than he'd intended. Kaia's face went still, a controlled stillness that he recognized because he wore it himself when someone mentioned Vestara. The mask you built when the wound was too deep for flinching.

"That's not relevant."

"When a researcher's theory about the place that killed someone they loved becomes the basis for a mission into that same place, it's always relevant."

"My theory is sound."

"I'm not questioning your theory. I'm asking whether your judgment is affected by your personal stake in proving it."

Across the table, Yara had stopped pretending to organize her data slates. She was watching the exchange with the fixed attention of someone who'd just realized the ground beneath her was less stable than she'd thought. Dex had angled his chair so his back was to the wall. Old habit. Also strategic.

Kaia's jaw worked once. Then she picked up her data slate and held it out to Ben. "Read the models. Check my methodology. Ask any question you want about the data. I'll answer them all." Her eyes held his. "But don't reduce three years of work to a grief narrative. That's not what this is."

Ben took the slate. He could feel the weight of the moment, the fragile equilibrium that would tip one way or the other based on what he did next. Push harder, and she'd withdraw behind her walls. Back off entirely, and she'd know he didn't trust her but was too polite to say it.

He chose a third option. "I'll read it tonight. All of it. And tomorrow we'll go through it together, the whole team, and decide whether to adjust the mission profile."

"Together," Kaia repeated, testing the word.

"That's how this works." He set the slate beside his own notes. "Nobody's an island out here. Especially not in a place where the Force only responds to collective willpower."

Dex snorted. "The Force doesn't respond to me at all."

"You sure about that?" Yara had found her voice again. She was looking at Dex with an expression that combined academic curiosity with something warmer. "The readings from the boundary crossing showed a response from all four crew members. Including you."

"I'm not Force-sensitive."

"No. But the wound region doesn't seem to care about that distinction." She pulled up a data display and turned it so everyone could see. Four waveform signatures, each subtly different, each clearly responding to the same external stimulus. "Whatever the Celestials built here, it wasn't designed for Jedi. It was designed for people."

The room went quiet again, but it was a different kind of quiet. Not the brittle silence of people avoiding conflict. Something more contemplative. Dex looked at his waveform signature with the expression of a man being told his reflection did things he hadn't noticed.

Ben leaned back in his chair. The metal frame protested softly. "We have three more days before we need to decide whether to approach the energy sources. I want everyone's assessment on my desk by end of day tomorrow. Kaia's theoretical models, Yara's structural analysis, Dex's tactical evaluation."

"And yours?" Dex asked.

"I'll be reading." Ben tapped Kaia's data slate. "Apparently I have three years of research to catch up on."

That earned a fractional relaxation of Kaia's posture. Not forgiveness. Not trust. But an acknowledgment that the conversation had ended in a place she could live with. It was the most he could hope for at this stage.

They dispersed by unspoken agreement: Kaia to her quarters, Dex to the cockpit for his watch rotation, Yara to the sensor station where the probe data continued its slow accumulation. Ben stayed at the table, Kaia's slate glowing in front of him, the shifted stars visible through the viewport at his shoulder.

He read for three hours. The models were dense, layered, and clearly the product of genuine expertise. They were also exactly the kind of work that a brilliant, grieving researcher might produce when the alternative was drowning: thorough, methodical, and built on a foundation of carefully controlled desperation.

The theory was sound. Kaia was right about that. The Celestial containment network showed every sign of being designed to hold something inside the wound regions, not to keep threats out of normal space. The math worked. The evidence trail, while incomplete, pointed in a consistent direction.

What troubled him wasn't the theory. It was the gap between what the theory explained and what Kaia hadn't mentioned. Her models accounted for the containment structures. They described the mechanism of failure that had created the wound regions. They even predicted the gradient boundary layer they'd crossed two days ago.

But they said nothing about what happened to the containment network's builders. The Celestials had created the cage and then vanished, leaving no note, no instructions, no explanation. Kaia's models were detailed maps of a prison built by ghosts. And she'd chosen to walk inside.

Ben set the slate down and rubbed his eyes. Through the viewport, the stars continued their slow, impossible dance. Somewhere ahead, three energy sources waited in their perfect triangle, patient and precise and utterly silent.

He thought about trust. About how it was easier to extend to people who hadn't earned it than to withhold from people who'd given you reason to doubt. About how his father had said to trust his crew, even the ones he didn't understand yet.

Especially those.

He picked up the slate and kept reading.
