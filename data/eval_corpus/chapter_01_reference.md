The holographic star chart cast blue-white shadows across Luke Skywalker's study, turning the familiar room into something that resembled the inside of a nebula. Ben stood at the edge of the light, arms folded, watching his father trace routes through the Unknown Regions with one finger.

"Three expeditions in the last eighteen months," Luke said, not looking up. "All lost contact within seventy-two hours of entering the wound regions."

"And you want to send a fourth."

Luke's hand paused above a cluster of red markers. Each one represented a ship that had gone silent. "I want to send a team that understands what they're walking into."

Ben studied the markers. They formed an arc, curving away from charted space like the edge of a wound. Which, according to the Jedi Council's latest briefing, was exactly what they were. The Abeloth crisis had left scars in the Force itself, regions where the normal rules didn't apply. Where the Force responded to different laws.

He didn't say what they were both thinking: that understanding and surviving were two very different things.

"Who else?" Ben asked instead.

Luke brought up a new display. Four dossiers materialized in the blue light. Ben recognized the first face immediately. Kaia Rensh, a Jedi scholar who'd spent the last three years buried in the Archives, researching Celestial artifacts. She had the kind of focused intensity that made other researchers nervous.

The second face belonged to someone he'd never met. A Mandalorian, armor stripped of clan markings, which told its own story. Dex Vorn, the file read. Independent security contractor. Former Protector.

"A Mandalorian?" Ben raised an eyebrow.

"He knows the Outer Rim transit corridors better than anyone alive. And he's worked recovery operations in the deep Unknown before."

The third dossier showed a young woman with close-cropped hair and an expression that hovered between eager and terrified. Specialist Yara Tann, xenoarchaeologist. Non-Force-sensitive. Twenty-three years old.

"She's a kid."

"She has more published research on Celestial engineering than anyone outside the Imperial Remnant's classified archives." Luke's voice carried the mild reproach that meant Ben had missed something obvious. "And she specifically requested this assignment."

That gave him pause. People didn't request trips into regions where ships vanished. Not unless they were desperate, crazy, or hiding something.

He filed that thought away and looked at the fourth dossier. His own face stared back at him. Recent capture, probably from the Temple's security systems. He looked tired in it.

"I need someone who won't freeze when things stop making sense," Luke said quietly. "Someone who's seen the Force do things that shouldn't be possible and kept moving."

Ben didn't answer immediately. Outside the study's viewport, Coruscant's evening traffic wove patterns of light against the darkening sky. A dozen levels down, someone was cooking something with too much jogan spice. The smell drifted up through the ventilation system, absurdly domestic against the gravity of what his father was asking.

He'd been seventeen when he helped bring down Jacen. Nineteen when Vestara chose the dark side over him, over everything they'd built together. Twenty-two when Abeloth nearly swallowed the galaxy whole. Each of those experiences had taught him the same lesson: the Force was bigger, stranger, and more dangerous than any framework the Jedi had built to contain it.

And now Luke wanted him to walk into a place where even that framework didn't hold.

"What's the mission profile?" he said, which was as close to yes as he could manage right now.

Luke dimmed the star chart. "Preliminary survey of the nearest wound region, designated WR-7. Three weeks maximum. You establish a base camp at the boundary, send probe droids ahead, document everything. If you encounter active Celestial infrastructure, you map it and pull out."

"And if the wound region doesn't want us to pull out?"

The silence that followed was answer enough. Luke crossed the room and placed both hands on Ben's shoulders. This close, Ben could see the fine lines around his father's eyes, the silver threading through his hair. The Grand Master of the Jedi Order looked old. Not weak. Never that. But old, in a way that reminded Ben that even legends had limits.

"Trust your crew," Luke said. "Even the ones you don't understand yet."

Especially those, Ben thought, but didn't say.

After Luke left, Ben stood alone with the four dossiers still glowing in the darkened study. He pulled up Kaia Rensh's file again and read it more carefully. Three years in the Archives. Specialization in Celestial-era artifacts. A Padawan lost during the Abeloth crisis. He scanned the details: her former apprentice had been stationed at Sinkhole Station when the barriers failed. No body recovered.

People processed grief in different ways. Some built walls. Some built obsessions.

He toggled to the Mandalorian's file. Dex Vorn had left the Protectors five years ago under circumstances the file described as "mutual separation." No criminal record. Extensive independent work in the Unknown Regions. His client list was redacted, which meant either Republic Intelligence had hired him or was actively investigating him.

Probably both, knowing Intelligence.

Yara Tann's file was thinner. Recent graduate from the University of Coruscant's xenoarchaeology program. Top of her class. Three published papers on Celestial construction techniques, all of which Ben skimmed and understood maybe a third of. She'd grown up on Chandrila, a world where the Jedi were legendary figures seen in holodramas, not people you shared breakfast with.

He wondered how she'd handle discovering that legends snored and argued about whose turn it was to clean the refresher.

Ben closed the dossiers and stared at the star chart. The red markers blinked steadily, each one a question mark that used to be a ship full of people. Somewhere beyond those markers lay something the Jedi didn't understand. Something that rewrote the rules.

He'd spent his whole life in a galaxy that ran on certainties. The light side and the dark side. The Force and its absence. Right and wrong, even when the line blurred. But the wound regions promised something different. A place where certainty itself was the enemy.

The ventilation system hummed. The smell of jogan spice had faded, replaced by the clean recycled air of the Temple's upper levels. Somewhere below, the evening meditation was beginning. He could feel it, a gentle tide of collective calm washing through the building's foundations.

He turned off the star chart and left the study in darkness. Tomorrow he would meet his crew. Tonight he would sit with the weight of what he'd agreed to and try not to think about all the ships that had gone quiet.

The corridor outside was empty. His footsteps echoed against polished stone as he walked toward his quarters, passing the meditation gardens where moonlight filtered through the transparisteel dome. A fountain murmured. Water caught the light and threw fragments of silver across the walls.

Ben paused at the garden's entrance. He could feel the other Jedi meditating below, their collective presence a warm pressure at the edges of his awareness. It would be easy to join them. To sink into that shared calm and let it smooth away the edges of his unease.

Instead he kept walking. Some unease deserved to stay sharp.

In his quarters, he pulled up the mission coordinates one more time. WR-7 sat at the edge of the Unknown Regions like a bruise on the galaxy's skin. The charts showed nothing beyond it. Not empty space. Just nothing. The cartographers had left it blank, which was the most honest thing you could do with a place that defied mapping.

He set the coordinates aside and lay down on his bunk without undressing. The ceiling above him was the same pale stone as every other ceiling in the Temple. He'd stared at this one since he was a boy, mapping its faint cracks and imperfections the way other children mapped constellations.

Sleep came reluctantly, and when it did, he dreamed of empty space where the stars should have been.
