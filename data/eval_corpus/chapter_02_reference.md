The Wandering Star dropped out of hyperspace with a shudder that rattled every loose fitting on the ship. Dex Vorn's hands were already moving before the vibration settled, fingers dancing across the navigation console with the precision of someone who'd spent two decades reading ships the way other people read faces.

"Nominal reversion," he said, more to himself than to anyone listening. "Navicomputer's compensating for local gravitational anomalies."

"Compensating how much?" Ben asked from the co-pilot's seat.

Dex checked the readout. The numbers were wrong. Not dramatically wrong, not in the way that meant immediate danger, but wrong in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up. "Point-three percent deviation from predicted exit coordinates."

"That's within tolerance."

"For normal space, yes." Dex pulled up the sensor array. The viewscreen filled with stars, but the star field had a quality he'd never seen before. The light was slightly shifted, like looking through very old glass. "We're not in normal space anymore."

Kaia Rensh appeared in the cockpit doorway. She moved quietly for someone carrying that much equipment: a scanner pack slung over one shoulder, a data slate in each hand, and the faintly manic expression of a researcher who'd just found what she'd been looking for.

"The ambient Force readings are already elevated," she said. "Fourteen percent above baseline and climbing."

"We haven't even reached WR-7 yet," Ben pointed out.

"That's what's interesting." She wedged herself into the navigator's station without being invited and started pulling up the ship's Force resonance monitors. The screens filled with waveform data that looked like a heartbeat drawn by someone with trembling hands. "The boundary isn't sharp. It's a gradient. The wound region doesn't start at a line. It bleeds outward."

Below them, Yara Tann's voice came through the intercom, tinged with the breathless quality of someone trying to be professional about something that terrified and excited her in equal measure. "I'm reading anomalous radiation patterns from the forward array. They match the signatures in the Celestial archive fragments."

"Match how closely?" Kaia asked sharply.

"Ninety-one percent correlation. The remaining nine percent might be instrument drift, or it might be something we haven't catalogued yet."

Dex watched the exchange with the careful attention of someone whose survival instincts had been honed by environments where people's tone of voice mattered more than their words. Kaia's question had come too fast. Not surprise. Confirmation.

He filed that observation alongside the three waypoints in her pre-filed course that didn't appear in any navigational database. Two data points didn't make a pattern. Three was getting close.

The ship's systems chimed as the autopilot adjusted their trajectory. Outside, the shifted starfield rippled like water disturbed by a stone. Ben leaned forward in his seat.

"Did the stars just move?"

"No." Dex checked the navigational fix. "We did. The local spacetime geometry shifted underneath us. Like a current." He'd worked salvage operations in the Maw cluster, where black holes played games with space and time. This felt different. The Maw was violent, unpredictable. This shift had been gentle. Almost deliberate.

Kaia's scanners beeped. She bent over them with an intensity that blocked out the rest of the cockpit. "The Force gradient is increasing exponentially now. We're approaching the outer boundary layer."

"How do you know it's a boundary layer?" Ben's tone was mild, but Dex had been around Jedi long enough to recognize the particular quality of mildness that preceded pointed questions.

"The waveform data shows a clear transition zone. Standard physics." Kaia didn't look up. "It's consistent with every model of Force-active regions we've studied."

It was a perfectly reasonable answer. It was also, Dex noted, not actually responsive to the question Ben had asked. Ben had asked how she knew. She'd answered with what she knew. The distinction might have been accidental. Might.

The ship crossed the boundary layer at 14:37 standard time, and everything changed.

Dex felt it first in his hands. The controls went soft, not mechanically but perceptually, as though the connection between his intentions and the ship's responses had acquired a slight delay. He tested the maneuvering thrusters. They responded correctly. The instruments confirmed normal operation. But the feel was wrong, the way a familiar tool felt wrong in a dream.

"Anyone else feeling that?" he asked.

Ben had gone very still in the co-pilot's seat. His eyes were unfocused in the way that meant a Force-sensitive was perceiving something beyond the visible spectrum. "The Force is different here."

"Different how?"

"Quieter. No." Ben shook his head. "Not quieter. It's like trying to hear a conversation in a room where the acoustics have changed. The same sounds, but they're bouncing off different walls."

Kaia's knuckles had gone white around her data slates. Whatever she was reading, it had stripped away the researcher's composure and left something rawer underneath. She caught Dex watching her and forced her grip to relax.

"The Celestial signatures are much stronger inside the boundary," she said, and her voice was almost steady. "I'm reading at least three distinct sources within sensor range."

"Sources of what?" Yara asked over the intercom. Her voice had lost its breathless quality. She sounded younger now, and scared.

"Structured energy patterns. Artificial." Kaia paused. "Or engineered. The distinction matters when you're talking about beings who could shape stars."

Dex began a standard perimeter sweep, angling the ship in a wide spiral that would give the sensors maximum coverage while keeping them pointed toward open space and a clear exit vector. Old habits. The kind that kept you alive when things went sideways.

The sensor data painted a picture he didn't like. Three energy sources, as Kaia had said, arranged in a pattern that was too regular to be natural. An equilateral triangle, each point separated by exactly forty-seven thousand kilometers. The precision was unsettling. Nature didn't do equilateral triangles. Mathematics did. Engineering did.

"I want to approach the nearest source," Kaia said.

"Not yet." Ben had straightened in his seat, the unfocused look replaced by something sharper. "We follow the mission profile. Establish a base camp at the boundary, send probe droids forward, document everything."

"The probe droids won't be able to read Force resonance patterns. Only a trained sensor operator with direct access can capture this data."

"Then we'll capture it from here with the ship's instruments."

"At this range, the resolution is insufficient to determine whether the sources are active Celestial infrastructure or residual energy signatures. The distinction matters."

The cockpit was very quiet. Outside, the shifted stars continued their slow distortion, rippling in patterns that almost suggested meaning. Dex kept one hand on the throttle and the other near the emergency reversion controls. Whatever decision came next, he wanted to be ready for either outcome.

Ben turned to look at Kaia directly. "We'll deploy the probes first. If the data warrants closer investigation, we approach as a team. Nobody goes ahead alone."

For a moment, something crossed Kaia's face that Dex couldn't read. Then she nodded, a single precise movement, and turned back to her instruments.

"Deploying probe sequence alpha," Dex announced, and launched three droids into the shifted space ahead. They tumbled away from the ship like seeds scattered by a careful hand, their running lights blinking against the distorted starfield.

The telemetry came back strong for the first seven minutes. Standard readings, energy measurements, spatial geometry data. Then, at the eight-minute mark, the lead probe's signal stuttered. Not interference. The signal itself changed character, as though the probe were transmitting from a place where the rules governing electromagnetic propagation had been rewritten.

"Getting coherent data from probe two," Yara reported. "Probe one's signal is degrading. Probe three is returning what looks like structured noise."

"Define structured noise," Ben said.

"Patterns in the interference. Regular intervals. Almost like..." She trailed off. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to something barely above a whisper. "It's like the interference itself is carrying information. Something is communicating through the medium we'd normally filter out as background noise."

Kaia's hands had stopped moving over her instruments. She was staring at the waveform display with an expression that Dex recognized from his years of reading people in dangerous situations. It was the look of someone seeing exactly what they expected to see, and being afraid of that confirmation.

He made a decision. Reached under the console and activated the cockpit's secondary recording system, the one that logged everything to an isolated, encrypted partition that couldn't be accessed remotely. If something went wrong, and in Dex Vorn's experience something always went wrong, he wanted a record that couldn't be edited.

The probe signals continued to degrade. The structured noise grew louder. And somewhere ahead, in the shifted space between three precisely placed energy sources, something waited with the patience of deep geological time.

Dex checked the exit vector one more time. Still clear. Still possible. He held that knowledge close, the way a soldier holds a loaded weapon in unfamiliar territory.

Just in case.
