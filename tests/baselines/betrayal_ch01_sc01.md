The galaxy was not at peace.

Technically, it had not been at peace for forty years—not since a farm boy from Tatooine stepped into a war that should have belonged to other people. But the unrest of this fortieth year of the Alliance’s reckoning had a different grain than rebellion or invasion. Corellia was drawing away from the Galactic Alliance like a continent deciding it preferred to be an island, and the Alliance’s answer—a surveillance apparatus of quiet, institutional thoroughness—had only made the separation harsher. On a dozen worlds, beings who had survived the Yuuzhan Vong by consenting to be governed were asking what, precisely, they had consented to. The Force had no politics, but it carried fear, intention, grievance, and will, and it was not calm. It shifted the way a tide shifts before a storm: not dramatic yet, but no longer neutral. Anyone trained to read it, and honest enough to say what they read, would have reached the same conclusion: something was already in motion.

Luke Skywalker was already awake.

He stood at the balcony rail of their Coruscant apartment with one hand flattened against the durasteel, three hours before the city’s endless running lights would have to share the sky with dawn. Cool air moved in off the upper lanes, faintly metallic, threaded with the low thrum of repulsorlift traffic far below. He had stood there long enough for the chill to settle into his palm without his noticing. He noticed it now. He kept his hand where it was.

The dream had not been, technically, a dream. He had done enough ordinary dreaming since Endor to know the difference. This had been the other kind—the kind with Force-weight behind it, the kind that left residue after waking the way smoke lingered in a room. He had come awake with his eyes open and his breathing measured, the way he woke when the Force was trying to tell him something it did not yet have words for.

Three times in two months. Clearest last night.

The door behind him hissed open, then shut, and he heard the soft cadence of Mara’s bare feet on the balcony tile. She did not ask why he was awake. She came to stand beside him at the rail.

“Tell me,” she said.

Not are you all right, not bad dream. Mara Jade Skywalker had been the Emperor’s Hand before she had been his wife, and long before she met him she had learned the difference between a man standing on a balcony because he could not sleep and a man standing there because he was waiting until he was ready to speak.

Luke kept his eyes on the city. “A figure. Hooded. I couldn’t see the face. Moving through darkness, but not uncertain. Moving toward something specific. There was a sense of—” He stopped, reaching for a word that was not quite purpose and not quite arrival. “Destination,” he said at last. “As if the route had already been calculated and all that remained was to walk it.”

Mara let the silence stretch for a beat. “Carrying anything?”

“Nothing I could see.” He considered the dream again, the way it had clung at the edge of waking. “That wasn’t the point. The carrying wasn’t the threat.”

“The moving was.”

“Exactly.”

She shifted slightly, not turning to face him but angling toward him. Her robe belt hung loose, the way it did when she had tied it in the dark. “How many times?”

“Three. This was the clearest.”

Running lights from a transport lane washed the balcony in amber and white, then slid away. Beneath them, the city kept up its pre-dawn murmur, indifferent.

“Did you recognize the route?” Mara asked. “The direction of motion—was it toward something specific, or just forward?”

That was the question that had waited at the edge of his awareness since he woke. He was glad, without quite admitting it to himself, that she had asked it instead of making him raise it. “Toward something,” he said. “I couldn’t name it. But it wasn’t random. Whoever it was, they knew where they were going.”

“Worse,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

She set both hands on the rail, just beyond his. The balcony was narrow enough that her shoulder hovered close to his without touching. “You haven’t taken this to the Council.”

It was not quite a question. He answered anyway. “Not yet.”

“Because you don’t have anything to give them.”

“Because I don’t have a name.” He heard the distinction as he said it and felt how exact it was. That was what had kept him at the rail since before she joined him. “Mara, I’ve had Force-dreams that were memories. Echoes of things that had already happened. This isn’t that.”

She listened without interrupting. He could feel the quality of her attention—the one she gave operational problems, stripping them down to what carried weight.

“It felt like a warning,” he said. “Not a memory. Something that hasn’t finished arriving yet.”

Wind from the upper lanes passed through the space between them. Somewhere below, a cargo speeder’s repulsors shifted frequency and steadied again.

“Then it’s present tense,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

She was quiet for a moment, and he knew she was not trying to reassure him. She was mapping the shape of the problem, the way he had been mapping it for two months and through most of the night. He was grateful for that. He had not come out here to be told Force-dreams were unreliable, or that three incidents in two months might be anxiety looking for a dramatic path. He knew what this was. He had known since the second time.

“The figure’s patience,” she said. “In the dream—is it the patience of someone who hasn’t decided yet? Or someone who has?”

He had not framed it that way. He turned the question over now, feeling for the texture she meant in the dream’s residue. “Someone who has,” he said. “Someone who has already decided and is simply moving through the interval.”

“Then that isn’t a threat that’s coming,” Mara said. “That’s a threat that’s already started.”

“Yes.”

She did not say then we need to act, or then we need to tell the Council, or anything else that would have put an edge on the problem he could grasp. She simply stood with him at the rail while they looked out over a city that had no idea something patient and unnamed might already be moving within it.

Amber light swept up again from below, slower this time as transport-lane traffic thinned toward the pre-dawn lull. For an instant it caught the line of Mara’s face, then passed on.

At some point in the last few minutes Luke’s breathing had settled without his noticing. Not because the problem had grown smaller—it had not. But some kinds of weight changed when they were no longer carried alone. He did not turn to look at her. He kept his gaze on the running lights and the dark intervals between them, and he thought about a hooded figure walking a route already chosen. He did not know the name. He would not pretend he did.

He had been right to tell her first. Right to stand here until the words were ready. Right about what this was.

He was not right about what to do with it yet. That belonged to a day that had not started.

Mara’s hand on the balcony rail beside his, neither of them speaking.
