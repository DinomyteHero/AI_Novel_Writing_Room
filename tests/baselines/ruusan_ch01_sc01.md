The training blade cracked across his shoulder.

Ben gave ground and brought his guard back up on reflex, the sting settling deep into the joint—sharp, clean, and about three days overdue. Rendell had been building to that combination since Tuesday. Ben had seen it every time. This was the third time he’d answered a half-beat late.

“Again?” Rendell asked.

“I’m good.” Ben thumbed off his training saber. The hum cut out. “Call it.”

Rendell paused—a Knight’s pause, brief and polite—then shut down his own blade without comment. He was good at that, Rendell. Knowing when to leave something alone. Ben had trained with worse partners, worse moods, worse evenings.

“Long week,” Ben said.

“Yeah.” Rendell clipped the hilt to his belt. His face didn’t say he bought it. His mouth didn’t challenge him, either. That was the courtesy: one Knight letting another keep his own story intact. Ben felt it land under his ribs.

Rendell collected his gear and headed out. His boots crossed the salle floor, then faded into the corridor. In their place came the Temple’s late-shift noise—lift chimes, distant voices bleeding around corners, the low mechanical rasp of a cleaning droid somewhere down the hall.

Ben stood in the empty salle and heard none of it.

His shoulder throbbed. He rolled it once, then again, easing out the sting. Not the problem. He’d taken worse hits half-asleep, and the muscle wasn’t what was talking to him now.

He walked to the middle of the floor.

The room’s Force presence was there. It always was in the Temple, sunk into the walls and the floor and the long memory of every sparring match, meditation session, and private disaster this room had held. Ben had trained here since he was twelve. The Force in this salle should have been as familiar as the recycled-air tang in the back of his throat.

Except it wasn’t arriving when it should.

He went still for a moment, not reaching, not testing. Just listening. The wrongness was there. It had been there all week, patient as a bruise he couldn’t stop pressing. It ran under the room’s rhythm the way a second beat runs under the first—close enough to almost fit, off enough that it never quite did. His body expected the Force the way it expected the floor beneath his boots. The wrongness made it feel as if the floor had drifted a fraction late.

He drew his training saber and left it dark.

The opening sequence of Shii-Cho was reflex. He’d run it ten thousand times if he’d run it once: broad strikes, wide pivots, the basic geometry his hands knew before his thoughts caught up. He moved through the form with the blade unlit, the dead hilt cutting the pattern through the air. His feet landed where they should. His weight shifted on cue.

The room lagged behind him.

Not his muscles. His muscles were fine. The sequence stayed clean, angles true, each position arriving exactly where years of practice had put it. The wrongness wasn’t in his body. He’d suspected that. He’d even hoped for it, in the stupid private way hoping for bad news was still hoping.

He came into the fourth position and held.

There.

The beat where intention and the Force’s answer should have been the same thing, the way they always were, the way years of practice taught a Knight’s body and the room to move together until the distinction hardly mattered. The beat where his grip should have tightened with that answer already under it—and instead there was only his own hand on the hilt, and then, a half measure later, the confirmation came in like an echo from the wrong direction.

He lowered the saber.

First he’d blamed fatigue. The Abeloth aftermath had left stranger residue than bad timing. Then he’d blamed perception—the specific skew that came from pushing too hard for too long, the nervous system lodging a complaint. Then he’d blamed the week itself, the cumulative humiliation of losing three times to a combination he should have read in his sleep.

He wasn’t blaming any of that anymore.

The wrongness wasn’t in him. It was in the field—in the Force itself, the layer under his own reach, the ground everything else stood on. It had sat there all week like a note played slightly flat, and he’d been compensating without admitting he was compensating. Tonight Rendell had caught him in the middle of that adjustment and tagged his shoulder for the third time, and Ben was done pretending the numbers worked.

He clipped the training saber back to his belt.

The room had gone very quiet. Out in the corridor, the cleaning droid rounded the corner, brushes whispering over the floor—a small domestic sound, completely indifferent to whatever was wrong with the Force. Beyond the high windows, Coruscant’s light pressed against the transparisteel in amber and gray, the city still grinding through another night shift. Somewhere below, people were eating late, missing appointments, arguing over speeder permits. The galaxy kept moving.

The wrongness sat under all of it, patient and steady, not fading the way trauma residue faded, not sharpening the way a personal crisis sharpened. Just there. Directional, almost, if he let himself lean that far into it. Like a sound that had to be coming from somewhere.

He pressed the heel of his hand to his sternum, an old habit from childhood he’d never managed to explain and had stopped trying to. Checking for something. He still couldn’t have said what.

The wrongness was real. It was outside him. He’d been right all week, and being right felt exactly as good as it should have.

He stayed in the empty room another moment, long enough to let the admission settle—not a revelation, not a crisis, just a fact waiting for him to stop arguing. The half-beat lag in the room’s rhythm. Real. Consistent. External. Not going away.

He turned toward the door.

Luke was standing there with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
