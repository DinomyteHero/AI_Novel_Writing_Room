Register is Allston dialogue-forward — performance and banter carry the discovery half; precise combat choreography carries the action half. # Legacy of the Force: Betrayal
**Franchise**: Star Wars (Legends EU)
**Era**: Legacy of the Force era / post-New Jedi Order consolidation (40 ABY). Galactic Alliance is the ruling power; Jedi Order headquartered at the rebuilt Jedi Temple on Coruscant; Corellian independence movement reaching breaking point.
**Tone**: dark_gritty
**POV**: Multi-POV ensemble with a clear tragic-protagonist spine. Jacen Solo is the nearest thing to a primary POV (appears across the book's investigative, philosophical, and tragic strands) but Allston rotates through at least six named POV characters per chapter-group, often switching mid-chapter at an untyped scene-break. Principal POVs in rough order of page count: Jacen Solo > Ben Skywalker > Luke Skywalker > Mara Jade Skywalker > Han Solo > Leia Organa Solo. Supporting POVs include Nelani Dinn (Ch 1-2, 32-33), Lumiya (ch 23-29 villain spotlight), Wedge Antilles (Corellian-front chapters), Aidel Saxan and Thrackan Sal-Solo (political chapters), Admiral Klauskin (Ch 15-16 midpoint), and brief glimpses through several others. No prologue. No epigraph. Third-person limited, scene-break conventions destroyed by the EPUB conversion but visible empirically from POV shifts.

## Premise
**What If**: What if the hero of the Yuuzhan Vong War, the Solo son who held back from the dark path the first time it was offered, took a single step across the line when a Sith survivor of the Emperor's cadre showed him a vision of himself dying to save the galaxy — and took that step not for power, but for love?
**CDQ**: When Jacen Solo glimpses, through the Force, the one future in which the galaxy survives — a future in which he must sacrifice his own innocence to prevent catastrophic outcomes — will he commit the first irreversible act that dark-side logic requires, and will his family be able to see him do it?

## Conflict
**Antagonist**: Twin antagonist lines. The exterior antagonist is the Corellian independence movement and its fallback engine Centerpoint Station — orchestrated by Corellian Prime Minister Aidel Saxan, Governor-General Thrackan Sal-Solo, and assorted insurgents who do not want the Galactic Alliance's surveillance regime imposed on their system. The interior antagonist is Lumiya — the last surviving Emperor's Hand and Dark Lady of the Sith from the late Rebellion era — who has studied Jacen for years, identifies him as the Sith student whose moral sensitivity will let him finish her Order's work, and begins a patient seduction that culminates in his first dark-side commitment. Subordinate antagonist: Darth Vectivus, dead Sith whose asteroid training nexus Lumiya uses to walk Jacen through the argument that a Sith with genuine love does not fall — an argument the book pointedly does not refute.
**Motivation**: The Corellian movement's motivation is local, political, and legitimate on its face: they will not be policed by the Galactic Alliance, they will not surrender Centerpoint Station for disarmament, and they consider the new GA surveillance apparatus indistinguishable from the old Empire. Lumiya's motivation is theological and long-patient: the Sith Order she belonged to died with the Emperor; she has spent thirty years studying the galaxy's next generation of Force-users and has concluded that Jacen Solo — a Jedi who killed his own aunt during the Vong war and did not fall, a young man capable of grief and love — is the student her teachers would have wanted, the one who can take the Sith path without becoming a monster. She does not want a throne; she wants an heir.
**Lock-in**: The Adumar investigation in Chapters 1-2 pulls Jacen and Ben into a Corellian paramilitary operation that they cannot walk away from without betraying either Jedi protocol or their own convictions. Once Jacen kills the Dammant Killers in the Ch 2 raid and experiences Nelani Dinn's disappointment in him, the mission has consumed his Jedi-neutral cover — he is now a GA-aligned combatant in an undeclared war. That commitment deepens at Toryaz Station (Ch 18-19) when Lumiya arranges for Mara's oldest friend Tresina Lobi to die in the bombing, and again when Jacen accepts the GAG commission that binds him to Cal Omas. By Ch 28 he has no institutional exit — only the philosophical one Lumiya offers.

## Theme
**Premise**: The most dangerous falls to the dark side are the ones made out of love — and the hardest lies for a moral person to detect are the ones told in their own voice.

## Cast
- **Jacen Solo** (Primary POV and tragic protagonist — Jedi Knight, son of Han and Leia, undergoing his first-step dark-side commitment): Measured cadence. Long sentences when explaining, short when deciding. Thinks in forks — 'the future where X' versus 'the future where Y'. Uses 'Uncle Luke' and 'Master Skywalker' in the same conversation depending on which frame he is operating in. Rarely profane. Never shouts.
- **Ben Skywalker** (Co-protagonist and thematic foil — Luke and Mara's thirteen-year-old son, Jedi apprentice navigating the pull of the new Galactic Alliance Guard): A thirteen-year-old's cadence — direct, slightly blunt, occasionally too-fast. Uses 'Mom,' 'Dad,' 'Jacen,' 'Uncle Han' without ceremony. When he asks Jacen a philosophical question he does not know he is asking one. Does not swear; does crack small dry jokes, usually at his own expense.
- **Luke Skywalker** (Grand Master of the Jedi Order; moral constant; dreams of a dark enemy he cannot identify): Spare. Short sentences. Rarely a figure of speech where a direct statement will do. Uses 'Master' as a form of address for his peers, 'apprentice' for Ben. Never 'I think,' often 'I sense.' When speaking to Ben he drops Master entirely. Does not laugh often; when he does, the book notes it.
- **Mara Jade Skywalker** (Jedi Master, former Emperor's Hand, Luke's wife and Ben's mother; the only character in the book reading Lumiya correctly): Dry, precise, unsentimental. Calls Luke 'Skywalker' when teasing and 'Master' never. Calls Ben 'kiddo' once per chapter. Calls Jacen 'Solo' when they disagree. Has the sharpest of the ensemble's senses of humor; the book lets her land one joke per chapter and then gets out of her way.
- **Han Solo** (Co-POV; husband of Leia, father of Jacen; the book's Corellian conscience): Harrison-Ford cadence, sixty-two-year-old version. Still contractions always, still short declaratives. Calls Leia 'Princess' when he is teasing and 'Sweetheart' when he is genuinely happy. Calls Jacen 'kid' even now. Calls Thrackan 'cousin' with visible effort.
- **Leia Organa Solo** (Co-POV; former Chief of State; Jedi Knight in training; the book's reluctant third-way operator): Still crisp, still diplomatic, more willing than in her youth to say the unpleasant thing directly. Calls Han 'flyboy' once per book and it lands each time. With the Council she is formal. With Mara she is blunt. Dry sense of humor; never shouts.

## Special Mechanics
**Rule**: The Force shows fragmentary, interpretable futures. A strong precognitive can see multiple forking outcomes simultaneously and must choose which interpretation to act on; the choice itself is morally loaded, because the act of choosing which future to close is a use of power, not a reading of destiny.

## Style Constraints
- Multi-POV ensemble with frequent in-chapter POV shifts; scene-break markers lost in EPUB conversion must be restored empirically from POV shifts.
- Allston's dialogue-forward cadence — characters talk their way through setups that other authors would narrate.
- Aerial combat written with Rogue Squadron precision when present; fighter and freighter choreography is meticulous.
- Dry humor threaded through violence — a scene can contain both a killing and a bad pun without tonal rupture.
- Jedi/Sith philosophical dialogue is extensive but never prolix — Lumiya's seductions are short paragraphs, not monologues.
- Adult register throughout; violence and political cynicism are frankly shown without gratuitous dwelling.

## Character Voices
### Jacen Solo
**Role**: Primary POV and tragic protagonist — Jedi Knight, son of Han and Leia, undergoing his first-step dark-side commitment
**Voice**: Measured cadence. Long sentences when explaining, short when deciding. Thinks in forks — 'the future where X' versus 'the future where Y'. Uses 'Uncle Luke' and 'Master Skywalker' in the same conversation depending on which frame he is operating in. Rarely profane. Never shouts.
**Surface**: Quiet, focused, articulate. Physically present in a room the way Luke is — confidence without swagger. Most comfortable in a conversation that has moved two levels past politeness. Dresses simply, travels light, declines honorifics. Has Vergere's habits of answering questions with questions; prefers to let the person he is talking to reach the answer themselves. With Ben he is warm, almost paternal. With Nelani he is collegial. With Luke he is reverent but quietly withholding.
**Under Pressure**: Calculates. Will out-think, out-feel, and out-wait an opponent rather than fight them. In physical confrontation he fights with an economy that is unnerving — Dammant Killers in Ch 2 are dispatched one-and-done. When the stakes become personal he will act decisively without consultation: his choice to accept Lumiya's teaching in Ch 28 is made in thirty seconds of interior silence, and his choice to kill Nelani in Ch 33 is made even faster. The dangerous version of competence.

### Ben Skywalker
**Role**: Co-protagonist and thematic foil — Luke and Mara's thirteen-year-old son, Jedi apprentice navigating the pull of the new Galactic Alliance Guard
**Voice**: A thirteen-year-old's cadence — direct, slightly blunt, occasionally too-fast. Uses 'Mom,' 'Dad,' 'Jacen,' 'Uncle Han' without ceremony. When he asks Jacen a philosophical question he does not know he is asking one. Does not swear; does crack small dry jokes, usually at his own expense.
**Surface**: Slight, serious, watchful. Short hair, plain tunic, never wears his inheritance on the surface. Asks practical questions that adults have forgotten are questions. When Jacen teaches him something, he wants to know why it is true, not just how to perform it. Has the Skywalker stubbornness; has his mother's operational competence in the field. Will follow Jacen into a missile factory at thirteen and stay calm through it.
**Under Pressure**: Watches carefully, speaks carefully, acts when asked to. Has a child's deference to Jacen that the book trades on: when Jacen is present Ben defers, and when Jacen is absent Ben reasons. By the back half of the book he has started to distinguish Jacen's Jedi teaching from something else he cannot name. The Galactic Alliance Guard uniform is offered to him (Ch 24-27) and he does not quite refuse, which will matter in the next book.

## Voice Rules (MANDATORY)

**POV**: Third-person limited, multi-POV ensemble. Scene-level POV ownership with frequent in-chapter rotations between principal POVs (Jacen, Ben, Luke, Mara, Han, Leia). Secondary POVs (Nelani, Lumiya, Klauskin, Saxan, Thrackan, Wedge) are used sparingly and are always clearly owned within their scenes. No omniscient narration beyond the book-opening atmospheric paragraph.
**Register**: Allston's dialogue-forward pulp register — NOT literary-novel register. Scenes built as conversations with minimal interior reflection; when interior comes, it is tightly focused on a single question. Paragraphs average 2-4 sentences in dialogue scenes; a 5+ sentence interior-reflection paragraph is a flag. Sentences are economical — no more words than the beat needs. When a beat would work as a 5-word sentence, write the 5-word sentence. Combat choreography is Rogue-Squadron-precise and diegetically paced. Political dialogue is functional, not exposition-heavy. Humor is dry, rarely drawn attention to, and frequently delivered through the Solo voices. Metaphors for threat or damage use thematic-abstract nouns Allston actually uses (pain, ignorance, denial, certainty, loyalty, ruin), NOT modern-literary architectural abstractions ('structural damage', 'damage shape', 'the kind of damage that changes the shape of what comes after'). When a character describes an ominous vision, let them say 'he brings great pain to the galaxy' — direct, plain, in-character. Reserve elevated prose for the book-opening cosmic paragraph only.

### Reference Authors:
- **Aaron Allston**
  - Emulate: Dialogue-forward scene construction; ensemble POV piloting; dry, integrated humor; fighter and freighter combat written with the precision of an actual pilot writer; political worldbuilding delivered through argument rather than exposition.
  - Avoid: Don't over-lean on the humor register when scenes are darkening — the book's late chapters are genuinely grim and Allston lets them be grim without relief.
- **Timothy Zahn**
  - Emulate: Sith-politics complexity and the treatment of Force-using antagonists as thinkers rather than screamers — the Lumiya voice in particular should read as considered, articulate, patient.
  - Avoid: Don't let any Sith or Jedi voice become a lecture; Lumiya wins the reader's attention by restraint.

### Narrative Voice:
The book-opening paragraph is a rare omniscient flourish — a cosmic-scale 'hyperspace' panorama that pulls down into Luke's Coruscant bedroom. The rest of the book is strict third-limited. The narration never editorializes on Jacen's moral slide; it shows the slide and lets the reader name it. Characters act; the narration reports what they did. Never narrate a character's body making a decision their mind did not ('he hadn't chosen to sit up, hadn't chosen to speak — his body had made both decisions while some other part of him still stood inside the vision'). That construction is modern-literary and outside Allston's register. If Luke sits up from a vision, the narration says 'Luke sat upright in bed' and the next sentence is what he said or what he did. Character interiority is short and externalized — a sentence of reflection followed by dialogue or action, not a paragraph of psychological cataloging.

### Anti-Slop Rules — Do NOT violate these rules:
- No mid-book revelation of Lumiya's identity — the book manages the reveal gradually; the text never tips who the dark enemy is before Jacen meets her on the asteroid.
- No Jedi-on-Jedi rhetorical beatdowns; philosophical disagreements are resolved (or not) at the level of a short exchange, not a monologue.
- No in-dialogue fan-service for earlier Allston books — Wedge references are earned by the Corellian plot, not by nostalgia.
- No 'chosen one' or 'prophecy' framing of Jacen's fall — the book explicitly rejects prophecy structure in favor of choice.
- No romance-on-page between Jacen and Tenel Ka in this book — their relationship is referenced but not enacted.
- No abstract-architectural metaphors for damage or threat ('structural damage', 'damage shape', 'the kind of damage that changes the shape of what comes after', 'the contours of what follows'). When a character describes a vision of an enemy, use plain language Allston would write: 'he brings great pain to the galaxy' / 'he'll bring ruin' / 'he'll hurt the Order.' Thematic-abstract nouns are allowed (ignorance, denial, certainty, loyalty); modern-literary architectural nouns are not.
- No body-vs-mind meta-narration: never write that a character's body decided something independent of their mind ('he hadn't chosen to sit up, hadn't chosen to speak — his body had made both decisions'). Allston's characters act. The narration reports what they did. If Luke sits up urgently, write 'Luke sat upright in bed' — not a paragraph about his body's agency separating from his mind's consent.
- In a scene built primarily from dialogue, limit narration paragraphs between dialogue beats to 3 sentences or fewer. Longer interior paragraphs are rare and load-bearing (a revelation, a backstory brief, a scene-level transition). A dialogue scene where narration paragraphs regularly run 4+ sentences has drifted out of Allston register.
- When introducing a named character or in-universe concept a first-time reader would not know (Raynar Thul, the Killiks, the Nest, the Yuuzhan Vong war, Nom Anor, Abeloth), brief the reader in the narration — ideally in the same paragraph that names them — with a short expository aside. Example from the source: 'Raynar Thul, former Jedi Knight, presumed dead during the Yuuzhan Vong war, had been discovered a few years earlier — horribly burned during the war, mentally transformed in the years since through his involvement with the insectoid Killik race.' Don't assume reader knowledge of series continuity; Allston earns accessibility through exposition-in-narration.
- Preserve Mara's sardonic deflection voice. When Luke escalates gravely, Mara often deflects with a dry needle — 'if he brings pain to the galaxy, you're obviously going to feel it' / 'Wonderful. My favorite kind of threat.' These deflections are load-bearing character texture and punctuate Luke's gravity. Don't skip them; don't replace them with sympathetic reassurance.
- Anchor domestic scenes with specific physical detail: named objects with origin stories (the crude Tatooine sandglass statuette of Luke sent by a child; Mara's spy gear kept somewhere Luke isn't told about; lightsabers on the headboard shelf, older grips worn where her hand closes), wall racks of practice weapons, the disorder of hair or clothes, the specific dimness of the chamber. These objects are Allston's primary character-building tool — each scene needs its layer.
- No vague sensory metaphor for Force phenomena ('moving through the Force the way a person moves through a room with purpose, with direction, with intent'). Allston writes Force descriptions as either plain statement ('it was coming to me through currents in the Force') or specific fragment ('wrapped up in shadows — a dark hooded cloak, but more than that, shadows of light and ignorance and denial'). The thematic nouns do the lifting, not the metaphor structure.

### Banned Structural Patterns:
- No prologue and no epigraph — the book opens on POV chapter 1.
- No Jedi Council omniscient-narration scenes — council sessions are written from a specific POV's seat.
- No 'Sith laugh' tropes — Lumiya never laughs in villainy.
- No 'Emperor's return' hooks — the book is explicit that Palpatine is dead and Lumiya is his heir by philosophical descent only.
- No literary-novel interiority: long abstract-metaphorical paragraphs cataloging a character's psychological state. Allston's interiority is short (1-3 sentences), concrete, and usually externalized through dialogue or action on the next beat. When a character is upset, the next beat is what they DO about it, not another paragraph examining how they feel about it.
- No over-explaining a Force vision: Luke describes the vision in dialogue once, Mara probes it with specific questions, Luke answers them. Do not then give the narrator a second pass explaining what Luke 'really' meant or what the vision 'really' is. The reader assembles the meaning from the dialogue.

### Magic / Force Description Guidelines:
Phenomenological rather than mechanistic. 'He sensed' over 'he read.' Precognitive visions described as fragmentary and forking rather than sequential — Jacen sees branches, not a movie. Lightsaber combat is described at the body level (breath, footwork, angle of cut) rather than the saber-color level. Dark-side uses are named in the narration (e.g., 'a dark-side application') when a neutral reader would not know, without the character acknowledging the naming.

### Franchise Terminology Notes:
Speak of the Old Republic only as history. The Clone Wars and the Empire are historical. The New Republic is recent history. The Galactic Alliance is the current order. The Jedi Order is actively operating. The Sith are a historical order with at most one or two operational practitioners alive. Mandalorians exist but are not foregrounded in this book. Vergere is dead and referenced by Jacen; Anakin Solo is dead and referenced by the family; Chewbacca is dead and referenced by Han (the book does not resurrect him).

### Pacing Feel:
