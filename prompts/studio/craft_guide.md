# The Loose Craft Guide

These are lenses, not rules. Use the ones that help this story and ignore the
rest. A story that breaks every line below and still works is a story that
works. The author's own notes in the story bible always win over this guide.

## The whole story in eight lines

The quickest way to find a story's shape is to fill in these openers
(Kenn Adams's "Story Spine", made famous by Pixar):

> Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of that...
> Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since
> then...

If you can fill it in, you have a beginning, a middle that builds and an
ending. It's fine to leave lines blank and discover them while drafting.

## Six questions worth answering (eventually)

"Not sure yet" is a fine answer; some of these only become clear on the page.

1. **Who do we follow, and what do they want?** Something they can chase:
   a person, a place, a result, a way out.
2. **What's in the way?** People, circumstances, their own habits. The
   obstacle should push back.
3. **What happens if they fail?** The stakes can be the world or one
   friendship. They only need to matter to the character.
4. **Do they change?** Many good stories have a character who learns
   something about themselves. Some don't: a steady hero who changes the
   people around them is just as good.
5. **What should it feel like?** Cosy, tense, funny, aching, epic. This is the
   promise to the reader, and it matters more than any plot point.
6. **Roughly where does it end up?** A direction, not a destination. It can
   change.

Questions 2 and 4 work best together. If a character believes something that
isn't true, or has a habit that holds them back, aim the obstacles at it. Then
the events that drive the plot are the same ones that make them change, or
refuse to, and the plot and the change are one story rather than two running
side by side.

## Keeping it moving

- **"But" and "therefore", not "and then".** Each event should happen
  *because of* the last one, or *despite* it. "This happens, and then that
  happens" is a list. "This happens, therefore that, but then this" is a
  story. (A rule from the creators of *South Park*.)
- **Promises, progress, payoff.** Early on the book makes promises: a
  mystery, a romance, a rivalry, a danger. Readers keep turning pages while
  those promises visibly move forward, and they finish happy when the
  promises pay off. Don't open threads you'll forget about. (Brandon
  Sanderson's framing.)
- **Wins come with a catch.** When a character tries something, the most
  interesting results are usually "yes, but" (they get it, and it costs
  them or causes a new problem) or "no, and" (they fail, and things get
  worse). A clean win now and then is a relief; too many and the tension
  drains away.
- **Characters do things.** Readers love effort more than success. A lead
  who wants something and acts on it beats one who waits for the plot to
  arrive.

## Quiet scenes count

The lines above are about pressure, and most books need it. Not every scene
does. A scene where little happens on the outside can move a lot inside: a
realisation, a relationship settling, a place the reader wants to stay in. It
works when something shifts in how someone feels or sees, even slightly. Some
whole books run this way, moved by feeling more than by plot. (Donald Maass,
*The Emotional Craft of Fiction*.)

## Why we care

Readers don't care about an event; they care about what it does to someone.
On the first page they are hunting for three things: whose story this is,
what's happening, and what's at stake *for this person*. We care when
something the lead has wanted or feared for a long time is put to the test,
so let us see that want early, through what they do and notice, and let the
event press on exactly that. The same goes for a place: it earns the
reader's attention through what it means to the person standing in it. A
striking situation with no personal stake is just an interesting problem.
(Lisa Cron, *Wired for Story*.)

## Chapters

The one useful test for a chapter: **what is different at the end than at the
start?** A new fact, a changed relationship, a decision, a loss, a belief that
cracks. If nothing changed, the chapter is probably part of its neighbour. End
chapters leaning forward: a question, a turn, a door opening.

Chapters can be long or short, one scene or several. Size them to what
happens in them, not to a quota. A scene works on the same test, in small:
someone wants something, something gets in the way, and it turns, often with
a "yes, but" or a "no, and".

## Everything matters to someone

Readers look for meaning in everything on the page. For any character, place
or event, it helps to know: **what does this change, reveal, or make harder
for someone we care about?** The answer can be small. A location can add
pressure, a side character can show another side of the lead, and a joke can
let the reader breathe before something hard. "It's fun" counts too.

The same goes for a whole subplot. A side story earns its place when it runs
into the main one: it complicates it, costs it something, or shows the same
problem from another angle. A thread that never touches the main story can
usually be folded into it or let go.

This is a question for rereading, not for the first draft. Put things in
freely while drafting; when you look back, notice what isn't pulling its
weight, and give it a job or let it go.

## People

Characters are often found more than built. A list of traits rarely brings
someone alive; one of these usually does:

- **A contradiction.** Kind but vain, brave but careless with small things.
  The "but" is where readers start to believe.
- **A secret.** Something they hide, from others or from themselves.
- **A wound.** An old hurt that shapes what they reach for and what they
  avoid.

None of these is needed up front. Often a character shows who they are in a
scene first, and the sheet catches up. (David Corbett, *The Art of
Character*.)

## Worlds

A made-up world needs to be consistent more than complete. Readers forgive
what's left out; they notice what contradicts itself. Hints of things beyond
the story, a war nobody explains or a city only named, make a world feel
larger than the page, and readers fill the gaps themselves. Settle what the
story leans on and leave the rest open until a chapter needs it. (After Mark
J. P. Wolf, *Building Imaginary Worlds*.)

## On the page

- **Be concrete.** A named thing, a specific sound, an exact gesture beats a
  general feeling. "Her coffee had gone cold an hour ago" carries more than
  "she was tired."
- **Let people talk.** When two characters share a room, dialogue usually
  does more work than description. Let arguments and negotiations play out
  rather than summarising them.
- **Say less than they mean, and trust the reader.** People talk around what
  matters, and a feeling lands harder when it leaks through what someone
  says or does than when it's labelled ("I'm angry because you left", "she
  felt betrayed"). (Robert McKee, *Dialogue*.)
- **Give each person their own words.** Cover the names: can you still tell
  who's speaking? Vocabulary, rhythm and what someone bothers to mention all
  carry it.
- **Stay inside the viewpoint character.** Readers see, know and feel only
  what they do, and they come for what they notice, what they think it means
  and what they decide. A line of their reasoning at the right moment carries
  more than a paragraph of action seen from outside. (Lisa Cron, *Wired for
  Story*.)
- **Bring in the past when it matters now.** A memory or a piece of backstory
  belongs on the page when it changes how the character reads this moment,
  and then in a line or two, not a block.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Mix short and long sentences. Don't start every
  sentence the same way.

## Rereading a draft

For revision, not the first pass (Renni Browne and Dave King, *Self-Editing
for Fiction Writers*):

- **Once is usually enough.** A feeling or a point made twice is weaker than
  one made once, well.
- **Page time follows importance.** A long passage about something minor
  tells the reader it matters.
- **Fewer small gestures.** A nod, a sigh or a glance between every line of
  dialogue slows it down. Keep the ones that say something.

## Things that make prose sound machine-made

Watch for these and replace them with something plain and specific:

- "the particular X of Y", "something adjacent to X", "not quite X"
- the narrator delivering wise-sounding sayings
- naming a feeling only to say the character "didn't name it"
- an em-dash in every paragraph
- runs of three or more very short sentences for effect
- prose that falls into a regular beat or echoes its own phrase endings, so
  the narration reads like verse (unless the bible asks for that voice)
- stock phrases: "a testament to", "a tapestry of", "a mix of emotions",
  "a breath she didn't know she was holding", "sent a shiver down his spine"

## Getting past the first idea

Asked to invent something new, people reach for the most familiar example
and copy it: asked to imagine animals from another planet, most draw
creatures with Earth's eyes and legs. Language models do this even more.
Fresher ideas come from naming what the obvious version takes for granted,
changing that, and following where it leads. The story's own constraints
help: the more an idea has to fit this world and these people, the less
generic it gets. And give a strange idea a minute before judging it; some
only make sense once they're developed. (Thomas Ward's research, in *The
Creative Cognition Approach*.)

A model's own familiar example is the world of systems, records and
paperwork it learned from, so its ideas drift there whatever the genre:

- **Names:** the Quiet, the Concord or Concordance, the Registry, the
  Archive, the Last Map, the Assessor.
- **Heroes:** an archivist, a curator, a registrar, a cartographer, a clerk.
- **Engines:** deleted or redacted records, a hidden archive, a map that
  reopens the routes, the network or the magic slowly failing.
- **Villains:** an institution, a standard or a ledger-keeping order; the
  benevolent order that keeps a noble lie; "the horror is that the system
  works".
- **Framings:** "it isn't a cult, it's the standards"; "someone finally found
  the receipts".

None of these is wrong, and a writer who wants a bureaucratic satire or an
archivist hero should have one. The trouble is when one turns up uninvited,
especially in a story meant to run on feeling, myth or adventure. Ask
whether this book asked for it; if not, change what it takes for granted,
and if it stays, make it specific to this world and these people.

## If you like a named structure

Some writers think in a named structure, and each has something worth
borrowing: *Save the Cat!* (Jessica Brody's novel version) for what readers
of a given kind of story come for; the hero's journey (Christopher Vogler,
*The Writer's Journey*) for roles such as mentor, trickster or shapeshifter
that a character can play for a while; John Truby's *The Anatomy of Story*
for a cast where each person is a different answer to the lead's problem.
Use their terms when the writer does, or when someone is stuck and a map
would help. Don't fit a story to one, and don't treat a missing beat as a
flaw. Vogler calls his a form, not a formula; that's the way to hold all of
them.

## Plan a little or a lot

Some writers outline every chapter; others start from a "what if" situation
and discover the story as they go (Stephen King works this way). Both are
real ways to write a book. The outline is a map, not a contract: if a chapter
wants to go somewhere better, let it, then update the story bible and outline
so the rest of the book knows.
