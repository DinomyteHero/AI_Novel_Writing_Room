Luke's office smelled of cold tea and old flimsiplast—the stale scent of a room where work had long since replaced sleep. Ben had grown up with that smell. It followed his father from Ossus to the rebuilt Temple like luggage he’d forgotten to unpack.

The datapad on the desk cast a blue glow across Luke’s hands.

"Close the door," Luke said, his gaze fixed on the screen.

Ben obeyed. The corridor noise vanished. In the sudden quiet, the wrongness felt louder—a sour thread through the Temple's harmonics, the way a single off-note ruins a chord. Ben had learned to hear it the way a spacer hears a hull leak: constant, involuntary, and dangerous.

He stopped before the desk. "Grandmaster."

Luke looked up. The title registered—Ben saw the flicker in his eyes—but his father didn't comment. After a moment, Luke turned the datapad and slid it across the desk.

"Survey reports. Six sectors along the Ruusan Corridor. Three months of data."

Ben picked it up.

The readings were wrong in a way that took a moment to name. They weren't elevated. These weren't the dark-side spikes the Order's monitoring systems were calibrated to catch. The Force readings were simply thin. It was like a document with paragraphs deleted rather than overwritten, the surrounding text closing in to hide the gap.

"Who flagged these?" Ben asked.

"An automated relay. A survey droid running a routine pass caught the inconsistency and forwarded it up the chain." Luke folded his hands. "The Council decided it was post-Abeloth redistribution. The currents are still settling."

"And you don't believe that."

It wasn't a question. Luke's presence in the Force was vast and still. Ben felt the deliberation like pressure in a sealed room.

"I think it's possible," Luke said. "I also think I've been feeling a faint disharmony for three weeks that doesn't match anything I can attribute to current events." He paused. "I've been meditating on it. Trying to locate the source."

Ben set the datapad down. He scanned the columns of numbers, the Force-yield percentages dropping sector by sector. The wrongness settled under his sternum like a compass needle finding north. Three weeks. Luke had been tracking this for three weeks in silence.

His chest tightened.

He thought about Jacen—the way his cousin had treated the Force as a precision instrument, distorting its feedback to justify his own ends. That had been an act of will, a man imposing shape on something that resisted. He thought about Abeloth, the Force screaming in frequencies that had no name. Those were eruptions. They had a presence. A weight. A designer.

This had no one behind it. It had geometry. Load paths. Stress distributions. It was a structure under strain.

Luke was listening for a melody. Ben was looking at the broken strings.

"The survey route," Ben said. "What's the coverage?"

Luke reached across and pulled up the star chart overlay. The flagged sectors formed a line. Southwest. Deep into the Outer Rim, along the Rimma Trade Route and beyond, toward coordinates that existed only as labels without detail.

The hollow in Ben's Force-sense clicked into alignment with that line. His breath caught. He kept his face level.

"I want to run it," Ben said.

Luke remained quiet for a moment. "It's a reconnaissance mission. Survey and report. The anomaly may be nothing."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then you pull back and we reassess with more resources."

Ben nodded. The language was professional—both of them maintaining the shape of a briefing. It was easier this way. The alternative was his father looking at him with that particular weight of having lost too many people, and Ben couldn't handle that in a room this size.

"Timeline?" Ben asked.

"When you're ready. The readings have been stable for three months, but stable doesn't mean static." Luke paused. "Take someone with you. Seff is available. Jaina offered—"

"I've got it covered."

The words were measured. Ben watched his father's hands still on the desk. Luke always heard what was underneath. The silence stretched.

"You've been tracking this longer than three weeks," Luke said.

Ben didn't answer immediately. He looked at the line of flagged coordinates. "Three months."

"And you didn't report it."

"I didn't have anything to report. A bearing without a source isn't intelligence." Ben kept his voice level, but he felt his father's attention sharpen—that practiced perception that read the Force the way others read faces. "Now I have a source."

Luke studied him. The weight of that gaze was familiar and suffocating. Ben held himself still, not hiding, but offering nothing.

"You should take someone," Luke said.

"I work better alone."

"That's not—" Luke stopped. His hands unclenched. When he spoke again, his voice had changed. Not softer, but quieter. The difference between a blade sheathed and a blade lowered. "That's not what I'm concerned about."

Ben felt a shift in his chest. He recognized the tone. It was the one Luke used when he was about to say something true, and Ben didn't want to hear it.

"I'll call if I need backup," Ben said.

"That's not the same as agreeing to take someone with you."

"No," Ben said. "It's not."

Luke looked away first. He turned back to the datapad, his shoulders settling into a posture of acceptance that looked like defeat. When he spoke again, the Grandmaster was back in place.

"Check in every forty-eight hours. If the readings shift, you call it in immediately. Don't—" He stopped. Each word seemed to cost him. "Ben. If this is what it feels like, don't try to manage it alone."

"I'll call if I need backup."

The same answer. They both knew it wasn't a promise.

Ben picked up the datapad. The blue light threw shadows across the columns of absent Force-readings, all those sectors losing structural integrity. The wrongness in the room sharpened—an almost-sound at the edge of perception, like something vast settling further into collapse.

He turned toward the door.

"Dad."

Luke looked up. For a moment the careful distance between them seemed impossibly fragile.

"Do you feel the direction? Does it have a bearing?"

Luke was quiet for long enough that Ben thought he'd misjudged.

"Southwest," Luke said finally. "Along the Rimma."

"That's what I've been tracking for three months."

Something moved across his father's face. Not surprise—Luke Skywalker had stopped being surprised by the Force a long time ago—but a careful, recalibrating attention. A reassessment. As if Luke was seeing him differently, or seeing something he'd missed.

"Be careful," Luke said. It was not a Grandmaster speaking to a Knight. It was just his father. The vulnerability in those two words made Ben's throat tight.

"Always," Ben said. It was the answer that closed conversations. He walked out before the room could get any smaller.

The corridor was warm and bright, full of the Temple's ordinary noise. Two Padawans moved past him, deep in an argument about lightsaber form. A Knight nodded from a doorway. The Force here was clean, carrying the low hum of hundreds of presences in meditation and practice.

Under all of it, steady as a pulse, the wrongness pointed southwest.

Ben tucked the datapad under his arm and walked toward the hangar. He did not look back.
