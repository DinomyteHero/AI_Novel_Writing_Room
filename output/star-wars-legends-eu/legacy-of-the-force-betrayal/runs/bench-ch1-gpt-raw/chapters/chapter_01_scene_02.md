The turbolift doors slid open and warm air rolled over Jacen Solo, Ben Skywalker, and Testan ke Harran, carrying the odor of labor, propellant, high explosives, fresh composites, machine lubricant, and worker sweat. Not rot. Not battlefield death. Industry. The kind that killed at a distance and filed invoices afterward.

Jacen stepped out first.

His cloak lifted behind him in the updraft from the shaft and settled as he strode onto the observation catwalk. Boot heels rang on the metal decking. He did not hurry. He did not look back to see whether Ben and Testan were following. Leaving them half a pace behind was part of the inspection. In Cartann, in a place that built warheads for clients who liked their accounting deniable, a Jedi did not merely arrive. He arrived like he expected people to notice.

Below him spread a manufacturing floor the size of four hangar-maintenance bays set side by side, split into eight lanes by waist-high duracrete barriers and moving under hard white light. Conveyor belts ran luminous and pale through each lane. Workers in hazmat suits handled head-sized green spheres with exquisite care at one station, dipping sensors into them and sealing ports as if they were cradling eggs that might explode from embarrassment. Farther down, crews plugged cable bundles into guidance units and watched black-and-white monitor feeds flicker and crawl. Beyond that, technicians with rubber-headed hammers tapped missile-casing housings in a musical rap, listening for flaws.

Everything had rhythm. Belts advancing. Hands lifting. Heads turning at the right interval toward supervisors’ stations. Security patrols moving with bored regularity along the edges. The place had discipline. It also had a pulse that skipped somewhere out of sight.

“Imprassive, ah?” Testan ke Harran said from behind him. “Best plant in Cartann. Maybe best in saction.”

Testan had dressed for visitors. His orange-and-purple diamond-patterned tunic flowed over gold trousers; a matching scarf lay just so around his throat; his black beard shone as if each hair had been negotiated with personally. He sounded relaxed. His body did not. A small stiffness sat in his shoulders, and his attention kept flicking to Jacen and away again, as if he could not quite decide which one was more dangerous.

Ben trotted up to Jacen’s left and glanced over the rail. “He says it’s impressive.”

Jacen kept his face grave and faintly detached, as if Testan’s Basic required interpretation by someone younger and less burdened by official responsibility. He understood the Adumari accent perfectly well. But there were uses for being thought picky and uses for being thought theatrical, and today he was trying to be both.

Testan gave Ben a look. “I did not need transla—”

Ben cut in with all the innocence of a thirteen-year-old who knew exactly what he was doing. “No, but it helps the official mood.”

Jacen let his gaze travel across the floor. Workers looked up, then looked down fast when they saw the robe and lightsaber. A few didn’t look down fast enough. The postwar reputation of the Jedi had become many things in many systems. Here on Adumar, where pilots still told stories the way other worlds told prayers, it carried a fresh layer of the supernatural each year.

Testan spread a hand toward the nearest lane. “As you can see, ar workars observe all safaty protocols. That rumor of substandard handlin’—ridiculous.”

Ben leaned toward him and squinted up at the beard. “Do you brush it with butter?”

Testan blinked. “What?”

“The beard. It’s very shiny.”

“It’s conditionad.”

“Oh. Right.” Ben nodded seriously. “That was my second guess.”

Jacen moved on, letting the exchange continue behind him. Ben was better at this than most adults. People tried to decide whether he was a harmless child, a privileged Jedi apprentice, or a nuisance. While they were deciding, they stopped being careful.

He walked the catwalk’s curve and looked down lane by lane. Guidance package assembly. Propellant housings. Shell-casing checks. Final crate loading farther down, partly screened by a partition wall and the angle of the bay. Security doors at either end of the floor. The eastern relay point was where it had been in the plant schematics, but the feel of the place around it was wrong. Like a map with a fold in it. A little pressure off to the side where the official layout had decided not to say something.

Testan caught up, beard glinting under the lights. “You are vury quieet, Jadai Solo.”

Ben translated dutifully. “He says you’re very quiet.”

“I heard him,” Jacen said.

Testan’s brows rose. Ben looked wounded for half a heartbeat, then recovered. “That’s disappointing. I was doing really good.”

“You were doing something,” Jacen said.

Ben brightened. “I’m writing that down as praise.”

Testan gave them both a quick uncertain glance, still not sure how much of this was an act. Good. Uncertainty was useful.

Jacen stopped at the rail over the third and fourth lanes. Two hazmat workers lowered a luminous-green sphere into a rack. Another crewman at a nearby monitor station looked up, saw Jacen watching, and missed a cable seat on the first try.

The wrongness in the place tugged again.

He let his attention widen, not reaching so much as allowing the currents around him to sort themselves. The workers below were focused, tired, wary of mistakes. The guards were professionally irritated by inspection duty. Testan was performing local pride over a layer of watchfulness and, beneath that, an increasingly frantic effort not to think about something off to his right and out of sight.

“That station is for sensar calibration,” Testan said. “Black-and-white monitor gives cleanast faedback. Color can obscure micro-shifts.”

Ben translated again, even though Jacen had not asked. “He says black-and-white is better than color.”

“I know,” Jacen said.

“Right.” Ben folded his arms. “I’m mainly here to admire the beard.”

Testan sniffed. “You are an odd boy.”

“I get that a lot.”

Jacen resumed walking. The catwalk’s metal vibrated faintly through his boots with the passage of the belts below. He let his steps fall a little harder, more official, more impatient. Testan followed. Ben drifted at Testan’s elbow, asking questions at random intervals, some useful, some ridiculous.

“Are those lots of twenty-four?” Ben asked, nodding toward a stack of marked shipping cartons in the open loading section.

“Some lots, yas.”

“Is twenty-four lucky on Adumar?”

“No.”

“Good. That would make accounting weird.”

Jacen half heard it. Most of his attention had turned to the pressure point. It wasn’t a visual thing at first. More like the shape of omitted thought. Personnel moved around a blank in the internal map with the easy habit of those trained not to notice what they were avoiding. Testan’s awareness brushed it each time Jacen steered the group nearer the far side of the chamber, then jerked away again. There was discipline in that. Training, maybe. Fear, certainly.

He slowed near a section of catwalk where the wall looked no different from the others: broad gray panels, industrial, clean enough to pass a cursory inspection. Below, a casing team kept tapping housings with their rubber mallets, the notes quick and hollow. To his left, Ben was asking Testan if the beard required its own travel case.

“Do not be absurd,” Testan said.

“So that’s a yes.”

“It has no case.”

“Dedicated shelf?”

“Boy—”

The pressure sharpened. A sliver of thought escaped Testan’s control: supervisors in dark blue jumpsuits hustling out, not by this catwalk, not by any marked access lane; a broad gray door descending from above; the abrupt clean line where it met deck plating; relief after it shut.

Jacen stopped.

“There is something here,” he said. “Something wrong.”

Those were his first words to Testan since the tour began. They hit the air with more weight than volume.

Testan went very still. “Wrong?”

Ben looked at Jacen at once, humor gone. That was another useful thing about Ben. He shifted gears without discussion.

Jacen let the silence stretch. He turned his head slowly toward the wall panel. Testan followed the motion despite himself. There. Another flicker. The door again, broader this time, and the thought attached to it: not now, not now, not now.

Jacen started walking.

This time the pace was not theater. He moved fast enough that the ring of his boots quickened into a hard metallic cadence, his cloak swinging wide around his legs as he ate up the distance. Testan made a small sound and hurried to keep up. Ben came with them, already reaching for the hilt at his belt.

“Jadai Solo,” Testan said, voice thinning. “Whar you are going is only wall.”

Jacen stopped in front of the panel. Up close it was exactly what it had seemed from the start: painted metal, flush seams, factory-standard surface scoring near the lower edge. But the pressure behind it had shape now. Crates. Empty air where the plant map denied empty air. Armed men moving toward this point from two routes. Fast.

He looked at Ben. “Your lightsaber.”

Ben blinked. “My practice lightsaber?”

Testan made a strangled noise. “Practice?”

“From a certain point of view,” Jacen said, “he does practice with it.”

Ben grinned once, fast, and drew the weapon.

Its blue blade snapped into being with a hiss that cut across the clatter of the place below. Workers glanced up. A few froze outright.

“Meter and a half circle,” Jacen said. “Center chest height.”

Ben nodded and stepped in. There was no hesitation in him now, only concentration. He set the blade to the panel and began cutting. Metal spat bright sparks. The air filled with the hot tang of scorched durasteel. A glowing line traced a circle through the panel, steady and neat despite the awkward angle.

Testan backed away. “You cannot do this. This is private proparty. This is—”

The cut finished. Ben planted a boot against the panel and shoved.

The circle tipped inward.

It fell with a resonant clang into darkness beyond and bounced once, revealing a hidden passage packed floor to shoulder height with transport containers. Gray. Stenciled. Each stack neat, each crate marked DANGER, DO NOT DROP and DAMMANT KILLER MODEL 16. Twenty-four to a lot.

For half a heartbeat the place seemed to stop around them.

Then alarms began.

Not klaxons. Boots. Shouted orders. The slap of men breaking into a run from both ends of the concealed passage.

Ben stared through the opening. “Well,” he said. “That seems bad.”

“It seems useful,” Jacen said.

Testan had gone pale under his beard. “You have no authority—”

Jacen turned his head slightly toward him. “You can continue that argument with a magistrate.”

The first six security troops appeared from the far bend of the hidden route in orange uniforms and green carapace armor, blasters already up and spacing good enough to show training. Their commander was shouting before he cleared the angle.

“Down! Weapons down!”

Jacen ignited his lightsaber.

Green light flared in the opening, reflected off the passage walls and the curves of trooper armor. He stepped through the cut panel and put himself between Ben and the oncoming squad.

“Stay behind me,” he said.

Ben was already moving to do it. “That part I can manage.”

The first volley came in disciplined pairs, center mass, low-high variation meant to challenge a blade line and force movement backward. Competent. Not cartoon villains. Plant security who had drilled for internal breach scenarios and meant to keep breathing.

Jacen advanced anyway.

He walked, blade turning in a butterfly pattern before him, folding and opening through the narrow passage so the green glow crossed itself in quick shifting arcs. Bolts struck and glanced away. The geometry mattered. Passage width, crate stacks, armor angles, the risk of sending a shot into missiles rather than men. He adjusted in fractions.

“Could you speak up?” he called. “I’m a little deaf.”

Behind him Ben snickered once.

That was enough. Tension loosened in the exact right place.

Jacen caught the next bolt high and tilted it into the commander’s helmet ridge. The man dropped as if his knees had been cut, not dead, just out. A second shot he turned low into a trooper’s shoulder plate, spinning him sideways into his own line. Two others fired a beat late and hit their comrades instead. The opening seconds ended with four orange-and-green figures down, two from redirected fire, two from their squad’s bad timing.

The survivors did not break. They pulled back, trying to widen their spacing in a place that would not let them. More boots hammered closer from the rear.

“Recording,” Jacen said.

Ben had the expensive little holocam out already. He edged sideways enough to film past Jacen’s shoulder into the revealed passage, sweeping over crate markings, the cut panel, fallen security men, and the far approach where a second wave was forming.

“Um,” Ben said to the device, “this is Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight Jacen Solo and I are in a secret part of the Dammant Killers place in Cartann. You can see the DAMMANT KILLER MODEL 16 containers back there, and their guys are trying to kill us, which usually means people know they’re not supposed to be there.”

A bolt snapped past Jacen’s ear and scorched the paneling.

Ben lowered the holocam a little. “Do I sound too cheerful?”

“No,” Jacen said, catching the next shot and sending it into the passage wall just above a trooper’s head. “You sound admissible.”

The second wave charged. Six again. Better commander this time; they came low, using the fallen as partial cover, trying to shoot under Jacen’s blade and force him into retreat over the threshold where Testan still stood frozen and useless.

Jacen slid his front foot half a step left, changed the pattern, and kept moving forward. A blaster bolt struck his blade near the hilt. Heat bit into his hand. He accepted it and killed the energy there rather than throw the shot aside into the crate stacks. Pain flared, sharp and local. Acceptable.

Ben saw the hit. “You all right?”

“Yes.”

“You sure?”

“Yes.”

“Okay. Very reassuring style.”

Another bolt came low. Jacen turned it up into the passage ceiling, showering sparks onto the nearest trooper. The man flinched, fired reflexively, and caught the soldier beside him in the chest. Jacen swept the next shot into a knee plate. The trooper spun and went down hard.

“Move,” Jacen said.

Forward was no longer useful. Not with reinforcements massing deeper in the route and workers below the cut panel starting to scatter on the visible floor outside. They had the evidence. They needed more evidence, then a route out that didn’t leave Ben pinned against a missile cache.

He backed through the opening in two steps, blade still working, and turned right onto the catwalk at a run. Ben followed, holocam in one hand, lightsaber in the other. Testan had recovered just enough to flatten himself against the paneling and stay out of the way.

“Stay with us if you want to live,” Ben told him.

Testan looked offended and terrified in equal measure. “I was planning that anyway.”

The catwalk curved to the far end of the chamber where a second section overlooked another production bay through a broad interior opening. Security troops below were converging on ladder wells. Some workers were bolting for exits; others had dropped to the deck beside their stations, hands over heads.

Jacen kept the green blade angled low as he ran. The place’s shape came clear to him now: the hidden passage had bridged this chamber and the next, bypassing official traffic routes. Smuggling line built inside a legal line. Efficient.

Ben lifted the holocam again as they reached the rail overlooking the second assembly chamber. “Still recording. Secret route connects to another assembly area. There.” He zoomed on stacked shell casings moving down a luminous belt, on packing stations, on another security squad pouring through a side hatch. “If anybody edits this later, please leave in the part where they keep trying to shoot us. It adds context.”

“Very professional,” Jacen said.

“I learned from the best.”

“You’ve met my mother.”

“That too.”

A volley came from the hidden passage behind them and from the side hatch across the second chamber. Crossfire. Better. More dangerous.

Jacen planted himself at the rail and opened the butterfly pattern wider, body turning from hips and shoulders while his feet held the line. Bolts came from two directions now, and each decision took place in the width of a breath. Send this shot into the paneling. Take that one dead on the blade. Return the next at armor, not at the worker crouched behind the packing console. The command squad across the chamber was trying to drive them away from the rail and into the open run of the catwalk with no cover.

Ben tucked himself in the pocket off Jacen’s right shoulder and kept filming. “I think they’re camera-shy.”

“They may object to the evidence chain.”

“Or to me narrating.”

“That too.”

A bolt sparked off the rail near Ben’s hand.

He pulled the holocam in closer. “Definitely objecting.”

Jacen risked a quick look over the lower chamber. Conveyor lines. Shell casings on the nearest belt, opalescent and empty, moving toward a small portal opening at the far end where completed sections were fed into the next phase of assembly. Narrow. Human-sized only if the human committed to the idea.

There was a future where they held this rail another ten seconds and got boxed in from both directions. A future where a deflection went wrong and struck explosives. A future where Ben hesitated. That last future was the least likely.

“Over the rail,” Jacen said. “Nearest belt. Run for the end portal.”

Ben looked once, measured the drop, and clipped the holocam back to his belt. “Dad says you never want to fight the enemy on ground he’s chosen if you can avoid it.”

“Your father is right more often than he admits.”

“He admits it a lot, actually.”

“Go.”

Ben vaulted.

He went over the rail cleanly, small and quick, dropping to the moving conveyor belt below with a jarring bend of knees that made Jacen’s own legs answer in sympathy. The belt carried opalescent shell casings toward the portal in a steady glide. Ben windmilled once, recovered, and sprinted with the line rather than against it.

Troopers across the chamber adjusted at once and opened fire on the belt.

Jacen cut down three shots in quick succession, knocked a fourth into the packing station’s support strut, and then vaulted the rail after Ben. The drop hit hard through his boots. The conveyor moved under him, trying to steal balance. He rode it a half step and ran.

Bolts carved sparks from the machinery around them. Ben was already halfway to the diminutive portal, ducking under a low guide bar and leaping a seam where two belt sections met.

“Little warning next time,” Ben called.

“That was the warning.”

“I meant earlier.”

Troopers from the hidden passage had reached the rail above and were firing down now, adding a third angle. Jacen kept his blade close to his body, compact work now, no room for broad flourishes. A shot came in near the hilt again. He caught it there and absorbed the heat into the blade rather than let it ricochet into the shell casings. His burned hand protested. Still acceptable.

Ben dropped to one knee at the end portal, twisted sideways, and shoved himself through. For a moment only his boots stuck out. Then they vanished.

Jacen was three strides behind him when the surviving troopers finally got the range and began stitching blasterfire across the conveyor belt where they had just been running. He ducked, reached the portal, and squeezed through after Ben while the shots kept slamming into the line they had just vaulted.
