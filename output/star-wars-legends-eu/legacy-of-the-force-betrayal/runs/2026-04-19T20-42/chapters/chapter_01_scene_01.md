The galaxy was not at peace.

It had not been at peace for a generation, not in any way that mattered. The Yuuzhan Vong had come and altered everything they touched—worlds, populations, the very shape of grief—and the New Republic that had endured them had renamed itself the Galactic Alliance and behaved as if names could heal wounds. In the Corellian system, five planets were in the process of deciding whether they believed that. Along the outer shipping lanes, GA surveillance pickets sat on jump points that Corellian freighter captains had used freely for forty years, and the freighter captains were not pleased, and their government was less pleased, and the word sovereignty was turning up in communiqués where it had not appeared in decades. At the Jedi Temple on Coruscant, Grand Master Luke Skywalker had read three of those communiqués before bed and slept badly, which was not the same thing as sleeping over them.

He stood at the balcony rail as pre-dawn gathered—not sunrise, not yet, but the gradual brightening of a city that never truly went dark. Coruscant’s sky shifted through deep amber and gray. The speeder lanes were already thickening toward morning, running lights laying down the same corridors they had traced the night before and the night before that. Luke kept one hand flat on the transparisteel rail. Cool air moved against his face and into the loose collar of his shirt. He had been standing there long enough for the cold to creep through him.

He did not go back inside.

The residue of the night stayed with him. Not a chain of images he could put in order, but an aftermath: certainty without content. He had seen a hooded figure in a darkness he could not place or map, and the Force had registered it the way it registered a drawn blade nearby. Not information, exactly. More the body’s knowledge before the mind caught up.

Behind him, he heard Mara. Her steps on the apartment floor. The brief pause while she gathered her robe. The whisper of the balcony door opening. She came to stand at the rail without touching him, which meant she had already read the line of his shoulders.

“You’ve been out here a while,” she said.

“Yes.”

She waited. That was one of the things about Mara: she could wait without turning it into a performance. She simply stood in the cool air with her robe belt loose, watching the speeder lanes build themselves back toward morning, and Luke knew she was giving the conversation its shape before he filled it.

“I had it again,” he said.

“The same one.”

“The same one.” His hand stayed on the rail. “Hooded figure. Moving through darkness. I couldn’t see the face—I don’t think it was meant to have a face. It wasn’t that kind of vision.” He paused. “There was a sense of arrival. Something already in motion, and not slowing for anything.”

Mara was silent for a moment. “How long have you been having it?”

“Three times in the last two months. Last night was the clearest.”

She did not tell him it might be nothing. He appreciated that more than he could have explained.

“When you say arrival,” she said, “do you mean the figure was coming toward you, or toward something else?”

The precision of the question made him stop and think. He had been treating the vision as a warning about himself, or the Order, or some threat gathering at the edge of the galaxy—and Mara had put a finger on a point he had not fully examined.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Toward something. A destination. It wasn’t—” He stopped, then tried again. “The figure wasn’t hunting. It was moving with a purpose it already had. The way you move when you know where you’re going and have known for a long time.”

“Patient.”

“Patient,” he said. The word fit. He had carried that patience out of the half-sleep the way he carried the chill now, as an ambient fact. Whatever the figure was heading toward, it had been heading toward it for years.

Warning, he thought. Not memory.

He heard the word as if he had spoken it aloud a beat earlier and only now understood what it meant. Memory would have let him set the vision aside, file it with the other things the Force had shown him that he had not yet understood. Warning meant the threat was present. Warning meant something was already in motion.

He turned his head and looked at Mara. She was watching the city, amber speeder-lane light moving over the planes of her face, and he could see she was running through the problem the way she ran through any tactical assessment—without announcing the process.

“You’re taking it seriously,” he said.

“You take it seriously. That’s enough for me.”

“I might be wrong.”

“You might be,” she said, with no special inflection. “You usually aren’t, about this kind of thing.”

It was not comfort. It was an operational judgment, delivered with the same dry precision she brought to everything, and it was more useful than comfort would have been. Luke felt the weight of the vision settle into something denser, something he could carry. Not resolved—nothing was resolved—but no longer only his.

“I haven’t told the Council,” he said.

“I know. You’re telling me first.” A beat. “Good.”

The sky lightened by degrees. Below the balcony, morning traffic was filling in—cargo haulers, commuter pods, the occasional military shuttle on a perimeter check, lights blinking in strict regulatory sequence. Luke watched a line of transports arc along an upper lane and thought of the Corellian communiqués, then let them go, because they were not what the vision was about. He was fairly certain they were not what the vision was about.

The trouble was that he could not be certain of anything else.

“When I was younger,” he said, “I thought the Force would get clearer. That the more I learned, the sharper the signal would be.”

“And?”

“The signal gets sharper. The thing you’re trying to identify stays the same distance away.”

Mara made a sound that was not quite a laugh—more acknowledgment than amusement, as if the observation was accurate and mildly annoying. “Skywalker. You’re describing every intelligence problem I ever worked.”

“I know.” He almost smiled. “I keep thinking there ought to be a Jedi answer to that.”

“There probably isn’t.”

“No,” he said. “Probably not.”

They stood there. Running lights washed the balcony in a slow amber rhythm. Cool air moved past the transparisteel and between them. Luke did not know what was hooded and patient and already in motion somewhere in the Force, and he could not yet name it or place it or act on it, and those facts were simply true. He had said warning aloud. He had heard Mara not dismiss it. The vision was real enough to share, which meant it was real enough to take seriously, which meant that somewhere in the galaxy—on some world or ship or station he could not yet place—something was heading toward a destination it had held in mind for a very long time.

He did not know what it was. He did not know when he would.

Mara’s hand on the balcony rail beside his, neither of them speaking.
