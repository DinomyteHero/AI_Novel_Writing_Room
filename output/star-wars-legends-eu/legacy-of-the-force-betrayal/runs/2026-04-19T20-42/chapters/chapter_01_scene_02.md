Heat rolled off the service pad in slow pulses, and Jacen Solo had been counting them.

He lowered the macrobinoculars. Two hundred meters downslope, the factory’s loading area squatted behind the perimeter fence: durasteel walls, a pair of repulsorlift cranes folded idle, and four guards locked into a rotation that hadn’t changed in the forty minutes he’d watched. Two on the main approach. One on the fence line. One at the bay doors, not pacing at all, which suggested either boredom or a fixed post worth assigning. The crates stacked beyond the doors were reason enough. He had already seen one load moved, a gray-uniformed crew handling it with the easy efficiency of people who had done this before and expected to do it again.

They wouldn’t.

“Guard on the east fence just changed direction,” Ben said. He had the second set of macrobinoculars pressed to his face, his breath fogging the eyepiece a little with every exhale, as it had for the last twenty minutes without his noticing. “Third time. He’s not just walking a line—he’s checking something at the corner post.”

“Sensor relay,” Jacen said. “He’s resetting it manually. The automatic cycling is broken.”

Ben thought that over. “How long has it been broken?”

“Long enough for him to know the interval by memory.” Jacen set the macrobinoculars in the dry scrub beside him and studied the factory with his own eyes. Through the Force he could feel the facility’s rhythm: the guards’ boredom, the thin thread of anxiety from the crew inside, the heavy, inert pressure of the crates. Missiles had no presence in the Force the way living beings did, but the intent built around them did. “Twenty minutes between resets. He’ll be back at that corner in twelve.”

Beside him, Nelani Dinn hadn’t spoken in several minutes. She sat with her knees drawn up, robe pulled close against the dry wind, the hem gone gray with dust sometime in the last hour. Her attention stayed on the loading area with the patience of someone who had already reached a conclusion and was waiting to put it into words.

She chose the moment now.

“We should call it in,” she said. “Local authorities, GA security liaison, proper documentation. We have the facility location, and we have the supply-chain documentation from the Adumar port records. That’s enough for a warrant.”

Jacen let a beat pass before answering. He watched the guard at the bay doors shift from one foot to the other.

“A warrant takes six hours at minimum,” he said. “Probably twelve, with the liaison backlog.”

“The protocol exists for reasons.”

“The protocol exists for situations where the cell doesn’t have a departure window.” His voice stayed level. “They moved a load two hours ago. They’ll move another before dawn. If we hand this to the GA security apparatus now, it will still be filing authorization requests when the next shipment clears the system.”

Nelani was silent for a moment. Not convinced—he could feel that in the slight tightening around her mouth, the stillness of her shoulders—but still listening. He waited until she had finished considering, then went on.

“The Jedi Code doesn’t require us to be ineffective. It requires proportion.” He flicked another glance toward the loading area. “Three Jedi Knights against a paramilitary crew of eight—nine, counting the one inside who hasn’t come out since we got here. The Force advantage is ours. The window is now.”

“The Code also requires us to operate within the law of the jurisdiction we’re in,” Nelani said. Her tone remained professional, and he respected that. She wasn’t making it personal. “Adumar is a signatory to the GA security protocols. We are not authorized to conduct an interdiction operation on Adumari soil without their government’s clearance.”

“No,” Jacen said. “We’re not.”

He let the acknowledgment stand between them for a moment.

“The future where we wait,” he said, “the cell moves the missiles tonight. We file the documentation. The GA liaison opens a case file. Three weeks from now, someone on Corellia gets a shipment we could have stopped today, and the next time there’s a political flashpoint, those missiles are pointed at something.” He paused. “I’ve seen that future. I don’t like it.”

Ben had lowered the macrobinoculars. He was watching Jacen with that particular stillness he always had when a lesson arrived without being announced.

“And the other one?” Ben asked.

“We go in now,” Jacen said. “In daylight. Before the crew finishes the load and before the guard rotation gets a relief shift.” He turned his head and met Nelani’s eyes. “We take the cell, secure the missiles, and hand the facility to the local authorities with the crew already in custody. The documentation comes after. It always comes after. That’s what documentation is for.”

Nelani held his gaze. Her expression stayed precise and unsatisfied—the look of someone who found the argument structurally sound and still didn’t trust what it meant.

“You’re describing a fait accompli,” she said.

“I’m describing an operation with a time constraint.” He picked up the macrobinoculars, checked the loading area again, then set them down. “The distinction matters.”

“Does it.”

“It does to me.”

She looked back toward the loading area. The guard at the doors still hadn’t moved. The east-fence guard remained crouched over the sensor relay at the corner post. Twelve minutes had become ten.

“If this goes wrong,” Nelani said, “the political fallout lands on the Order, not on the GA security apparatus.”

“Yes,” Jacen said. “It does.”

He gave her no apology, because anything softer would have been dishonest. The risk was real. He had measured it already and decided the balance favored the operation, not the paperwork. That weight belonged to him. He wasn’t asking her to pretend otherwise.

She was quiet for three seconds. Then, “What’s the entry point?”

“Loading area doors,” Jacen said. “The guard there is primary contact. Ben takes the east-fence guard before he finishes the reset—quietly, if possible.” He looked at Ben. “Can you manage that distance?”

Ben’s hand had already gone to his lightsaber hilt, the movement so habitual it was nearly reflex. “I’ve got it.”

“I want him down before the perimeter guard finishes his next circuit. That gives us a thirty-second overlap before the rotation expects contact.” Jacen shifted his attention to Nelani. “The two on the main approach are yours, if you’re in.”

She held his gaze a moment longer. Whatever she was measuring, she measured it, finished, and made her decision.

“I’m in,” she said.

The reservation remained. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, in the way her fingers flexed once against the fabric of her robe before settling. But it was contained now, professional—the objection of someone who had made her point and wouldn’t pretend it vanished because she’d been overruled. He didn’t need it to vanish. He only needed her moving with them.

He rose from cover.

Dry wind hit him at once, carrying the dusty sage smell of the low brush and, under it, the chemical bite drifting off the service pad. His saber hilt sat warm in his palm. He didn’t ignite it. Not yet. The unlit blade was a choice, a statement to himself that he would escalate only as far as necessary.

The choice to escalate at all had already been made.

He didn’t look back to see whether they were following.

They were. He could feel Ben at his left shoulder—steady, intent, trust so absolute it registered like weight—and Nelani half a step behind on his right, robe hem dragging dust, her disagreement folded into something she would carry through the operation and raise again on the far side of it.

The loading area waited two hundred meters down the slope. The guard at the doors still hadn’t looked up. The east-fence guard remained crouched at the corner post, back turned.

Ten minutes to reset.

Jacen moved toward the loading area.
