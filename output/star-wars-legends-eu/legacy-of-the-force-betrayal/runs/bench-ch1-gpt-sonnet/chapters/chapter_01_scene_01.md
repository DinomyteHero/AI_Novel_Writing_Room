"He doesn't exist."

Luke was already upright when the words reached his own ears. The blankets had slid to his waist. Cool air touched his chest. Behind him, on the headboard shelf, he could feel the familiar weight of two lightsabers and the steadier, less definable fact of Mara waking beside him.

Her hand found his arm once — not comfort, confirmation. He was here. He was awake. "That one again?"

He drew a slow breath and stared into the dim room. Pale stone on every side in the low light, drawers fitted flush to the walls, a rack of practice staves standing in orderly shadow. The datapads and flimsi notes on the table caught a dull sheen. The small sandglass statuette from Tatooine sat crooked among them, its rough face lit from one side like something waiting to be noticed.

"Yes."

Mara pushed herself up on one elbow. Her hair was a red tangle, her green gaze open and fixed on him at once. Sleep never seemed to get a grace period with her. "Clearer?"

"Yes."

That was enough. He felt her attention shift and settle — all of it on him now, all of it practical. Not a nightmare. Not a Council vote. Force.

He set his feet on the floor. The stone was cold against his soles. "He was there from the start this time. Not just at the edge."

"In the hood."

Luke nodded. "In shadow. But not darkness alone." He let the image gather itself before he spoke. "Shadows of light. Ignorance. Denial. Layers over him — or around him. I couldn't see a face. I still can't. But I know what he carries."

Mara sat up fully, the blanket gathered around her waist. "Trouble narrows it to the whole galaxy."

"Pain," Luke said. "A great deal of it. To the galaxy. To us." He paused. That last part had been the sharpest point in the vision — not loud, but exact, like a finger pressed directly on a bruise. "To me."

She studied him for a moment. Not his words. His certainty.

Luke had learned long ago not to decorate what the Force gave him. The dream carried the same weight as the earlier two, but tonight it had pressed harder, like a hand that meant to be obeyed. It had brought him awake before thought did. That alone mattered.

Mara said, "Personal usually helps."

"Not this time."

"Male?"

"I sense male." Luke rubbed a hand across his face, trying to clear the last grit of sleep. The vision had left residue behind — a wrongness that had no business in a bedroom before dawn. "Human, or near-human."

"That rules out a lot of Senate committees."

He glanced back at her. "I'm serious."

"I know." Her tone didn't shift. "So am I."

That was Mara. Keep the blade sharp; don't wave it around.

Luke looked down at his hands. Steady. That helped less than it should have. "Three times in two months. The same destination each time. The same certainty. He's moving toward something."

"Someone?"

Luke turned it over. In the vision the figure had always been advancing — patient, unhurried, as if delay were irrelevant because the end of the path was already fixed. "Perhaps. But I don't sense pursuit. I sense intent."

Mara swung her legs over the side of the bed. "All right. Short way. Who fits?"

He almost smiled. She had no patience for mystical fog when a list would do.

She said, "Raynar."

The name had occurred to Luke before she finished it. He tested it anyway, because the question deserved a clean answer. Raynar Thul — burned, changed, dragged through too many identities and not all of them his own. Dangerous once. Still damaged. Still held in the Temple under healers and watchfulness and walls.

Luke shook his head. "No."

"Too contained?"

"Too alien."

Mara's brows drew together. "He is human."

"He was." Luke reached for the right edge of it. "What I sense in the vision isn't Killik-touched. None of that collective weight. No insect mind. No chorus." He disliked the word, but it fit well enough. "This is singular. Focused. Human enough that I keep reaching for a name I should already know."

"Should know," Mara repeated.

He nodded.

That was the part that sat worst. Not the threat. Not even the pain wrapped around it. The shape of recognition with nothing behind it — a door that looked familiar and opened onto a wall.

Mara leaned back on her hands and followed the thought where he had already taken it. "Someone already in the Order?"

"I don't know."

"Someone you knew and lost track of."

"I don't know."

"Helpful vision."

Luke exhaled through his nose. "Very."

She let that sit for a beat, then said, "What about your father?"

The question should have landed harder than it did. Perhaps because it had already been waiting for him.

Luke turned toward the table — the dim outline of the statuette, the scattered work he would have to return to in a few hours. "I considered him."

Mara said nothing. She was good at silence when silence was the faster road.

Luke looked past the room and into memory, but only as far as he had to. Not the armored shape the galaxy remembered. Earlier than that. The dangerous point. "Not Vader. Anakin in the last stretch before Vader. The face still his. Features a Jedi would recognize. But the eyes—" He stopped.

"Gold," Mara said.

"Or orange." His voice stayed level. "Bright with anger. With power he was already using the wrong way."

Mara watched him. "And?"

"It isn't him."

That was certain. The image from the vision carried echoes, yes — a pattern he knew too well, a Jedi shape bent toward ruin. But if the Force had meant to show him his father, even as comparison, it would have resolved. It had not.

Mara drew one knee up, rested an arm across it. "Because you'd know."

"Yes."

"Or because the eyes are wrong."

Luke considered that, then shook his head. "Not the eyes. The presence. My father, even then—" He let the sentence trail, found a shorter one. "I would know him."

The room went quiet.

Somewhere past the chamber walls, the Temple remained what it always was before dawn: occupied, never busy; alive, never loud. The door held the corridor out. Here there was only the faint hum of climate control, the dry smell of paper and machine-warmed data plastics, and Mara beside him — awake enough for both of them.

"He doesn't exist," she said, testing the phrase this time, hearing how absurd it sounded stripped of the Force-weight.

Luke looked at her. "That's what it is."

"A man who exists enough to threaten you, the galaxy, and your sleep schedule, but not enough to identify."

"Yes."

She gave a small nod. She didn't ask whether he doubted the vision. She knew better. Doubt wasn't the problem. The problem was that he trusted it completely and could do nothing with it yet.

Luke said, "I won't take it to the Council like this."

"Good."

He raised an eyebrow.

"You want me to disagree?"

"No."

"Then save the expression, Skywalker. 'A hooded male person of undefined existence may become a problem at some point' isn't a report. It's the start of an argument." Her mouth twitched. "Cilghal would be patient. Kyp would be impossible. Corran would want to know if the nonexistent man had violated local regulations."

Luke did smile at that, briefly. "He might."

"He would."

The smile faded, but not entirely. Luke lowered himself back against the headboard. He was still tired. The body didn't argue with Force-dreams indefinitely; it simply paid for them afterward. "I could be looking at someone who hasn't made the choice yet."

Mara's expression sharpened. "That's different."

"Yes."

"Different enough to matter?"

"I sense it is."

There it was — the nearest thing to the crack he would let himself name. Not in the vision. In his position before it. He could identify threats. He had built a life on doing that too late and then attempting the impossible to correct for it. But this might be a warning about a person before the person became what the warning described.

A shape without a face. A future pressing forward into the present.

Mara said, "Then we watch."

Luke nodded.

"For what?"

He closed his eyes for a moment, testing the residue. The answer was no clearer than it had been in sleep. "For someone moving with certainty. Someone hidden by what people refuse to see."

"That narrows it to politics."

He opened his eyes. "You think I should ignore it?"

"No. I think you should sleep before you turn one dream into six bad decisions tomorrow."

Luke let out a soft breath that might have been a laugh in someone less tired. "That sounds like advice."

"It is. I'm versatile."

He settled farther down, drawing the blanket up. Mara remained sitting a moment longer, watching him with the same bright, unsparing attention she had once turned on Imperial briefings and assassination dossiers. Luke took comfort in it. The dream had left him with no name, but at least the burden was in words now. Mara had it too.

"Raynar isn't the answer," she said.

"No."

"Anakin isn't the answer."

"No."

"And when you meet the real answer, you'll know."

Luke wanted to say yes at once. The honest answer took a beat longer. "I hope so."

Mara absorbed that without flinching. "Good enough for pre-dawn."

Luke lay back. Above him on the shelf rested the twin weights of their lightsabers. Beyond them, the Temple. Beyond that, every obligation waiting for daylight. None of it changed because of one dream. That, too, was part of being Grand Master — the galaxy never paused to let a warning sharpen into focus.

Mara looked at him sidelong. "Let's wait until he shows up, then crush him."

Luke smiled, closed his eyes, his breathing slowing into natural sleep within a minute. Mara lay awake a little longer, reaching through the Force for any flicker of what was troubling her husband, found no sign of it, and she too slept.
