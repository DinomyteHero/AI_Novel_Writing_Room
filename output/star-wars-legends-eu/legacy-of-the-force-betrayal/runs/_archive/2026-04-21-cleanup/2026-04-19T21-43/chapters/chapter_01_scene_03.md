The alley tasted of metal dust, with something colder under it—the ozone edge of coolant cycling through the plant's vents, breathing out in slow, uneven pulses against Ben's face. He crouched behind Jacen and worked at breathing like this was ordinary.

His thumb found the ignition stud on his lightsaber hilt. He didn't press it. He only let the ridged metal sit beneath his skin.

Still there.

A repulsorlift transport crossed low overhead, and engine wash spilled down the alley, shoving a wave of hot air across the back of his neck. The drone rolled away. Jacen hadn't moved.

Ben checked the stud again.

He'd run the Forms in the Temple practice hall so many times that Shii-Cho no longer felt learned. It felt like walking. Like breathing. Like something his body simply did. Out here, folded into the shadows of a missile factory on Adumar with a guard rotation memorized and a plan already ticking forward, the hilt felt different. Heavier. Not in mass. In meaning.

Jacen's voice came low and level, barely louder than the wind. "Two minutes."

Ben nodded. He didn't answer. Nothing useful came to mind.

Nelani was three meters off to his right, one shoulder against the permacrete wall. She'd been motionless so long she almost looked built into the alley. Ben had watched her go quiet the way a blade went still before a cut—not absent, just contained. Whatever she was thinking, she wasn't going to say it again.

He took that in and let it settle. She'd already said her piece.

Jacen turned his head, not all the way. His eyes stayed on the service entrance; only enough of his profile shifted for Ben to catch it. "When we move," he said, "you're left of the hatch."

Ben waited for the rest. The reason. The part about why left, or what he was supposed to do if the loading-bay guard came around the corner early, or what "left of the hatch" meant once they were through and inside.

Nothing else came.

Jacen had already turned back.

Ben's first impulse was to ask. He knew the alley's measurements. He knew the guard rotation. He knew the east relay was still dark and that their window would not stay open. Given thirty seconds, he could probably work through the reasoning on his own. But the question that rose in him—why left, specifically?—ran into Jacen's stillness and stopped there.

Jacen knew. Jacen had seen the angles and chosen this one. Jacen had put him left of the hatch because left was where he was supposed to be, and the explanation was already folded into the plan, and the plan was already moving.

Ben said, "Where do you want me when we're inside?"

Jacen flicked a glance back at him. "Left of the hatch first. I'll signal."

"Okay."

That was all. The whole exchange. Ben eased his weight back onto his heels and let the question go. He was left of the hatch. He would wait for the signal. Everything after that was Jacen's to carry until it became Ben's problem.

Something in his chest had been tight and uneven since they'd left the scrubland and slipped into the alley. He noticed it the same way he noticed the coolant tang—there, real, manageable. His breathing was too fast. He made it slow, counting against the thump of some loader working somewhere inside the wall. In. Out. The hilt stayed solid under his palm.

He was fine.

Nelani shifted. Barely anything—just weight settling, one hand drawing nearer her own saber. Ben caught it from the corner of his eye without turning his head. She wasn't looking at him. She was watching Jacen's back, and her expression was the same one she'd worn out in the scrubland: not angry, not scared, just exact and watchful and already past argument.

Ben had seen his mother wear that look. It meant I disagree, but I've said so, and now we move.

He found that reassuring in a way he couldn't have explained.

Jacen rose.

No signal. No warning. He was simply still, and then he was in motion, and the change between the two was so clean it hardly felt like a change at all. He came out of the alley's shadow and crossed the open ground toward the service entrance with an unhurried speed that only looked slow.

Ben moved after him.

The permacrete was pale and cracked along the edges, and his boots made almost no sound on it. Fifteen meters. Ten. The service entrance was a heavy panel set low in the wall, sealed with a manual latch, no keypad in sight. The loading-bay guard Jacen had marked was around the corner and still not audible. Ben could hear his own pulse.

Jacen reached the panel first and stopped beside it. His saber hilt was in his hand now—still dark, gripped loosely, the way Ben had been taught to hold a tool before it became a weapon.

A sound drifted from around the corner. Footsteps. Deliberate. Unhurried. The loading-bay guard on his rotation.

Jacen stepped around the corner.

There was a short sound—not a struggle, not a shout—and then the footsteps ended.

Ben held at the panel and didn't look. He kept his eyes on the entrance, on the seam under the door, on the thin line of artificial light leaking through. His thumb rested on the ignition stud. Not pressing. Just knowing where it was.

Jacen came back. He settled the guard's weight against the wall with a care that was almost gentle, then straightened and looked at Ben, then at Nelani.

Nelani moved to the panel.

No hesitation. Her hand closed on the latch. With the other, she brought up her lightsaber, and the blade came alive with a clean snap-hiss that sliced through every other sound in the alley. Blue light washed over the permacrete, the panel, Ben's hands.

He felt the change in his chest—not tighter now, but steadier. Something locking into place.

He moved left of the panel and set himself there. His own saber stayed unlit in his grip. His breathing had gone even. Jacen was beside him, calm as ever, the factory wall hard at his back, and Nelani's blue blade threw sharp edges on everything.

Ben waited for the signal.
