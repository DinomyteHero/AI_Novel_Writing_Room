The loading bay had three guards on a twenty-minute rotation, and the one on the south approach checked the same patch of fence every four minutes. He had been doing it for the last hour. Jacen filed that away and swept the macrobinoculars left.

The east sensor relay was still dark. Forty minutes now. Somebody on the maintenance crew had logged it and failed to circle back, which meant either the facility was short-handed or its people had learned not to hurry.

"Guard on the north apron is bored," he said, keeping his voice low. "Same forty-meter stretch since we got here. At the far end he pauses about six seconds before he turns back. He’s doing math, or he’s tired. Either helps us."

Ben’s elbow pressed against his. When the boy leaned in, his breath fogged the macrobinocular eyepiece. "The relay’s still out."

"Yes."

"So the east fence is—"

"A gap. Another fifteen minutes at least before somebody notices and resets it by hand."

From Jacen’s other side, Nelani said, flat and precise, "Jacen. We need to contact the local GA security liaison."

He lowered the macrobinoculars. The scrubland around them was pale and brittle, the wind off the service pad hot enough to sting and carrying the scorched bite of permacrete. Somewhere inside the plant, a loader cycled. He felt the vibration faintly, a background pulse the Force confirmed without needing to be called. The loading pattern he’d tracked over the last hour matched it. They were moving crates. By nightfall, they’d be finished.

"We’d need a warrant," Nelani went on. "GA protocol for a Jedi-assisted operation on a non-Corellian world requires local authority sign-off and liaison approval before we cross that perimeter. That’s not caution. That’s procedure."

"I know the procedure," Jacen said.

"The liaison backlog is six to twelve hours. Minimum. That’s the last figure I have from Coruscant."

"I know that too."

She let the silence sit for a beat. Dust had grayed the hem of her robe. Her face gave away nothing, but her body did: the slight pitch forward, the line of her shoulders, the tension she kept leashed under her calm. Nelani Dinn did not argue with a commanding officer unless she meant to do it well.

"Then you understand," she said, "that what you’re describing is a fait accompli. We move, we clear the cell, and by the time anyone asks about the warrant, the situation is already over. The Jedi Order gets handed a political problem."

"The Jedi Order gets handed a political problem," Jacen said. "Or Corellia gets handed a missile shipment, and six months from now we’re reading casualty reports and asking why we waited for paperwork."

"You don’t know—"

"I do know." He said it without edge, as plainly as he might have said the relay is down. "Not specifics. I can’t give you a star chart showing exactly where these weapons end up. But the futures where we wait for documentation all bend the same way. The shipment moves tonight. The cell disperses. Whatever they’re supplying, it gets supplied."

Ben had drawn back from the macrobinoculars. He was doing the thing he did when he was listening hard and trying not to look as if he was: nudging his lightsaber hilt on his belt with one thumb, eyes on neither of them.

Nelani breathed out through her nose. "The Council should be making this call."

"The Council isn’t here."

"Which is exactly why protocol exists."

Jacen turned to face her. She was young—not much older than he had been on his first independent Jedi assignment—and she was right, technically, in every part of it. Protocol was protocol. The warrant requirement existed because Jedi operating outside legal structures had a way of leaving knots that took decades to untangle. He had no real argument with any of her premises.

"The future where we wait," he said, "and the future where we move—I have looked at both. I’m not asking you to take that on faith. I’m asking you to accept that I’ve made the calculation, and I believe the second future costs less."

"Costs less," she repeated. "That is one way to describe an unauthorized raid on a paramilitary facility in broad daylight."

"It’s the accurate way."

Inside the plant, the loader cycled again. On the north apron, the guard reached the end of his forty-meter path and halted. One second. Two. Three. Then he turned and walked back.

Ben spoke without lifting his gaze from the hilt. "If we’re going, we should go before he notices the relay is down and they fix it." A beat. "Just observing."

Nelani looked at him. Ben looked back with the expression he wore whenever he knew he’d said something inconvenient and meant it anyway.

Then she turned to Jacen.

"I want it on record," she said, "that I raised the jurisdictional concern."

"It’s on record."

"With who?"

"With me," Jacen said. "And with Ben, who appears to have excellent recall."

Ben’s expression stayed solemn. "I’m thirteen. I don’t have recall, I have anxiety."

Nelani looked at the plant, then back at Jacen. The objection remained. It would remain. He wasn’t trying to talk it out of her. But under it, something had shifted into the cooler shape of compliance. She had lodged the concern. He had heard it. The situation had not changed.

"All right," she said.

Jacen nodded. "East fence, through the relay gap. Ben, you take the guard on the south approach. He’ll be at the far end of his rotation in four minutes, with his back turned. One contact, clean. I don’t want him raising an alarm, and I don’t want him hurt beyond what’s necessary to keep him down."

Ben’s thumb settled on the saber stud without pressing it. "Got it."

"Nelani, the two on the main approach are paired. They overlap at the junction every rotation. You’ll have about thirty seconds when both are visible and neither has line of sight to the bay. I need you there first."

"And you?"

"Loading bay. The guard there is the only one actually paying attention. I’ll handle him before we breach."

Nelani’s eyes stayed on him. "Handle."

"Minimum necessary," Jacen said. "That’s the standard I’m using."

She held his gaze another moment, then glanced at Ben, then back to the plant. "Fine."

The wind shifted and shoved another wave of heat off the service pad. Somewhere inside, a door opened and shut. The loader’s rhythm changed pitch, then settled back into its old cadence.

Jacen rose.

No wasted motion. His saber hilt sat in his hand unlit—not raised, not threatened, only ready, the way a tool was ready a moment before use. He stepped out of the scrub and onto the open ground between cover and the perimeter, and the daylight struck him with the flat, pitiless indifference of an Adumar afternoon.

Ben moved in behind his left shoulder. Nelani came up on the right, her robe hem dragging one last line through the dust.

The loading bay was forty meters away.
