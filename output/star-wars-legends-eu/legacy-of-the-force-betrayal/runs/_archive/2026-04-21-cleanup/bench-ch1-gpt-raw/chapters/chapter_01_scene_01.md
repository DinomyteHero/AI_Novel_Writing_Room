“He doesn’t exist.”

Luke sat upright before he knew he’d moved. The words hung in the dim chamber, flat and certain.

Beside him, Mara came awake all at once. Her red hair stood in every direction it could manage, and her eyes were already open and sharp. One hand moved under the blanket, not panicked, just practical, checking where she was in relation to the shelf behind the headboard and the two lightsabers resting there.

“Good morning to you, too,” she said.

Luke looked around until the dark shape of the chamber settled back into place as a room again instead of the place the vision had left him. Stone-veneer walls. The shadowed rack of plasteel staves near the far side. A table burdened with datapads, flimsi notes, datachips from Masters who had decided midnight was the proper hour to send communiqués, and the crude little Tatooine sandglass statuette someone had given him years ago. Its rough edges caught the low light from the corridor strip under the door.

Mara pushed herself up on one elbow. “Vision?”

He nodded once. His mouth was dry. The weight of it still sat on him, not pain, not exactly fear, but the wrongness that came when sleep had carried more than sleep should. “The same one.”

“The one that doesn’t exist.”

“Yes.”

She studied his face for a moment, reading what the Force wasn’t saying. Mara had never needed mystical fog around her perceptions. “Clearer?”

“Yes.”

That one word stayed in the air longer than he liked. He put his feet on the floor. The stone was cool.

“There’s a man,” Luke said. “I never see his face clearly. He’s hooded, but that isn’t the important part. It’s as if the unseen parts of him are buried under shadows, except they aren’t shadows. Light, ignorance, denial. Layers of them. I know what he is before I know who he is.”

“And what is he?”

“A source of pain.” Luke rubbed a hand over his eyes, then let it drop. “For the galaxy. For me.”

Mara was fully upright now, blanket around her waist, awake in the quiet, stripped-down way she always was. “Personal pain?”

He turned to look at her. “Yes.”

“That narrows it to several billion beings who’d enjoy the job.”

A corner of Luke’s mouth moved, but not enough to count. “This one will matter.”

“You sound sure.”

“I am sure.” He paused. “That’s the problem.”

Mara glanced past him at the headboard shelf, then back to him. “You said he doesn’t exist. That sounds like the other problem.”

“It may be the same problem.” Luke let his hands rest on his knees. “I sense him in the vision as present. Not distant. Not hypothetical. He’s moving toward something, or through something. Patiently. There’s purpose in it. But when I try to attach that presence to a name, there’s nothing there.”

“Not nothing,” Mara said. “No match.”

He considered that and nodded. “No match.”

“Fine. We can work with no match.” She tucked one leg under herself. “Who did you rule out already?”

“Raynar.”

That came so fast she gave him a brief look. “I hadn’t asked yet.”

“I know.”

“Still bossy before dawn, then.” Her tone stayed dry. “Why not Raynar Thul?”

Luke turned the vision over once more, not trying to recover images so much as the impression behind them. “Because Raynar feels wrong in a different way. Even now. The Killik imprint is still there. However much treatment helps, however much of himself he’s recovered, there’s still that alien cast to him. A group where a man should be. This isn’t that.”

“He’s in a Temple cell under enough security to hold a small war,” Mara said. “But security’s never been your deciding factor.”

“No.” Luke looked toward the dark ceiling. “If it were Raynar, I’d know. I wouldn’t have certainty without recognition.”

Mara nodded once. “Human, then.”

“Human or near-human.”

“Male?”

“Yes.”

“Age?”

Luke exhaled. “Not clear. Old enough to be deliberate. Young enough that I don’t sense frailty. That isn’t much help.”

“It’s pre-dawn, Skywalker. We’re grading on a curve.”

He looked at her again. Her hair was still wild. It made her look less dangerous than she was, which had always seemed unfair to everyone else.

“Anything familiar?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said, and then had to stop there for a second because the answer went somewhere he did not like. “That’s what makes it worse.”

Mara waited.

Luke said, “The pattern feels familiar. Not the person. The pattern.”

Her expression didn’t change, but her attention tightened. “A Jedi.”

“Yes.”

“Falling.”

“Yes.”

The chamber was quiet except for distant Temple ventilation and the faint rustle of flimsi on the table whenever the air recycler shifted. Luke could still feel the residue of the vision where it had dragged him out of sleep. Not an image now. A direction.

Mara said, “Raynar was a Jedi.”

“But not this.” Luke shook his head. “Not this path.”

“All right.” She drew the blanket closer around her shoulders. “Who else?”

He almost said no one. That would have been easier. But Mara was the person he told first for a reason, and not only because she could take bad news before sunrise better than anyone he knew.

“My father,” he said.

He saw it again as he named it. Not the whole man. Never the whole man. Features he knew from old images and older wounds in the Force: the face of Anakin Skywalker before the mask, before the machine enclosed him, still recognizably Jedi and already moving away from it. And the eyes wrong with anger and power, gold edging toward orange, heat where there should have been restraint.

Mara watched him without interrupting.

“Not Vader,” Luke said. “Not as I knew him. Earlier. While he was becoming what he became.”

“Because the pattern matches.”

“Yes.”

“And because the Force enjoys being obscure when a direct answer would be useful.”

Luke gave her a brief look. “That too.”

“Does it fit?”

“No.” The answer came at once. “Not really.”

“Because he’s dead.”

“That’s part of it.”

“Only part?”

Luke leaned back a little, bracing one hand on the mattress. “If the vision were only symbolic, perhaps. A warning in the shape of the last great fall, the one everyone in the Order measures against whether they admit it or not. But it doesn’t feel symbolic. It feels immediate.”

“Present tense,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“And not him.”

Luke let the image go. “My father’s fall had rage in it. Despair. A kind of collapse dressed up as decision.” He frowned slightly. “This is colder than that. More controlled. Not less dangerous. More.”

Mara considered him for a moment. “That’s cheerful.”

“I wasn’t trying to improve the mood.”

“No, that was my job.”

He huffed a soft breath that almost became a laugh. Almost.

She said, “You’re ruling out Raynar because the mind in the vision isn’t insect-touched. You’re ruling out Anakin because the timing is wrong and the weight is wrong.”

“Yes.”

“So we’re left with a human or near-human male, connected somehow to the Jedi, walking toward a great deal of trouble, and the vision has gone coy on the name.”

“That’s one way to say it.”

“It’s the accurate one.”

Luke looked toward the table again. Datapads. Notes. Half the Order seemed to be asleep only in shifts these days, and he was one of them. The Temple was home and command post both, and sometimes the overlap sat easily. Sometimes it left a chamber looking like this.

“I’m not taking it to the Council,” he said.

Mara’s response was immediate. “Good.”

He turned back to her. “That didn’t take long.”

“You want to tell a room full of Jedi Masters that the Force warned you about a man who doesn’t exist?”

“When you put it that way—”

“I usually do.” She settled back against the headboard. “What would you tell them? That he’s male, dangerous, maybe has a Jedi connection, and definitely isn’t one of the first two names anyone would ask about?”

Luke let that run through his mind. “Not enough.”

“Exactly.”

“I should still record it.”

“Yes.”

He nodded. That was simple enough. A note for the private files. A memory preserved while the edges were still sharp. If other fragments came later, he could compare them. If they did not, then he had one more warning without a use attached to it, which was not new.

Mara said, “How many times now?”

“Three in two months.”

“And this one’s the clearest.”

“Yes.”

“Then either it’s getting closer, or you’re getting a better angle.”

“I sense it’s getting closer.”

She accepted that without argument. Mara did not waste time pretending skepticism where the vision was concerned. She saved it for interpretation, where it could do some good.

Luke lowered himself back against the headboard, then eased down onto the pillow. The chamber seemed steadier now. The pressure had not gone away entirely, but speaking it had put edges around it.

Mara watched him. “You planning to sleep, or just supervise the bedding?”

“I’m going back to sleep.”

“Good. You need at least an hour before people start asking you questions with titles attached.”

“That soon?”

“You’re Grand Master. If they’re polite, yes.”

Luke folded his hands over the blanket. “Mara.”

“Yes?”

“If this becomes clearer—”

“You’ll tell me.”

He looked at her.

She added, “And if it gets clearer while you’re asleep, try opening with a complete sentence next time.”

Despite himself, Luke smiled.

Mara’s mouth twitched. Then she said, “Let’s wait until he shows up, then crush him.”

Luke closed his eyes. The smile stayed for another second, then eased away as his breathing slowed and settled into natural sleep within a minute.

Mara remained propped against the headboard a little longer. Luke’s presence in the Force had gone quiet again, ordinary and familiar, the vision’s grip released. She reached outward with care, not searching deep enough to wake old instincts, only enough to test the dark water around them for a ripple that matched what had disturbed him. There was nothing. No flicker, no identifiable stain, no patient presence moving where she could catch it.

After a while she lay down again, watched the dim line of light under the door blur, and slept.
