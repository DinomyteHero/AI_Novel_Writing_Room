The turbolift doors slid apart, and the smell hit first.

Not death, exactly. Jacen had smelled death, and death had a wetness to it, a clinging specificity. This was its industrial cousin: propellant and composite resin, high-explosive stabilizer cooked into the ventilation cycle, machine lubricant gone tacky in the seams of the catwalk grating under his boots. The aroma of death and destruction translated into labor. Things built to kill, assembled by people who clocked in, clocked out, and probably complained about management over meals. He drew it in and let the factory identify itself.

He stepped onto the catwalk without waiting for Testan ke Harran. His cloak bellied behind him—the fabric heavier than he usually wore, chosen for exactly that response—and his boot heels rang on metal with a cadence that carried over the assembly-floor noise below. Both effects were incidental to walking at his natural pace, which was the point. A man conscious of being watched adjusted himself. A man who expected to be watched simply moved.

Testan hurried to catch up a pace or two back.

Below lay eight lanes divided by duracrete partitions, each its own stream of production. Luminous white conveyor belts ran the length of the chamber. At the near stations, hazmat-suited workers lowered sensors into luminous-green spheres the size of a human head, each sphere resting in its cradle with the serene menace of something too dangerous to understand itself. Farther down, casing housings passed under rubber-headed hammers, workers tapping the composite shells and listening for cracks in the note. At the far end, sensor-assembly technicians plugged cables into units and watched black-and-white monitor feeds with the absorbed indifference of people who had repeated the same careful motions ten thousand times.

Jacen took it all in by the time Testan reached his elbow.

"Ar workars," Testan said, sweeping one hand across the floor with practiced pride, "ar highly trained. Ach lane has its own team, its own supervisor. Whar you are standing, Mister Solo, is whar the plant's director stands ach morning for review."

Testan ke Harran was handsome in the way Adumari men became handsome after surviving long enough. In his forties, in other words: old enough to have earned the right to stop proving himself in duels. His orange-and-purple diamond-patterned tunic fell to mid-thigh over gold trousers, while belt and scarf added two more shades of gold that disagreed with each other. The beard was the centerpiece—black, lustrous, maintained to a degree that suggested scheduling.

Ben said, "He says these are the workers, and they're highly trained, and each lane has its own team, and you're standing where the director stands."

Jacen turned his head to look at him.

Ben's face stayed professionally blank. He was thirteen, and Testan's beard had occupied a meaningful share of his attention since the lobby forty minutes ago.

"Tell him," Jacen said, "that we're grateful for the access and look forward to reviewing the production documentation."

"He's grateful," Ben told Testan. "And he wants to see the production documentation."

Testan beamed. "Of course, of course. The Galactic Alliance saction team will find everything in order. Our operation is fully compliant with—" He paused, hunting the Basic term. "—with the relevant farce standards."

"He says everything's compliant with the relevant force standards," Ben said.

"Excellent." Jacen resumed walking, and his heels resumed announcing him. He did not look at Ben. He did not need to.

"Your beard," Ben said to Testan in a conversational tone, "is really something."

Testan touched it with the reflex of a man used to hearing that and still pleased by it. "I thank you. It requires some attention."

"Do you use oil?"

"A blend. There is a specialist in the upper market district who—"

"Do you brush it with butter? My uncle does."

Testan looked honestly unsure whether he had been insulted. "Butter."

"He has this whole thing," Ben said. "It's very complicated."

While Ben occupied Testan, Jacen walked the catwalk and let the facility's rhythm come through. Not the surface rhythm—he could see that, the timed belt cycles and supervisor stations, the orderly flow of people and materials. What he wanted was the shape behind the visible shape. The way a room breathed. Patterns of weight, attention, and concealment that touched the Force not as images but as pressure, as directional certainty, the way a pilot felt bad weather before the instruments agreed.

The factory was proud of itself. That was the first impression. Workers moved with the easy competence of people convinced their work mattered. Supervisors watched the lanes with real investment. This was not a front staffed by conscripts dreaming of somewhere else. These were craftspeople. They made missiles, and they made them well, and the factory took satisfaction in that.

The second impression was the gap.

Not a literal one. The catwalk was continuous, the partitions even, the floor plan regular. But on the east side there was a section the room did not attend to. Worker movement bent a little away from it. Supervisor routes stopped a few meters short and turned back. It was a place the facility had taught itself not to see, and that collective act of not-seeing shone in the Force as plainly as a sealed hatch.

Jacen kept walking. His pace did not alter.

"The sensor-assembly saction," Testan was saying, "uses the most advanced calibration equipment available in the—"

"He says the sensor assembly is very advanced," Ben said.

"I heard him," Jacen said.

Ben glanced at him.

"My Adumari," Jacen said, "is adequate. I simply prefer to have you translate."

Testan's expression shifted. Not much—just enough for a man recalculating the room after discovering his guest understood more than he had pretended. His composure held. Something underneath it tightened.

There.

Jacen did not push. He did not probe in any showy way. He simply remained present, attentive, impossible to ignore, the way a gravity well did not need to announce itself to shape an orbit. Testan's mental discipline was real. He had been briefed for this inspection, had organized his tour carefully around the legitimate parts of the facility and away from the rest.

But preparation was not comfort, and discomfort had edges.

The memory leaked through in fragments: a door, broad and gray, dropping from above instead of swinging on hinges; supervisors in dark blue jumpsuits scurrying out ahead of it, not quite running, moving with the clipped urgency of people who knew better than to be on the wrong side when it sealed.

Jacen stopped.

He stood at the railing, looking down at the production floor for a heartbeat, then said quietly, "There is something here."

Ben went motionless.

Testan said, "I beg your pardon?"

"Something wrong." Jacen turned from the railing and looked toward the east side—not directly at the exact section, not yet, but in that area, like a man following an instinct he had not decided to trust. "The layout of this section does not correspond to the schematics we were provided."

"The schematics are—" Testan broke off. "There may be minor discrepancies. Updates are filed—"

Jacen was already moving toward the east side.

His heels rang louder now because his pace had increased, not because he cared whether anyone heard. The cloak moved harder behind him. He heard Testan following, heard the man's breathing change, heard Ben slide in a half-step back with the quiet speed of someone who had understood, without instruction, that the operation had changed phases.

The wall panel was durasteel, gray, featureless. A faint seam traced a rectangle almost invisible against the surface texture—almost invisible, but not to a man who had already seen a door descend on precisely that line.

Jacen stopped before it.

"This saction," Testan said, "is a maintenance access. For the ventilation—"

"Ben," Jacen said.

Ben already had his lightsaber in hand. He looked at it, then at the panel, then at Testan. "He brought it for practice," he said. He thumbed the activator, and the blue blade snapped-hissed to life, throwing hard-edged shadows across the surface.

Testan stared.

"That's true from a certain point of view," Jacen said. "He does practice with it."

Ben set the blade to the panel and began cutting. Durasteel parted with a sound like a long breath pulled through clenched teeth, and the cut edges glowed orange with residual heat. He traced the circle steadily—a meter and a half across, at arm height from the catwalk floor—the blue blade flashing over the surface in quick arcs of reflected light. When he completed it, he stepped back, and the panel dropped inward with a clang that boomed through the space beyond.

The hidden corridor was lit, not dark, by the same stark white strips as the rest of the factory. In that light, stacked along both sides from floor to ceiling, stood row after row of transport containers. Each was about the size of a speeder bike's cargo compartment and stenciled in industrial lettering: DAMMANT KILLER MODEL 16 — CONCUSSION MISSILE — 24 UNITS. Beneath that, in red: DANGER. DO NOT DROP.

The crates extended farther back than the building's exterior dimensions should have allowed.

Jacen studied them for a moment. Then he turned to Testan.

Testan ke Harran was a man who had survived enough duels to stop having them, and he had done so by reading situations accurately. He read this one accurately. His beard was still immaculate, his gold sash still in place, his orange-and-purple tunic still elegant. But the man inside them had stopped performing.

"I want," he said carefully, "to contact my legal representation."

"That's reasonable," Jacen said. "You can do that from wherever they take you."

The sound came from the far end of the passage: boots hammering metal, moving fast, and the distinct clatter of armor coming to ready.

Jacen turned.

The first wave rounded the corner six abreast, which the passage barely tolerated, and compressed into a column by force of geometry. Orange-and-green uniforms: X-wing-pilot orange in the fabric, speeder-bike green in the carapace armor. At the front was the commander, a compact woman with her sidearm already out. Five more followed with blaster rifles braced.

"Drop your weapons," she said. "Both of you. Down on the deck."

Jacen ignited his lightsaber.

Green light ran over passage surfaces, over missile containers, over orange-and-green armor. The commander's aim did not flicker, which told him something useful.

"Stay behind me," he said to Ben.

Then he started walking toward them.

"I'm going to ask you," Jacen said in his normal conversational tone, his green blade turning a slow butterfly pattern before him, "to reconsider your current approach. This passage is narrow. The crates behind you are marked with a strong recommendation against dropping them." He tilted his head a little. "Could you speak up? I'm a little deaf."

Ben made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

The commander fired.

The bolt came fast and low, aimed at his legs—a sensible target, below the higher arc of the blade. Jacen dropped the pattern to meet it and returned it at an angle. It struck the trooper on the commander's left in the shoulder plate—not the shoulder, the plate—and spun her sideways into the trooper beside her. Both went down in a clatter of armor and startled profanity.

The next five bolts came almost together, overlapping. Jacen moved through them. The butterfly pattern wasn't flourish. It was geometry. He knew where each bolt would be before it arrived, and his blade was there first, and the redirected fire went back into the passage along lines the troops had not accounted for. The commander's second shot snapped back and clipped the rim of her own helmet. She sat down hard against the side, dazed but breathing.

Four troopers were down in the opening seconds. Two remained standing, and both wore the look of beings abruptly reconsidering their profession.

"Second wave," Jacen said—not to them—and stepped over the fallen without breaking stride.

They came from a side branch he had not seen: six more, heavier armor, longer rifles. At the rear, a third group was assembling at the main junction.

"Ben," Jacen said.

"Holocam," Ben said, and Jacen heard the small expensive unit click on behind him.

"Um," Ben said, "this is Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight Jacen Solo and I are in a secret part of the Dammant Killers plant in Cartann on Adumar. We found it behind a panel in the main production section. There are—" He paused, counting. "—there are a lot of crates in here. The labels say DAMMANT KILLER MODEL 16, twenty-four units per crate, and there are maybe forty crates, maybe more. The Dammant Killers security people are trying to kill us. Jedi Knight Solo is handling that part." Another pause. "The date is—"

He gave the date.

"That's good," Jacen said, batting aside two more bolts in quick sequence. One he sent into the side. One took a trooper in the outer thigh—not the artery, not worse than necessary—and the trooper dropped with a shout that sounded painful rather than final. "Keep recording."

The second wave pressed in, and Jacen yielded ground—not retreating, merely spending space with calculation. He moved backward at a walk, blade alive in his hand, keeping himself between the troops and Ben. The passage acoustics stacked each blaster report on top of the last until the air seemed to crackle, but the geometry stayed readable. He felt each shot an instant before it was fired, the intent of the trooper arriving through the Force before the trigger pull, and answered accordingly.

A shot came from the right at an angle that would have sent a clean return elsewhere than he wanted. He shortened the arc and dumped it into the ceiling instead of the ranks. The ceiling was durasteel. The ricochet went wide. Acceptable.

Another came in close to the hilt. He took it there rather than send it back toward a worker station visible through an open opening at the passage edge. The impact jolted his wrist. He absorbed it and kept moving.

"Jacen," Ben said. "Rail."

They had reached the passage's end. A railing crossed the terminus, and below it spread a second production chamber—another set of lanes, another web of conveyors, these carrying opalescent shell casings that caught the overhead light and broke it into little prismatic sparks. The chamber was active. People looked up from their stations. Some backed away from the lines. Supervisors moved toward emergency comm panels set into the side.

"My dad says," Ben said, still recording, "that you never want to fight the enemy on ground he's chosen if you can avoid it." He paused. "I think that applies here."

"Your father is usually right," Jacen said.

The third wave was coming at a run now—eight troops, and one of the rear pair had what looked like a stun grenade in hand, which altered the calculations.

Jacen turned. "Go."

Ben vaulted the rail without hesitation. He dropped to the conveyor belt below, landed in a crouch among the opalescent casings as they shifted under him, caught his balance, took two quick steps, and sprinted toward the far side of the chamber.

Jacen went over a beat later. He hit the same belt farther back and felt the casings roll under his boots. From the catwalk, blasterfire came over the rail, the angle bad, the shots slashing into the belt surface and duracrete floor beside it. People scattered. Somewhere a supervisor hit a general alarm, and the chamber lights shifted to a pulsing amber strobe.

Ben was already at the far side. The conveyor ran through a diminutive opening in the side ahead, a small rectangular aperture barely large enough for a person, intended for shell casings moving into the next stage of production. Ben went through in a low scrambling slide, shoulders first, and vanished into the amber-lit opening.

The troops were locating the stairs down from the rail. He had perhaps four seconds.

He ran.

The belt moved beneath him and with him, shell casings skittering off the sides. The amber strobe chopped the chamber into frozen images: workers flattened against side surfaces, a supervisor with both hands raised, blaster impacts walking across the conveyor in bursts of heated composite. One shot passed close enough for him to feel its heat through his sleeve. He did not deflect it; there was no clean line that did not end in a worker or an explosive casing, and he was already committed to the opening.

He tucked his lightsaber in close, dropped into a slide, and went through.

The opening was exactly as small as it had looked. He squeezed through a narrow transfer section—a meter of enclosed conveyor between chamber and whatever came next—with his shoulders scraping both sides and the smell of propellant and heated casing material thick in the confined air. Then the belt carried him clear, and he was in a loading bay.

Ben was already up, holocam still running, filming the opening behind them.

"—and we're coming out the other side now," Ben said. "There's a loading bay. I can see a vehicle area and what looks like an exit ramp." He lowered the holocam. "Hi."

"Hi," Jacen said. He rose from the belt and checked his saber wrist. Sore, but useful.

Behind them, amber light continued to strobe through the opening. Boots thudded on the conveyor beyond, then a blaster shot fired blind through the opening punched into the loading-bay side two meters to their left.

The troops were not going to fit through the opening in any worthwhile numbers. But they would find stairs.

"Exit ramp," Jacen said.

Ben was already moving.

They crossed the loading bay at a run, past idling repulsorlift pallets, stacked empty crates, and a bored-looking dock worker who apparently had not yet interpreted the alarm correctly. Cool air from the exterior ramp reached toward them as they ran.

Behind them, through the opening and chamber and hidden passage and all the durasteel between, the surviving troopers were still firing into the conveyor belt they had just vaulted—the bursts short and futile, the sound of them muffled by distance and architecture into something almost rhythmic. Jacen squeezed through the diminutive opening at the end of an assembly line—following Ben, who had just landed and scrambled through ahead of him—while behind them the surviving troopers continued firing into the conveyor belt they had just vaulted.
