“He doesn’t exist.”

Mara was awake before the last word left his mouth. She came up on one elbow, red hair spilling across her cheek, and fixed on him. No grogginess at all. Her bright green eyes were already doing what they always did—cataloguing, assessing, deciding whether she needed the lightsaber on the shelf above his head.

Luke sat upright against the headboard, staring into the dimness. On the table, the small Tatooine-made sandglass statuette caught a sliver of light—some gap in the shielding, perhaps—and held it. He barely saw it.

“The dream again,” she said. Not a question.

“Clearer this time.” He rubbed the heel of his hand against one eye, then let it fall. “Three times in two months, and tonight it was sharper. More detail. More weight.”

Mara drew herself upright beside him, one knee tucked under her, and waited. Over the years she had learned to let him give a thing its shape before she started cutting into it.

“A figure,” he said. “Hooded. Moving through—not darkness exactly, but shadows made out of light and ignorance and denial. As if he’s chosen not to be seen, and the choice itself hides him.” He paused. “This being causes tremendous pain. To the galaxy. To me.”

“And you can’t see the face.”

“I can’t see the face.” He let out a slow breath through his nose. “The Force-weight behind it is wrong for memory. It isn’t an echo. It isn’t old experience bleeding into sleep. This is present tense. Whatever it is, it’s already in motion.”

“I know,” she said. “You told me that after the second one.”

“I know.” He glanced at her. “I needed to say it again.”

She reached down and straightened the blanket over her legs with one exact tug, the way she did everything. “All right. Let’s work it. Hooded figure. Patient. Already moving. Galaxy-scale pain, with a personal note for you.” She tipped her head slightly. “Raynar.”

Luke was shaking his head before she finished.

“He’s in a Temple cell,” she said, not pushing, just completing the thought. “Well protected. Post-Killik, still being treated. He fits the pain category.”

“Not Raynar.” The answer came from deeper than logic, from the same place the vision had. “What I sense there is human. Near-human, at most. There’s no hive-resonance. None of the insectoid alienness in the way the presence moves through the Force. The moment you said his name, I knew.”

Mara gave one short nod and set it aside. “All right. Not Raynar.”

She went quiet, and Luke could feel her thinking—the particular stillness Mara had when she was sorting possibilities and not sharing them yet.

He looked across the room. The wall rack of plasteel staves and practice weapons cast thin, angled shadows.

“There’s another possibility,” he said.

She waited.

“My father.” He kept his voice level, the way he had taught himself to say things that cost him. “Anakin, in the middle of the fall. The face is still a Jedi’s face—I knew that much at once. I’ve seen the holos, the early ones, from before. But in the vision the eyes are wrong. They’re turning. Gold. Or orange. The color that comes from—” He broke off.

“From the dark side,” Mara said quietly. “From anger used too long.”

“Yes.”

The room went still around them. Somewhere below, deeper in the enclave, a night-shift sentry’s footstep touched stone and faded.

Luke had already worried this over before saying it aloud. After the second dream he had lain awake beside Mara and told himself it was grief-residue, his mind reaching for a familiar shape to fill an unfamiliar warning. Tonight had stripped that comfort away. The vision had come again, and the eyes had been clearer—that molten orange-gold, the color of something heating toward a fixed point.

“My father is dead,” he said. “I know that. I was there. What I’m seeing isn’t a ghost. It isn’t even him, not exactly. It’s the pattern. A Jedi with the capacity to go that way, and already going.”

Mara studied him for a moment. Something moved across her expression—not concern exactly, not calculation either. Whatever it was, she kept it to herself.

“So the vision is showing you a shape,” she said. “Not a person.”

“A shape without a face.” He spread one hand against the blanket, then curled it shut. “Which is why I said what I said. Whoever I’m being warned about—whatever his name, whatever body he’ll wear—I can’t point to him. I look at the people I know, the people who fit the Force-register of what I’m sensing, and none of them are this. The thing the vision is warning me about doesn’t exist yet.” He paused. “Or I haven’t met him. Or I have, and I’m failing to see it.”

“Three possibilities,” Mara said. “All equally useless before dawn.”

“Yes.”

Silence settled again. The residue of the dream still sat in the room with them—not darkness exactly, but pressure, like the air before weather breaks. He knew the texture of his own anxiety too well to confuse it with this. This came from outside him. This was real.

The statuette on the table picked up that thread of light again as the shielding shifted with the enclave’s night air-cycling. It was a crude little thing—sandstone, grit, someone’s determined approximation of a human profile. He had kept it for twenty years without ever fully deciding why.

“You’re not going to take this to the Council,” Mara said. No accusation in it.

“Not yet. I have a recurring dream about an enemy who doesn’t exist. The Council would listen very carefully and then ask me what I expected them to do with that.”

“Fair.”

“I’ll tell them when I have something to tell them.” He turned and looked at her fully. “I’m telling you now because you asked me to. After the first one.”

“I did.” She held his gaze. “And I’m still asking.”

He nodded. There was something in that simple geometry—the asking and the answering—that made the burden a little easier to carry. Not lighter. Not solved. Just shared.

Mara brushed her hair back from her face, a motion so familiar he almost didn’t see it. “All right,” she said. “Here’s my tactical read.”

He waited.

“We don’t know who he is. We don’t know when. We don’t know whether the dream is showing something that can be prevented or something that has to be survived.” She looked at him steadily. “So let’s wait until he shows up, then crush him.”

The laugh moved through Luke before he chose to allow it—a short, real sound. The Force-weight remained, patient as ever, but Mara had named the only response available, and she had done it without ceremony.

He smiled.

“Reasonable,” he said.

“I thought so.” She reached past him and tugged the blanket straight on his side as neatly as she had on hers. “Sleep. You have the Corellia briefing at seventh hour.”

He closed his eyes. The residue of the dream still lingered at the edges—that hooded patience, those burning eyes—but it sat differently now. He breathed in, then out, and felt his body begin making its own decision to let go.

Within a minute, he was asleep.

Mara stayed upright a little longer. She looked at the wall, at the practice staves, at the little statuette holding its scrap of light. Then she closed her eyes and reached carefully through the Force—not for the dream itself, which was his and not hers to pursue, but for whatever had sent it. Whatever was moving out there in that deliberate shadow.

She found nothing.

Not proof that there was no threat. Just nothing she could read. That was not the same thing, and she knew it.

She filed that away too, in the part of her mind reserved for unfinished things.

Then she lay back down, drew the blanket up, and slept.
