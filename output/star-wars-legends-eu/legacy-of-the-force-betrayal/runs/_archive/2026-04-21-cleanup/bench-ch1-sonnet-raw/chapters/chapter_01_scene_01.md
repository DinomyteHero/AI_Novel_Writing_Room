"He doesn't exist."

Luke said it to the dark, to no one, to the ceiling of their sleeping chamber. He was already sitting upright, both hands flat on the bedsheet, before he understood that the words had left him aloud.

Mara was awake beside him. She hadn't made a sound doing it. Her red hair was a tangle against the pillow she'd pushed herself up from, and her green eyes were already clear—not the eyes of someone dragged out of sleep, but of someone who had been waiting for the room to wake around her.

"Okay," she said. Her voice stayed low, but it was all the way there. "Tell me."

He let out a slow breath. The residue of the vision still clung to him—not an image exactly, more like pressure behind the sternum that hadn't finished easing off. Three times in two months, and each time sharper than the last. Tonight he'd come awake with the certainty already formed, the words already at his mouth before his conscious mind could sort through what they meant.

"Hooded," he said. "Human, or close enough that I can't tell the difference. Moving toward something specific—not running, not retreating. Just moving, with complete certainty about where he's going." He paused, reaching back into the memory of it. "He's a source of pain. Not random. Personal. And the scale of it—" He broke off again. Some things in the Force refused language without losing shape on the way through.

Mara waited. She was good at that.

"Galactic and personal," Luke said. "Both at once."

"Raynar," she said.

He'd expected that. It was the obvious candidate—Raynar Thul, former Jedi Knight, still housed in a protected cell in the Temple while the healers worked through what the Killiks had done to him. The burns alone would have been enough to reshape a person. What came after the burns had been something else entirely.

Luke reached back for the vision, testing the memory of it against the name.

"No," he said. "The presence in the dream is—" He searched for the right word. "Clean. Human. Whatever Raynar is now, when I sit with him there's a quality to his mind that isn't his anymore. Something borrowed, or absorbed, or layered over. The dream doesn't have that."

Mara nodded once. She wasn't arguing, only tracking.

"Who else?" she said.

He'd been sitting with the other name since before he woke. He hadn't wanted to say it. He said it anyway, because that was the point of telling Mara—to voice the things he hadn't sorted through yet so she could help him find the edges.

"My father," he said. "Not Vader. Anakin, mid-fall. The face he still had. The eyes turning."

The room stayed quiet. The dim illumination caught the small Tatooine statuette on the table—crude sandglass, a gift from someone who'd meant well—and threw a shadow across the wall that didn't look like much of anything.

Mara let the silence sit for a moment before she spoke. "You're dreaming about Anakin?"

"I'm testing whether the shape fits." He kept his voice level. "It's the same structural pattern. A Jedi with the capacity to fall, moving toward something that will cost everyone around him." He paused. "The pattern is familiar. I know what it looks like. I've studied it."

"But."

"But the weight is wrong." He kept his hands flat on the sheet and made himself look at them rather than at the wall. "My father's fall was—there was grief in it. Desperation. The thing in the dream moves like it's already decided. Cold. Not angry, not in pain. Just certain."

He felt Mara's hand cover his. She didn't say anything for a moment.

"Anakin's eyes were gold," Luke said. "In the reports, the recordings. That color that comes when the dark side is close to the surface. The vision has something like that, but the face doesn't resolve. When I try to look directly at it, it's—" He exhaled. "He doesn't exist. That's the only description I have. He's real, I sense that much, but I can't point at anyone and say that's him."

"Not Raynar. Not Anakin." Mara's thumb moved once across his knuckles. "Anyone currently on the Council?"

"I considered that."

"And?"

"None of them fit the pressure of it."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure."

She was quiet a moment longer. He could feel her moving through the same list he'd been working through since the first dream—every Jedi Knight currently active, every student in the Temple, every former Jedi who'd drifted through the decades since the Order's rebuilding. The list was long. None of the names on it produced the resonance the vision had.

"I'm not going to bring it to the Council," Luke said. "Not yet. I don't have anything to bring. I have a feeling and a description that amounts to a man in a hood who moves with purpose. That's not a threat briefing."

"No," Mara agreed. "That's a horoscope."

He almost smiled. "I'll record it. Date, time, what I sensed. If it happens again, I'll have a pattern to show."

"And if it doesn't happen again?"

"Then I'll have been wrong about the pressure of it." He didn't believe that. The Force didn't press on him like this for nothing. But the discipline of leaving room for his own error was one he'd learned at cost and didn't abandon easily.

Mara was quiet for a moment. He could feel her weighing something, deciding whether to say it.

"Whatever it is," she said finally, "it's not active right now. I'd feel it if it were close." She said it the way she said most things—without decoration, as a statement of operational fact. She'd been reading Force-presences since before he'd known her, in circumstances that would have broken most people's perceptual clarity entirely. If she wasn't feeling it, that meant something.

"I know," he said.

"So we wait."

"We wait."

She considered this for another three seconds. "Let's wait until he shows up," she said, "then crush him."

He smiled. He couldn't help it—the precision of it, the complete refusal to be impressed by the unknowable threat, the confidence that assumed the ending before the beginning had even declared itself. Thirty years of marriage, and she could still find the exact right thing to say to make the pressure in his chest ease.

He lay back down. The pillow was cool on the side he'd left, and the familiar pressure of her shoulder against his arm settled him in a way that no amount of meditation had managed tonight. His breathing slowed on its own. The residue of the vision was still there, somewhere at the edges, but it had stopped demanding attention.

He was asleep within a minute.

Mara lay still beside him and listened to his breathing even out. Then, carefully, she opened herself to the Force—not a full reach, just a light extension, the kind of scan she'd learned to perform without disturbing the room around her. She moved her attention outward from the chamber, from the Temple, toward whatever had pressed on Luke hard enough to wake him three times in two months.

She found nothing. No shadow, no cold certainty, no hooded presence moving toward anything. The Force around the Temple was quiet, the way it usually was before Coruscant's traffic lanes reached full morning density.

She held the scan for a few minutes longer. Then she let it go.

Whatever Luke had sensed was real—she didn't doubt that, hadn't doubted it for a moment. The Force-weight behind his waking had been visible in his posture, in the flatness of his voice when he'd first spoken. It was real and it was not yet here.

She could work with that.

Mara closed her eyes. Outside, Coruscant's light was beginning its slow crawl toward dawn, and the city that never fully stopped shifted into its morning register. She listened to it for a while, her husband's breathing a quiet counterpoint, and then she too slept.
