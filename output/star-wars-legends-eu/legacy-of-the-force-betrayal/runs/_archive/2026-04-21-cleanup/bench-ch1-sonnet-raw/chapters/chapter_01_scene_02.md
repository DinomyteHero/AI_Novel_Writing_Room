The turbolift doors slid apart, and the smell hit first.

Propellant. Composite resins curing under heat. The mineral bite of freshly machined casing alloy, and beneath all of it, heavier, the dense tang of high explosive packed tight in enclosed spaces. Not the smell of death, not exactly—more the smell of death being manufactured, assembled from components, prepared for shipping.

Jacen Solo stepped through the doors and onto the catwalk without breaking stride.

His cloak caught the updraft from the manufacturing floor twelve meters below and billowed behind him—not a theatrical choice, just physics, but he let it work. His boot heels rang on the metal grating with each step, a clean, deliberate sound that carried across the chamber’s ambient noise. He didn’t slow to let Testan ke Harran catch up. If the guide wanted to present his facility, he could present it from farther back.

The chamber was larger than most military hangars Jacen had worked in. Eight assembly lanes ran parallel beneath the catwalk, each divided from its neighbors by chest-high duracrete barriers. Conveyor belts ran white and luminous down the length of each lane, carrying materials through worker stations at intervals. At the far end of the nearest lane, hazmat-suited figures bent over components the color of new leaves—luminous green, vivid even at this distance, the high-explosive spheres that would eventually become someone’s tactical problem. Each one was roughly the size of a human head.

Jacen read the facility through the Force the way a pilot reads weather.

The workers were calm. Not the calm of people who had nothing to hide—the calm of people who had done the same job long enough that nervousness had been worn away by routine. The institutional rhythm of the place was steady, practiced, confident. Whatever was wrong here had been wrong long enough that it no longer felt wrong to the people inside it.

He kept walking. Behind him he heard Testan’s rapid footsteps and then Ben’s voice, cheerful and slightly too loud.

“—so when he said ‘inspection,’ did he mean the whole facility, or just the sactions that are on the official schematic? Because I notice those two don’t match up, and I want to make sure we’re not wasting your time.”

Testan’s voice came back, slightly breathless: “All sactions ar open to Galactic Alliance review, of carse. We ar proud of our facility. The discrapancy you notaice is simply a matter of different filing periods—”

“Different filing periods,” Ben said. “Got it. Jacen, he says different filing periods.”

Jacen didn’t turn around. “I heard him.”

“Right, he heard you. Sorry, he usually needs me to translate. The accent.” A beat. “No offense.”

“None takan,” Testan said, with the tone of someone taking considerable offense.

Jacen stopped at the railing and looked down into the nearest assembly lane. A row of sensor-assembly workers sat at a long bench, each one plugging cables into housing units and watching the results on black-and-white monitors. The monitors showed waveform readouts that meant nothing to Jacen visually but told him through the Force that the sensors were functional, calibrated, ready to guide something to a target. To his left, another group worked casing housings with rubber-headed hammers, tapping a searching rhythm against the composite shells, listening for the hollow note that meant a crack.

Testan arrived beside him. The guide was dressed for the occasion—flowing orange-and-purple diamond-patterned tunic, gold trousers, a scarf that matched the belt. His beard was the kind of beard that required maintenance, black and lustrous, shaped with visible intention.

Ben materialized on Jacen’s other side. “He wants to know if you’d like to start with the sensor-assembly saction or the casing saction.”

“Both,” Jacen said. He let his gaze move slowly along the lane below. “Tell him I want to see the full production cycle.”

“He wants—”

“I heard him,” Testan said again, more firmly.

Ben glanced at Jacen. Jacen kept his attention on the floor below, but he felt the boy’s suppressed amusement clearly enough. Ben had figured out the game within the first thirty seconds and had been playing it without prompting ever since. That was worth noting.

“Excellent,” Ben said, switching registers smoothly. “So, Testan—can I call you Testan? I have a question. The beard.” He gestured vaguely. “What kind of maintenance does that require? Because I’ve been thinking about growing one, but I don’t know where to start.”

Testan blinked. “I—the beard?”

“It’s very impressive. Imprassive, sorry. Do you use oil? I feel like it must be oil. Or is it more of a butter situation?”

“It is not a—there is no butter involved in—”

“Because my uncle uses butter on everything. Not for grooming, I mean. Just in general. He’s got a very practical approach to—”

Jacen turned away from them and walked east along the catwalk.

He moved without hurrying. The ring of his boot heels marked his pace against the grating, steady and unhurried, the sound of a man who had all the time available to him and knew it. Below, a worker glanced up from a sensor housing, tracked him for a moment, then looked back down. The facility’s security personnel—he’d counted six on entry, orange-and-green uniforms, carapace armor that reminded him of speeder-bike troopers—were stationed at the lane intersections. Two of them had shifted position when the turbolift opened. They were watching him now with the particular attention of people who had been told to watch.

He kept his Force-sense extended and quiet.

Testan’s guilt was the loudest thing in the room.

It wasn’t a dramatic presence—not fear, not the spike of adrenaline from active deception. It was older than that, more settled. The guilt of a man who had made a decision some months or years ago and had since organized his entire professional life around the fact of it. Jacen had encountered that particular quality before, in other facilities, other guides. It had a texture. Heavy, slightly warm, like metal that had been sitting in sunlight.

And under the guilt, pushed down but not buried: a memory.

He didn’t reach for it. He walked, and he let the memory press against the surface of Testan’s mind under its own weight, the way a bruise makes itself known without being touched.

Behind him, Ben was asking whether the beard required a separate brush or whether a regular comb would work.

Jacen stopped.

He stood in front of a section of railing where the wall to his right—the facility’s eastern interior wall—was slightly different from the sections on either side of it. Not visually. The duracrete looked identical, the same gray-brown aggregate, the same maintenance seams. But the Force read it differently. There was a quality of concealment to it, not in the wall itself but in the space beyond it. A held breath.

He looked at the surface for three seconds.

Then he said, “There is something here. Something wrong.”

He said it to no one in particular, quietly, the way he might note a navigational anomaly. Behind him he heard Testan’s conversation with Ben go silent.

The memory broke through.

It came fast and involuntary—Testan’s mind releasing it the way a fist releases something it has been gripping too long. Jacen caught it without reaching: a broad gray door, descending from above, its lower edge sweeping the floor clean as it dropped. Supervisors in dark blue jumpsuits moving ahead of it, quick-stepping, one of them looking back over his shoulder. The door seating into its housing with a sound like finality. And beyond the door, stacked in rows that went back further than the passage should have allowed—

Jacen turned. “Ben.”

Ben was already moving. He’d read the shift in Jacen’s posture or the change in his Force-presence or simply the particular quality of that one syllable—it didn’t matter which. He unclipped his lightsaber from his belt as he came, and Jacen stepped back to give him access to the wall.

“That’s a practice saber,” Testan said. His voice had changed. “It cannot—”

The blue blade ignited with a snap-hiss, and Ben drew it in a long arc across the wall panel, a meter and a half of glowing cut that curved back on itself. The duracrete didn’t resist the way the guide expected. The blade went through it cleanly, leaving edges that radiated heat in visible waves.

“From a certain point of view,” Jacen said, “he does practice with it.”

The panel fell inward and away into the space beyond.

The hidden passage ran the full length of the eastern wall. Crates filled it from floor to ceiling, stacked in precise lots, each one stenciled with the same designation in block letters: DAMMANT KILLER MODEL 16. Below that, in red: DANGER. Below that, in larger red: DO NOT DROP.

Ben looked at the crates. Then he looked at Jacen. “How many is that?”

“Lots of twenty-four,” Jacen said. He counted rows. “At least sixty lots visible from here.”

Testan made a sound that wasn’t quite a word.

Jacen turned to look at him. The guide’s face had gone through several colors in rapid succession and had settled on something pale. His hands were at his sides, not reaching for anything, which was the correct instinct.

“Sit down,” Jacen told him. “On the catwalk. Don’t use your comlink.”

Testan sat.

From the far end of the passage, boots rang against permacrete. Not one set. Several, coordinating, the sound of a security response that had been watching the sensor feeds and had just seen what they were looking at.

Jacen ignited his lightsaber.

The green blade threw light across the stacked crates, across the walls, across Ben’s face—the boy had his own saber up and had moved to Jacen’s left without being told. Good. Jacen shifted his grip and addressed the approaching sound.

“Stay behind me.”

Six security troops came around the far corner at a run, blasters already up. Orange-and-green uniforms, carapace armor, the same model Jacen had clocked on entry. They were moving with discipline—spread formation, not bunched—and the lead trooper was calling something in Adumari-accented Basic that Jacen processed and set aside. The gist was stop and surrender, which was not the current plan.

Jacen walked toward them.

Not quickly. Not at a run. He moved at the same measured pace he’d used on the catwalk, the green blade turning in front of him in the butterfly pattern—folding arcs, geometry, the blade’s reflected light catching the passage walls at angles that made it hard to track. He kept his breathing even.

The lead trooper fired.

Jacen turned the bolt back into the passage wall above the formation. A second shot came from the trooper’s left; he redirected it into the ceiling. The third shot he sent into the lead trooper’s helmet—not the face, the back of the helmet, a ricochet angle—and the commander dropped without a sound, knocked cold by the impact of his own bolt.

The remaining five troopers fired simultaneously.

“Could you speak up?” Jacen said. “I’m a little deaf.”

Behind him, Ben made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

The five bolts came in a tight cluster—disciplined shooting, genuinely—and Jacen took three on the blade, redirected one into the shoulder of the trooper on the far left, and let the fifth go high into the ceiling. The shoulder shot spun its target into the trooper beside him; both went down in a tangle. Three left.

The second wave arrived before the first had finished falling.

Six more troops came from the catwalk entrance at Jacen’s back—he’d felt them coordinating through the Force a full four seconds before they appeared, which was why he was already turning. He brought the blade up and around, caught the first burst of fire on the flat of it, and sent the reflected shots back across the passage at a shallow angle. Two troopers from the second wave went down to their own fire. A third caught the ricochet from the wall and sat down hard against the duracrete, dazed.

The remaining four from the second wave and three from the first were regrouping at both ends of the passage, and Jacen could feel a third group assembling somewhere beyond the catwalk entrance. He ran a quick calculation.

“Ben,” he said. “Now would be a good time for the holocam.”

“On it.” The sound of Ben’s saber powering down, then the small electronic click of the holocam activating. “Um,” Ben said, in a tone of focused concentration, “this is Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight Jacen Solo and I are in a secret part of the Dammant Killers plant, in the city of Cartann, on the planet Adumar. We found it behind a false wall on the east side of the main manufacturing floor.” A pause. “Their guys are trying to kill us. We’ve got—” another pause, the sound of him counting, “—a lot of crates of Model 16 concussion missiles. I’ll get closer.”

Jacen stepped back to cover Ben’s movement toward the crates.

The third group came through the catwalk entrance—eight troops, better organized than the first two waves, the lead pair carrying what looked like riot shields. That changed the deflection geometry. Jacen shifted his weight and moved the blade into a longer arc, reading the shield angles through the Force, looking for the gaps. The shields were good against direct shots. They were less good against shots that came in from above.

He sent the next three deflections into the ceiling at steep angles and let them come down.

Two troopers dropped. The remaining six spread out and kept advancing, the shield-bearers providing cover for the blasters behind them.

“Got it,” Ben said. “I’ve got the crate markings, the lot numbers, the DANGER labels—Jacen, there are shipping manifests on the panel back here.”

“Get them.”

“Getting them.”

A blaster bolt came in low, aimed at Jacen’s knees—someone in the third group had figured out that head-height shots weren’t working. He dropped the blade and caught it, redirected it upward, felt the hilt vibrate with the impact. His right hand registered the heat of it through the grip, a sharp reminder of the physics involved. He adjusted his angle and kept moving.

The manifests on the panel would be the mission’s best evidence. The crates themselves were visible on the holocam footage, but a shipping manifest with dates, quantities, and destinations—that was the kind of document a GA court could build a case on. Ben was right to go for it.

From somewhere in the manufacturing floor beyond the passage, an alarm began sounding—a low, cycling tone that meant the facility’s lockdown protocols were activating. Jacen had expected that. It meant the exits were closing, which meant the escape route through the assembly lines was now the only reliable option.

“Ben. Rail.”

“Thirty seconds.”

“Fifteen.”

“Twenty.”

Jacen took two quick steps back toward Ben’s position, putting himself between the advancing third group and the boy. The shield-bearers were close enough now that he could see the expressions of the troopers behind them—focused, professional, not panicking. He respected that. He sent a deflection into the junction of the left shield-bearer’s armor, not the face, and the man stumbled but didn’t fall. The second shield-bearer angled his shield and kept coming.

“I’ve got the manifests on holocam,” Ben said. “Okay. Rail.”

They moved.

The rail was ten meters back through the passage opening, and they covered it at a run—Jacen’s cloak whipping back, Ben’s footsteps quick and light at his side. Behind them the third group was shouting, coordinating, the sound of boots against permacrete coming fast. Jacen hit the rail, planted his hand on it, vaulted.

The drop to the manufacturing floor was four meters. He landed in a roll, came up on his feet between two assembly lanes, and had the blade back up before the roll was finished. Ben landed at his side half a second later, already moving, the holocam in one hand and his saber in the other.

“Which lane?” Ben said.

Jacen read the floor through the Force—the workers scattering from their stations, the security troops repositioning on the catwalk above, the layout of the lanes and their convergence at the far end of the chamber. “Third from the left.”

They ran.

The lane was loaded with opalescent shell casings riding a white conveyor belt toward the far wall. Jacen kept pace with Ben, letting the boy set the speed. Above them on the catwalk, two troopers had gotten to the railing and were firing down into the lanes—shots that kicked off the belt surface and rang off the duracrete barriers. Jacen deflected the ones that had angles, let the others go wide.

“Dad always said,” Ben said, slightly breathless, “you never want to fight the enemy on ground he’s chosen if you can avoid it.”

“Your father’s right,” Jacen said.

“I’m just saying. We came here. This is their ground.”

“We came here with a plan. There’s a difference.”

“Is there?”

A shot came in from the catwalk—well-aimed, low, intended to catch Jacen’s legs. He turned it back without breaking stride and the trooper who’d fired ducked behind the rail. “Yes,” Jacen said.

The far wall was approaching. The conveyor belt ended at a portal—a small rectangular opening in the wall, sized for the opalescent casings moving along the belt, not for human beings. Ben didn’t slow down. He hit the end of the belt at speed, dropped to his knees in a controlled slide, and went through the portal headfirst, his voice briefly audible from the other side: “It’s clear—”

Jacen was three steps behind him.

A shot came in from his right—not from the catwalk, from floor level, a trooper who’d made it down ahead of the others and had positioned himself at the lane junction. The angle was bad for deflection; sending it back would put it into the neighboring lane where two hazmat-suited workers were still crouched behind their station, not yet clear. Jacen turned the blade and took the bolt on the flat near the hilt instead.

The vibration ran up through his palm and wrist, a deep, bone-level shock that made his grip go briefly uncertain. He tightened it, kept moving, dropped to his knees at the portal’s edge.

Behind him, the troopers who’d come down from the catwalk were reaching the lane, their shots pocking the conveyor belt surface, kicking up fragments of opalescent casing material. None of it was aimed well enough to matter.

Jacen deactivated his lightsaber, clipped it to his belt, and pushed through the portal.

The space on the other side was a second assembly chamber, smaller than the first, its own conveyor belts running in dimmer light. Ben was already on his feet, the holocam held up to capture the room. The portal opening was at their backs, and through it came the sound of continued fire—the troopers shooting into the belt they had just vaulted, still working through the tactical problem of where their targets had gone.

They’d figure it out shortly.

“Exit?” Ben said.

Jacen felt through the Force for the facility’s layout—the lockdown protocols had sealed the main entrances, but a manufacturing chamber this size had emergency ventilation routes, maintenance corridors, service hatches sized for equipment that was larger than a human being. He found one to the northeast, beyond a row of inactive assembly stations.

“Northeast corner,” he said. “Move.”

Ben moved. Jacen followed, his right hand still registering the heat from the bolt he’d taken, the ache in his palm that would remind him for the next several days that physics applied equally to Jedi Knights. He noted it and set it aside.

The evidence was recorded. Ben was intact. The manifest footage would give the GA court everything it needed to tie the Model 16 stockpile to a supply chain, and the supply chain would lead somewhere that mattered.

At their backs, the troopers were still firing.
