The shuttle broke atmosphere over a gray ocean that had been battering the coastline since long before the prison went up.

Ben saw Tarn Velos Coastal Detention Works through the viewport as the transport banked on final approach: a sprawl of low durasteel buildings braced against a cliff face, sea walls climbing in stepped tiers to meet the storm surge, landing pads clustered on the eastern edge as if somebody had added them after the fact. Weather over a place like this never seemed to stop being weather. Rain drove sideways. Whitecaps boiled on the water. The sea wall took the pounding in a steady rhythm Ben felt through the shuttle’s deck plates before the ship even touched down.

He was the only passenger. The transport had been running at quarter load since Coruscant, and the others had peeled off at the civilian terminal two stops back. Tarn Velos wasn’t the sort of place people shared a ride to.

Processing took forty minutes. Ben moved through the visitor intake protocol with the same quiet patience he’d used in GA facilities during the war, handing over the ALLIED PARTY badge and the visitor authorization Dorrin had filed, answering the standard questions in a voice that invited nothing extra. The intake officer, a middle-aged human woman with the institutional stillness of someone who had spent too many years processing visitors to remain curious about them, checked his credentials against the system, found the authorization in order, and handed him a visitor pass with a number on it and no name. She checked his credentials against the system, found the authorization in order, and handed him a visitor pass with a number on it and no name.

“Records auditor,” she said, not quite asking.

“GA Justice.” Ben clipped the pass to his jacket. “Material witness interview. Should be straightforward.”

Her expression didn’t shift. “Visitor room four. Forty minutes standard. The inmate will be brought in once you’re seated.”

Ben nodded and followed the corridor indicators toward the visitor wing. Inside, the prison was all gray durasteel and recycled air, the same institutional palette as every detention facility he’d ever been in, but the sound was off. Storm surge slammed the sea wall through the ventilation as a constant low percussion, a bass note that lived in the walls and made the spaces between footsteps feel heavier than they should. Salt threaded the air. The cold wasn’t temperature so much as the absence of anything warm.

He catalogued the environment as he walked. Exits: two visible from the main corridor, a third marked by emergency lighting near the turbolift bank. Guard station: elevated, transparisteel-fronted, sightlines covering the visitor processing desk and the corridor approach. Camera coverage: one per junction, standard placement, no obvious blind spots. The read was automatic, older than his attention. He let it run while the rest of him stayed on the meeting ahead.

Talon Veyr. Former GAG field agent, Tess’s protégé until the operational incident that had moved him to records and logistics. Eighteen months under Elya Rane, who had argued for keeping him on despite disciplinary concerns. Four years in Tarn Velos on a manifest-authorization fraud conviction the prosecution file had been careful about — careful enough to mean investigators had known they were only seeing part of the picture.

Ben had read the file twice on the shuttle. Talon Veyr knew the transfer architecture better than anyone in Elya’s office. He’d been convicted for doing what someone had done to the duplicate manifest. He could read the fragment not as a decoder but as a witness — someone who recognized the shape of concealment because he’d worked inside the systems that made it possible.

Whether he’d talk was a different question. Whether he’d trust a Jedi with GAG history showing up with a wartime manifest was a harder one.

The visitor room was smaller than Ben expected. Gray durasteel walls. A cold metal table bolted to the floor. Two chairs on either side of a transparisteel partition running the length of the room. The partition was thick enough to stop blaster fire and clear enough to show every detail of the person on the other side. A single light panel hummed overhead, just a shade off from the ventilation. The storm surge was louder here — the room must sit closer to the sea wall, or the shaft ran straighter.

Ben sat in the chair facing the door on his side of the partition. He could see the guard station through the transparisteel behind him, reflected faintly in the panel. The angle was fine. The exits were where he’d noted them. The camera in the corner swept on its standard pattern.

He waited.

The door on the other side of the partition opened, and Talon Veyr walked in.

The booking image in Dorrin’s file hadn’t captured the restlessness. Talon was lean in the way prison made people lean — not underfed, exactly, but carrying less weight than his frame wanted, the thinness that came from institutional calories and too few reasons to eat more of them. His hair was darker than the file photo, cut short in the standard prison crop, and his eyes were already moving across the room before his feet finished crossing the threshold.

He picked the chair with the door sightline before Ben could note the choice. One motion — a half-step to adjust his path, a hand on the chair back, a pivot that put his shoulders to the wall and his face to the door — and he was seated, the move so practiced it looked like preference instead of training.

Then he pocketed the nutrition bar wrapper from the table without looking at it first. His hand scooped it up on the way past, tucked it into the cuff of his sleeve, and was still again before Ben could fully catch the motion. A small thing. A wrapper, probably left by the last visitor or a guard prepping the room. Talon hadn’t glanced at it. He’d just taken it, the way someone took things in a place where even scraps had value.

Ben filed it away. The prison habits showed before Talon said a word. The visitor room was his territory more than Ben’s, and he’d proved it twice without breaking stride.

Talon looked at Ben through the transparisteel. His eyes did the same inventory Ben had done — exits, camera, guard station angle — and then settled on Ben’s face with an expression already halfway to a joke.

“GA Justice records auditor,” Talon said. “That’s what the visitor log says. I’ve been here four years and I’ve never had a records auditor visit before. You’d think I’d be popular. I’m full of records.”

His voice was faster than Ben expected, Coruscanti edges worn smooth by four years of talking to people from everywhere else. The joke landed lightly, but his eyes kept moving — reading Ben’s posture, his hands, the way he sat.

Ben let the silence hold for a beat. “You’re not. That’s not why I’m here.”

“No,” Talon agreed. He leaned back in his chair, easy, sightline to the door still perfect. “You’re not a records auditor. You walked in here like somebody who’s been in detention facilities before, and not on the visitor side. You checked the exits before you sat down. You’re carrying yourself like you expect someone to tell you to leave, and you already know what you’ll say when they do.” He tipped his head. “Also, you look exactly like your father, which is going to be a problem for you in undercover work for the rest of your life. Skywalker.”

Ben didn’t react. He’d known Talon would clock him; the file had made it clear Talon read people the way Ben read rooms, fast and mostly right. The only question had been how long it would take.

Thirty seconds, apparently. Maybe less.

“And you were GAG,” Talon went on, same light, quick rhythm, the jokes still doing work under the surface. “Not just attached. In it. The way you stand — that isn’t Jedi training. That’s Guard. You learned it young enough that it stuck, which means you were what, fifteen? Sixteen? No. Fourteen. You were fourteen. Jacen put a fourteen-year-old in the Guard and now you’re here, in a prison, asking to talk to me.” He spread his hands. “This is already the best visitor day I’ve had in four years, and we haven’t even gotten to the part where you tell me why you’re really here.”

Ben let the silence stretch another beat. Talon’s jokes were calibrations — testing his patience, checking his tells, figuring out whether he was another system representative or something else. Getting defensive would fail the test. Performing patience would fail it faster.

“You’re right,” Ben said. “I was fourteen. I was in the GAG. My name is Ben Skywalker. I’m here because Elya Rane is dead, and she sent me a message before she died.”

The humor didn’t leave Talon’s face, but it stilled. The way water stilled before it froze.

“Elya,” he said. Not a question.

“She was killed three days ago on Coruscant. Someone used a Force-interrogation technique on her — precise, controlled, systematic. She’d found something in the old GAG records. A duplicate manifest with Jacen Solo’s personal encryption on it. She hid a fragment of it before they got to her, and she sent me a message through a dead channel asking me to come alone.”

Talon’s hands were on the table now, still visible, still relaxed, but his knuckles had gone a shade paler. “And you’re here because?”

“Because when Inspector Dorrin cross-referenced the manifest fragment against the prosecution files, your name came up. The authorization patterns in your conviction file match the audit trace on Elya’s fragment. Same method of concealment. Same underlying logic.” Ben met Talon’s eyes through the transparisteel. “I’m not here to reopen your case. I’m here because you know what this records environment looks like from the inside. You can read the fragment in a way no decoder can.”

“Because I did it.”

“Because you worked in the systems that made it possible. And because you worked under Elya for eighteen months, and she argued for keeping you on when other people wanted you gone.”

That landed. Talon’s expression flickered — something too quick to name, gone before it could become a reaction. He looked down at the table, then back up at Ben, and when he spoke again the joke was back in his voice, but thinner now, a blade with less handle.

“So you want me to look at a fragment of a dead woman’s last find and tell you what it means. In a prison visitor room. Under a time limit you don’t know I’m already calculating.” He smiled, quick and sharp. “Good. I love careful. Careful is what people say before asking me to crawl through something illegal.”

“I’m not asking you to crawl through anything. I’m asking you to look at a manifest and tell me if you recognize the pattern.”

“Same thing, different paperwork.” Talon leaned forward. “Show me.”

Ben pulled the datapad from his jacket. The fragment was loaded on it — not the original chip, which was still in evidence, but a secure copy Dorrin had authorized for the interview. He turned the datapad toward the transparisteel and let Talon read.

Talon’s eyes moved across the display. The humor came off his face in stages — first the smile, then the tension around his eyes, then the restlessness in his hands. He read the fragment once, then again, his lips moving slightly on the second pass.

“That’s not a clerical error,” he said.

“I know.”

“No, you don’t know what you’re looking at. This isn’t a mistake in the routing. Someone cloned a shipment identity — took a real manifest, duplicated it, and dressed the duplicate up as quarantine cargo. Medical supplies, survey equipment, the kind of thing that gets waved through because nobody wants to be the person who held up the bacta shipment.” He tapped the display. “See the classification overlay? That isn’t standard. That’s custom work. Someone built a disguise that would pass a surface inspection and buried a military shipment underneath it.”

“Quarantine cargo,” Ben said. “That’s the disguise.”

“That’s the disguise. And it’s good. The authorization chains are clean, the transfer stamps are in order, the routing codes match the destination profile. If you didn’t know what you were looking for, you’d file it and forget it.” Talon looked up. “Elya found this?”

“She found the duplicate. Two routings for the same shipment ID. One went where it was supposed to go. The other went to a destination called Iresh.”

Talon went still. Not the stillness of someone processing information — the stillness of someone recognizing a name he hadn’t expected to hear.

“Iresh,” he said.

“You know it.”

“I know of it. Survey world, outer edge of the Expansion Region. There was a GAG survey mission there during the war — classified, small footprint, Tess’s operation. I wasn’t on it, but I handled the logistics.” His voice had lost the joke entirely now. “If someone was routing secret shipments to Iresh under a quarantine disguise, they were doing it through Tess’s operation. And if Elya found the duplicate manifest, she found proof that someone was moving material off the books to a world Tess had already classified as a survey site.”

Ben felt the case snap into focus. The fragment wasn’t just evidence of a concealed shipment — it was evidence of a concealed operation, one that had run during the war under Tess’s authority and survived long enough to kill Elya when she got too close. The Vigil had a logistics trail. The Foundation had a destination. And Talon, sitting across from him with his hands flat on the cold metal table, had just given him the connection.

“Tess,” Ben said. “He’s still active.”

“I don’t know what Tess is. I haven’t seen him since the war.” Talon’s voice was flat in a way that wasn’t calm. “But if somebody’s running old GAG shipments to Iresh, Tess knows about it. He knew about everything that moved through his operations. And if Elya found a manifest that proved it —”

The alarm changed pitch.

Ben heard it before his brain caught up to the shift — a drop in frequency, the tone sliding from the steady pulse of a standard security alert into something lower, faster, off-kilter. The storm surge was still pounding the sea wall, the ventilation still humming at its slightly wrong pitch, but the alarm had changed, and the change meant something.

Talon’s body went still. Not the way it had when Ben mentioned Elya — that had been a reaction. This was something else. His shoulders squared, his hands came off the table, and his eyes went to the door with the flat, operational focus of a man who had stopped being a prisoner and started being a survivor.

“That’s wrong,” he said.

“The alarm?”

“The pattern. That isn’t lockdown. That isn’t drill. That’s —” He stopped, listening. The pitch shifted again, a third tone layered under the first two, and Talon’s jaw tightened. “That’s an evacuation override. Someone’s triggering the storm protocol. The sea wall alarms.”

Ben was already standing. “The storm protocol is for weather.”

“The storm protocol is for anything that needs prisoners moved inland. Flooding, structural failure, attack.” Talon stood too, his chair scraping back, his body already angled toward the door. “Someone’s pulling us out of the visitor wing. That isn’t procedure. Procedure is lockdown until the threat assessment clears. You don’t evacuate prisoners during a security alert unless —”

“Unless the alert is the cover.”

Talon looked at him. For a second, the humor was completely gone — no deflection, no calibration, just a man who had survived four years in a maximum-security prison by reading every threat before it arrived and was now reading one he couldn’t see.

“Skywalker,” he said. “Why are you really here?”

“I told you.”

“No. You told me about Elya and the fragment and the investigation. You didn’t tell me why someone would trigger an evacuation override in a coastal prison during a storm the same day you show up asking questions about Tess’s old operations.” Talon’s voice was fast again, but not joking — the speed of someone running calculations out loud. “You filed a visitor request through GA Justice. That went into the system. Someone saw it. Someone who’s been watching old GAG case trails, someone who knew what Elya found and didn’t want anyone else finding it. And now there’s an alarm going off that isn’t in the standard response matrix and we’re about to be moved somewhere that isn’t the visitor wing.”

Ben heard the guard station come alive — boots on durasteel, a voice on a comlink, the clatter of equipment being yanked from lockers. Whatever was happening, it was moving fast.

“How bad?” he asked.

“I don’t know yet.” Talon moved toward the door on his side of the partition, then stopped, one hand on the frame. “But they aren’t evacuating the whole prison. The pattern is localized. East wing, visitor block, processing.” He turned back to Ben. “They’re evacuating us.”

The door on Ben’s side opened. The intake officer from processing stood there, face professionally blank and shoulders tight, a comlink in one hand and a security baton in the other.

“Sir, we need to move you to the inland shelter. There’s a storm-surge warning —”

“That isn’t a storm-surge warning,” Talon said through the partition. “Check the alarm pattern. That’s an override, not a weather alert. Someone’s routing the evacuation protocol through the storm system.”

The officer looked at him, then at Ben, then at the comlink in her hand. “I have orders —”

“Your orders are wrong.” Talon’s voice was flat, certain, the voice of someone who had memorized every alarm pattern in the facility and knew exactly what each one meant. “The storm protocol triggers a two-tone alert with a thirty-second interval. This is three-tone with a fifteen-second interval. That’s an evacuation override. Someone’s using the storm system to move prisoners without authorization.”

The officer hesitated. Ben could see her running the calculation — the prisoner who knew the alarms better than she did, the visitor who wasn’t a records auditor, the orders that had come through faster than standard procedure allowed.

“How do you know that?” she asked.

“Because I’ve been here four years,” Talon said. “And I’ve heard every alarm this place has. That one’s wrong.”

The alarm shifted again — a fourth tone, higher, urgent. Down the corridor, someone shouted. Boots pounded on durasteel, moving away from the visitor wing.

“They’re clearing the guard station,” Ben said. “Who gave the evacuation order?”

“I don’t —” The officer glanced down at her comlink. “It came through the duty office. Priority override. I didn’t —”

“Who’s in the duty office?”

“I don’t know. The shift commander was supposed to —” She stopped. Her face changed. “The shift commander went off-duty an hour ago. The relief hasn’t checked in yet.”

Talon met Ben’s eyes through the transparisteel. The joke was gone. The restlessness was gone. What was left was the field agent who had survived Tess’s operations and Elya’s records office and four years in a maximum-security prison by knowing when the system was being used against him.

“They aren’t evacuating us,” Talon said. “They’re isolating us. Clear the wing, move the guards out, leave the visitor room sealed. Whoever triggered that alarm wants us cut off from the rest of the prison.”

“Or wants us somewhere specific.”

“Same thing.” Talon looked at the officer. “You need to get to the duty office and verify the override. Right now. If the shift commander didn’t authorize it, somebody’s running a false protocol through the storm system.”

The officer stared at him for a long second. Then she turned and ran.

Ben watched her go. The corridor was emptying — guards pulling back toward the main hub, the visitor processing desk abandoned, the camera in the corner still sweeping its standard pattern as if nothing had changed. The storm surge hammered the sea wall. The alarm kept cycling through its wrong pitch.

“Skywalker.” Talon’s voice had gone quiet, the flat register of a man who had stopped calculating and started deciding. “You came here to ask me about a manifest. You’re about to get more than that. Whatever’s happening, it’s happening because you’re here. Someone saw your visitor request and moved faster than the system could protect you.”

“I know.”

“Do you know what that means?”

Ben looked at him through the transparisteel — the man who had been Tess’s protégé, Elya’s subordinate, a convict on a charge that didn’t quite explain the full shape of what he’d done. A man who had just read an alarm pattern in four seconds and identified a false evacuation protocol before the guards could finish their orders.

“It means my legal cover is gone,” Ben said. “And I’m going to need your help.”

Talon held his gaze for a moment. Then he smiled — not the sharp, testing smile from before, but something smaller and more honest.

“Good,” he said. “Because I’ve been waiting four years for someone to ask the right question, and you just did.”

The alarm kept cycling. The storm kept pounding. And somewhere in the prison, in systems that had been dormant since the war, a protocol that wasn’t supposed to exist was moving prisoners toward an exit that wasn’t on any evacuation map.
