Wind hit like a bulkhead thrown open to vacuum.

Ben braced against it, one hand locked around the salt-crusted rail, the other holding his lightsaber low and steady. Its blue blade painted the spray in broken flashes and turned the wet durasteel underfoot into a moving ribbon of light and shadow. Forty meters below, surf slammed the sea wall in rolling blows that he felt up through his boots.

Talon was already ten meters ahead, shoulders hunched, body canted into the gusts. His voice came in shredded pieces between them.

“—absolute worst possible route in the entire facility, and I am including the waste channel. Put that on the record.”

“You said the waste channel was cut off.”

“It is. That doesn’t improve this. It just makes this the only bad option left instead of the second-worst one.” Talon’s hand slipped on the rail and he caught himself with a curse. “I have spent four years memorizing every broken thing in this prison, and the one thing I didn’t account for was a private military contractor who could read the sensor grid faster than I could read the exits.”

Ben pushed his awareness outward through the Force, feeling past the storm’s noise. The gantry ran two hundred meters ahead, a narrow strip of durasteel hanging over black water, its seaward side lined with transparisteel panels that rattled in their frames, some already cracked. The wind was shoving at everything: the panels, the rail, the support struts, the two men on the catwalk, and beneath all of it Ben could feel the structure giving in tiny increments, stress building where welds had already gone soft.

Behind them, somewhere in the prison they’d just left, Greyline teams were still moving. He couldn’t pick them cleanly through the storm’s interference — too much motion, too much static — but the pursuit hadn’t eased. They were still on the hunt.

“Keep moving,” he said.

“I am moving. I have been moving. I intend to keep moving until we are somewhere that is not here.” Talon’s complaints ran on in a steady rhythm, the same valve Ben had heard in the maintenance corridors. “The skiff should be at the end of this gantry. Maintenance access only. It’s not going to be comfortable. It’s not going to be fast. It’s going to be a floating chunk of rust held together by institutional neglect, and I am going to be extremely grateful to see it.”

A gust struck the gantry broadside. The rail shuddered. One of the transparisteel panels three meters ahead of Talon tore free and spun out into the dark, catching the storm light for a heartbeat before the surf swallowed it.

Talon stopped.

The gap left behind was a hole in the seaward barrier, wind screaming through it, spray lashing across the catwalk. The metal around the empty frame had bent outward, and the panel beside it flexed hard enough to make Ben wince just looking at it.

“That’s not good,” Talon said.

Ben was already reaching through the Force.

He felt the remaining panel as pressure and vibration — wind loading its surface, torque twisting at the upper bolts, the frame flexing around points that were about to fail. He pushed into the metal, found where it wanted to tear, and held.

The panel stopped moving.

“Go,” Ben said. “Now.”

Talon went. He crossed the gap fast, one hand on the rail, never looking down. Spray soaked him through. His boots slipped, caught, slipped again, and then he was past it, turning back to look at Ben.

“You’re holding that.”

“Move.”

Ben followed, keeping the pressure steady. The panel trembled against him, fighting his grip, and he could hear the bolts grinding in their housings. Salt crusted his eyelashes. The cold had worked through his jacket and skin and into the muscle beneath. His lightsaber burned as a steady blue line in the gray, and he kept it low and away from his body so the wind wouldn’t catch the blade.

He crossed the gap in four steps. When he let go, the panel held for one second, then two, and then the remaining bolts sheared and it went the way of the first one, spinning out into the storm.

“That was the easy one,” Talon said. “There’s a junction about sixty meters ahead where the gantry turns around the sea wall. The wind comes from three directions at once there. The panels on that section have been loose since before I got to Tarn Velos.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I can hear them from my cell when the storm’s bad enough. That sound travels through the whole facility. Everyone in C-block knows which panels are loose on the sea wall.” Talon was moving again, faster now. “It’s a prison. We don’t have entertainment. We have sounds. We have patterns. We have counting how long the camera takes to sweep back and whether the guard on third shift stops to check his comlink at the same junction every night. Four years of that, and you learn things.”

Ben followed. The gantry narrowed as they neared the turn, the rail lower, the drop suddenly closer. Below them the surf was white and violent, hurling spray high enough to mix with the rain. Lightning flickered somewhere out over the water, and the thunder came three seconds later, a low roll that shivered through the metal.

He reached outward with the Force again. The Greyline teams were closer now — he could feel them as a cluster of intent moving through the prison toward the sea wall access. They weren’t on the gantry yet, but they would be soon.

“How many?” Talon asked.

“Six. Maybe eight. They’re not on the gantry yet.”

“But they’re coming.”

“Yes.”

Talon didn’t answer. His complaints had stopped — not the way they had in the junction when he’d realized they were being herded, but differently. Quieter. The rhythm was still there in his breathing, in the way he checked each step before committing his weight, but the words had run out.

They reached the turn.

The wind hit from three directions at once, just as Talon had said. It came off the sea, off the cliff face, and down through a gap in the storm wall’s upper structure, and it met at the corner of the gantry in a churning shove that made the rail sing. The transparisteel panels here were rattling so hard Ben could see the vibration as a blur. Two were already cracked. A third was missing entirely.

And across the gap, fifty meters out, the maintenance skiff was visible for the first time — a low dark shape moored to a small platform at the gantry’s end, pitching in the swells.

“There,” Talon said. “That’s our ride.”

A grappling hook punched through the spray and caught the rail three meters behind Ben.

He didn’t think. The Force was already moving through him, and his lightsaber was already sweeping up to meet the cable. The blade cut through it in one motion, and the severed end whiplashed back into the storm. The hook clattered onto the gantry and slid toward the edge, and Ben kicked it over before it could snag.

“Go,” he said again.

Talon ran.

Ben followed, half his attention on the gantry ahead, half stretched backward through the storm. The Greyline team was on the catwalk now — he could feel them as distinct presences, their intent sharp and professional. Not panicked. Not angry. Just moving with the efficiency of people who had done this before.

Another hook came out of the dark, this one aimed higher, trying for the upper support strut. Ben caught it with the Force and shoved it sideways. It missed the strut by half a meter and dropped into the surf.

The third hook caught the rail.

Ben cut the cable, but the team was closer now, close enough that he could hear boots hammering on the gantry, hear a voice bark an order that the wind tore apart before he could catch the words. They spread out, using the gantry’s width to present multiple targets.

He reached for the Force again, not for the panels this time but for the gantry itself — the vibration running through the metal, the rhythm of boots on durasteel. He found the lead pursuer’s next step and pushed, a small precise nudge against the man’s ankle, and the boot slipped on wet metal. The operative went down hard, and the one behind him stumbled.

It bought them five seconds. Maybe less.

The turn was behind them now. The skiff was thirty meters ahead, then twenty. The platform was a square of durasteel bolted to the sea wall, barely large enough for two people to stand on, and the skiff itself was exactly what Talon had promised — a battered maintenance vessel, maybe six meters long, its hull streaked with salt and rust, its canopy cracked in one corner. It looked like it had been parked in the storm for years.

It looked perfect.

“Can you fly it?” Ben asked.

“I can fly anything with an engine. That’s not the problem.” Talon was already at the platform, already working the mooring clamps. “The problem is getting the engine started before the storm smashes us into the sea wall. The problem is whether the fuel cells have enough charge. The problem is whether the nav computer remembers which way is up.”

“Can you fix those problems?”

“I’m about to find out.”

Ben turned to face the gantry. The Greyline team was closing — four of them now, the others still picking themselves up from the slip he’d caused. The lead operative had a blaster carbine raised, and Ben could see the hesitation in the man’s posture as he registered the lightsaber.

“Put it down,” the operative shouted over the wind. “You’re not walking away from this.”

Ben didn’t answer. He reached for the Force and found the transparisteel panels on either side of the gantry, the ones already cracked, already rattling in their frames. He pushed.

The panels shattered outward. Not toward the operatives — he wasn’t trying to hurt them — but into the gap between the gantry and the sea, a sudden burst of transparisteel fragments that caught the storm light and the spray and turned the air into glittering confusion. The lead operative flinched. The one behind him ducked.

Ben turned and ran for the skiff.

Talon had the canopy open and was already in the pilot’s seat, hands moving across the controls. The instrument panel flickered — once, twice — and then steadied. An engine coughed somewhere beneath them.

“Fuel cells are at forty percent. That’s enough to get us clear of the prison’s proximity, but we’re not going to make orbit on this thing.”

“We don’t need orbit. We need distance.”

“Distance I can do.” Talon’s hands kept moving. “Assuming the engine doesn’t flood. Assuming the stabilizers hold. Assuming the Greyline team doesn’t have a gunship waiting on the other side of the storm wall—”

“Talon.”

“—which they probably do, because that’s the kind of day we’re having—”

“Talon.”

“What?”

“Fly the skiff.”

Talon flew the skiff.

The engine caught with a grinding cough and a plume of gray vapor that the wind tore away instantly. The repulsors whined, and the skiff lifted off the platform, yawing hard to port before Talon brought it under control. Ben dropped into the seat beside him and pulled the canopy closed, and the storm’s roar fell to a muffled thunder.

Through the transparisteel, he watched the gantry fall away. The Greyline team had reached the platform. One of them raised a blaster, and Ben felt the bolt before it arrived — a hot spike in the Force — and nudged the skiff’s trajectory just enough that the shot went wide. The second bolt missed by more. Then they were out of range, the prison shrinking behind them, the sea wall becoming a dark line against darker water.

Tarn Velos fell into the storm.

The silence in the skiff was not quiet. The wind still hammered at the hull. The engine still whined at a pitch that suggested it was doing its best and its best was only barely enough. Rain drummed on the canopy. But the space between the two of them was the silence of a crisis ending, of a field widening back out, of everything Ben had set aside during the crossing returning at once.

He sat with it.

His hands were steady. That was the first thing he noticed. His breathing was even. His heart rate was coming down, the way it always did after, the way Jacen had taught him to bring it down — count the beats, slow the exhale, let his body tell his mind that the danger was past.

Jacen had taught him that when he was fourteen. It still worked.

He sat with that too.

The skiff cleared the sea wall’s wind shadow and the storm caught it broadside, shoving hard. Talon corrected, cursing under his breath, and the stabilizers groaned.

“We’re going to need to set down somewhere,” Talon said. “This thing isn’t rated for sustained flight in these conditions. The fuel cells are dropping faster than I’d like, and the port stabilizer is doing something I don’t have a word for.”

“How long do we have?”

“Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty if the storm eases.” Talon glanced at him. “It’s not going to ease.”

“Find us somewhere to land.”

“I’m working on it. There’s a coastal shelf about fifteen klicks south of here — old survey station, probably abandoned. Should have enough cover to keep the skiff from getting blown into the sea while we figure out what comes next.”

Ben nodded.

He was doing the accounting. He couldn’t stop himself from doing it. It was the same inventory he’d learned to take after every operation during the war, the same quiet itemization: what he’d used, what he’d lost, what he’d revealed, what it would cost.

He’d broken cover. That was the first item. The GA Justice records auditor identity was burned — Greyline had seen his lightsaber, seen his face, seen him use the Force. They would have footage. They would have reports. By morning, everyone who mattered would know that Ben Skywalker had walked into Tarn Velos under false pretenses and walked out with a convicted prisoner.

He’d freed a convicted prisoner. That was the second item. Talon Veyr was legally detained, legally sentenced, and now legally absent from the facility that was supposed to hold him. The paperwork on that alone would take weeks to sort out, assuming anyone was inclined to sort it out instead of issuing a warrant.

He’d generated a public crisis. That was the third item. A prison was in lockdown. A private military contractor had engaged a Jedi Knight in a running fight through a storm-exposed gantry. The GA Justice Bureau would have questions. The Jedi Order would have questions. His father would have questions.

Dorrin would have questions. That was the fourth item, and the one Ben didn’t have a clean answer for. Dorrin had authorized the allied-party access. Dorrin had trusted him to operate within the law. And Ben had just blown through every one of those bounds in the space of an hour.

The legal mess was unavoidable. That was the fifth item, and it wasn’t really an item so much as a summary of the first four. He had crossed a threshold he couldn’t cross back over. The life he’d had at Shedu Maad that morning — the training salle, the refectory, Amelia’s warm pointed banter, the careful silences around his name — was definitively over.

He didn’t regret it. That was the thing that surprised him. He sat with the accounting, with the weight of what he’d chosen, and he couldn’t find regret. He could find cost. He could find consequence. He could find the cold specific knowledge that this was going to get worse before it got better, and that some of the people he cared about were going to be caught in the blast radius.

But not regret. The decision had been real. The cost was real. The difference between knowing what something cost and having paid it was the difference between theory and fact, and Ben had just crossed that line too.

“How bad is it?” Talon asked.

Ben looked at him.

“The accounting. You’ve been doing it since we cleared the sea wall. I can see it on your face.” Talon’s voice was quieter now, the complaint rhythm gone. “How bad is the legal situation we just walked into?”

“Bad.”

“Define bad.”

“I broke cover. I freed a convicted prisoner. I used Force abilities against a security contractor team. The GA Justice Bureau is going to have questions. The Jedi Order is going to have questions. The investigating officer who authorized my access is going to have questions.” Ben paused. “And Greyline is going to have footage.”

“Footage of you cutting grappling hooks off the gantry.”

“Yes.”

“And shattering transparisteel panels.”

“Yes.”

“And generally making it very clear that a Jedi with GAG history is involved in this.”

“Yes.”

Talon was quiet for a moment. Then: “That’s pretty bad.”

“I know.”

“I want to be clear that I didn’t ask you to do any of that.”

“I know.”

“You did it anyway.”

“Yes.”

Another pause. The skiff shuddered through a patch of turbulence, and Talon corrected, his hands steady on the controls. When he spoke again, his voice had shifted — not back to the complaint rhythm, not quite a joke, but something else.

“I’ve been waiting four years for someone to ask the right question,” he said. “I didn’t expect the answer to come with a lightsaber and a storm-line escape and a legal situation that’s going to follow us for the rest of our lives.”

“It’s going to follow me,” Ben said. “You’re already a fugitive. I’m the one who just became one.”

“That’s not better.”

“No. It’s just true.”

Talon looked at him for a long moment. The skiff’s instrument panel flickered, the stabilizers groaned, and the storm kept running around them. Then Talon turned back to the controls and did something to the thrust that smoothed the ride a little.

“The coastal shelf is about ten minutes out,” he said. “There should be a comm station at the survey post. Old, probably decommissioned, but if the power cells are still good, we can get a signal out.”

“Who are you planning to call?”

“Nobody yet. But we’re going to need to call someone eventually. Your father, probably. The investigating officer. Whoever’s going to be least angry about the part where we broke a convicted prisoner out of a coastal detention facility.”

“Dorrin,” Ben said. “His name is Cale Dorrin. He’s the GA Justice investigator who authorized my access.”

“Is he going to be angry?”

“He’s going to be thorough. That’s worse.”

Talon almost smiled. Not quite. The humor hadn’t come back yet, the valve was still shut, but there was a flicker of recognition that acknowledged the shape of the mess without pretending it was funny.

“I’ve spent four years in a prison full of people who were angry,” he said. “Thorough is a nice change of pace.”

The skiff flew on through the storm. Tarn Velos was gone behind them — the prison, the gantry, the Greyline team, the life Ben had woken up to that morning. Ahead was the coastal shelf and the survey station and whatever waited after that.

Ben didn’t know what came after. That was the last item in the accounting, the one he couldn’t fill in yet. The door had closed. The next one hadn’t opened. The space between them was blank and cold and honest, and he sat in it without trying to fill it.

Talon was watching him again. Not staring — his eyes were on the instruments, on the storm, on the dark line of coast ahead — but his attention was on Ben in a way it hadn’t been before. Not grateful. Not simple. Just present.

“You’re taking this well,” Talon said.

“Taking what well?”

“The part where you just threw away your legal cover and your institutional protection and probably your ability to go home anytime soon, and you’re sitting there doing an inventory like it’s a post-flight checklist.”

Ben didn’t answer right away. The skiff banked slightly, following the coastline, and through the canopy he could see the survey station now — a low structure built into the cliff face, dark except for a single amber light blinking on its landing pad.

“It is a checklist,” he said at last. “That’s how I do this.”

“Do what?”

“Know what I just chose.”

Talon was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded once and turned back to the controls.

The skiff descended toward the landing pad. The storm was still running, the wind still hammering, the surf still pounding the cliffs below. But the immediate crisis was over.

The rest of it was just beginning.
