The Bureau of Post-Conflict Logistics sat on a mid-level Coruscant block that had been rebuilt twice since the war and still looked like it was waiting for the next one. Ben walked the third-floor corridor with an orange ALLIED PARTY badge clipped to his belt, the plastic still warm from where he’d been turning it over in his hand on the turbolift ride up. The badge meant welcome and not welcome at once: official enough to get him through the door, provisional enough that it could shut behind him the moment someone decided he’d seen too much.

Inspector Cale Dorrin met him at the security cordon. Mid-forties, human, a face that had spent long enough in this work to stop showing surprise. His badge hung from a lanyard around his neck, and his eyes did the same thing Ben had seen in cops and investigators across half a dozen systems: took him in once, filed the result, moved on.

“Skywalker,” Dorrin said. Not a question.

“Inspector.”

“You understand the limits.”

“I do.”

“Good.” Dorrin turned before Ben could add anything. “Then let’s not waste time.”

The corridor stretched ahead of them, lined with closed doors and the low murmur of climate control. Through a window on his left, Ben caught the mid-morning light striking the upper spires of the Jedi Temple. The building was still there. The Order was not. The Senate had seen to that, and the Temple now sat on the skyline like a word everyone had agreed not to say: visible, impossible to miss, and nobody’s business anymore.

Ben registered it the way you register a word you’ve decided not to say. He did not look at it again.

The records office was the third door on the right. A GA Justice seal had been slapped onto the frame, and as they approached, the air changed: recycled atmosphere cut with the chemical bite of forensic processing, with something beneath it that Ben’s nose tagged before his mind caught up. Old blood. Not much. Enough.

Dorrin stopped at the door. “She was found at 0300 by the night-shift supervisor. No sign of forced entry. No defensive wounds on the body. The medical examiner’s preliminary finding is sudden cardiac failure, but the investigation is still open.”

“But you don’t think it was her heart.”

Dorrin’s expression didn’t move. “I think Commander Rane was forty-three, in good health, and died at her desk three hours after sending a message to a Jedi Knight she had no professional reason to contact. I’m keeping the file open until I understand why.”

He pushed the door open and stepped aside.

Ben walked into the room where Elya Rane had died.

The overhead fluorescents were harsh in a room built for late shifts and quiet hours. Six workstations lined the walls, five of them dark. The sixth was lit, its chair still angled toward the terminal, and on the edge of the housing there was a dark smear, dried hours ago.

The body was gone. Everything else remained.

Ben counted exits before he could stop himself. One door behind him. A ventilation shaft in the ceiling, too narrow for a person. No windows. The habit fired in the space between one breath and the next, an old GAG threat-assessment reflex that had never quite gone away, and he let it run because stopping it took more effort than letting it finish.

Then he reached for the Force, and the room went sideways.

Not empty. Not silent. Just wrong. The way a room feels when someone has only just stopped shouting — the pressure still in the walls, the shape of the noise still hanging in the air. Elya Rane was gone, but the space where she had been was not quiet. It was absence with edges.

Ben stood very still.

“What is it?” Dorrin asked.

“Give me a moment.”

The Force residue in the room was precise. That was the first thing he understood. Not the ragged tear of a fight, not the blunt crash of violence done in anger or fear. Something controlled had happened here. Something systematic. Someone had reached into Elya Rane’s mind with pressure that left a clean wound behind, intimate and patient and wrong in a way Ben recognized without wanting to.

He knew the shape of it. He had felt something close to it before, years ago, standing in rooms where Jacen had finished interrogations and the air still remembered what had been taken.

The recognition came before the name. His body went still in the way it did when a threat was nearby and he hadn’t found it yet. A tightness gathered in his chest, a weight behind his eyes, because he knew this shape from the inside. Not the act. The method. The way Jacen had taught him to read a room, to find the pressure points in a person’s attention, to apply leverage where it would hold.

Someone else had learned the same lessons. Someone else had used them here.

Ben pulled back from the Force and let the room settle around him. The fluorescents hummed. The chemical smell sat at the back of his throat. Dorrin was watching him with the patience of a man who had worked with Jedi before and knew better than to interrupt.

“She didn’t die of cardiac failure,” Ben said.

“I know.”

“The person who killed her was looking for something.”

“I know that too.” Dorrin folded his arms. “The question is whether they found it.”

Ben looked at the terminal. The chair angled toward it, the smear on the housing edge, the way Elya had been sitting when whatever happened had started. A records officer, working late, finding something she shouldn’t have. She’d sent the message to Ben’s old GAG marker because she’d known what she was looking at and who could help. And then she’d hidden the evidence.

Not in the terminal. The killer would have checked the terminal. Not in a drawer or a safe or any of the obvious places.

Ben thought about the message. Four lines, sent through a dead channel, asking him to come alone. She’d been afraid. She’d been careful. She’d been a records officer with twenty years of institutional instinct and three hours between discovery and death.

“Where are the decommissioned workstations?” he asked.

Dorrin’s eyes narrowed. “Why?”

“Because she knew someone was coming. She hid the fragment somewhere a search wouldn’t find it, and she didn’t have time to be clever. She had time to be fast.”

The inspector studied him for a long moment. Then he nodded toward the far corner of the room, where a dark terminal sat against the wall, its housing dusty and its power coupling cold.

“That one. Decommissioned six months ago. Forensics checked it — nothing on the drive, nothing in the housing.”

“They checked the obvious places.”

“It’s a workstation. There aren’t many other places.”

Ben crossed to the dark terminal. The power-coupling housing was a rectangular panel set into the side of the unit, meant to be removed for maintenance and never touched otherwise. He crouched beside it and ran his fingers along the seam. The panel was slightly warped — heat degradation, the kind that happened when old electronics ran too long without ventilation — and the gap behind it was just wide enough for a data chip.

He didn’t use the Force, and he didn’t need to; he worked his fingernails into the seam and pried the panel loose, and the chip was there, wedged into the gap behind the housing exactly where Elya Rane had put it twelve hours ago.

Ben held it up.

Dorrin crossed the room in three strides. “That’s not possible. Forensics pulled that panel.”

“They pulled it. They didn’t look behind it.”

“The gap isn’t—” Dorrin stopped, looked at the warped housing, the heat-degraded edge, the space that shouldn’t have been wide enough for a data chip but was. “She knew the equipment.”

“She knew the building.” Ben stood, the chip small and warm in his palm. “She’d worked here for years. She knew which workstation ran hot, which panel warped, which gap would hide something a search team wouldn’t find because they weren’t looking for a hiding place that required knowing the hardware.”

He held the chip out. Dorrin took it, turned it over once, and crossed to the active terminal. “Let’s see what she died for.”

The chip was damaged. The read took longer than it should have, the terminal’s processor grinding through corrupted sectors while Ben stood behind Dorrin’s shoulder and watched the data resolve in pieces. A manifest header. A shipment ID. A destination that didn’t match the public record.

“QS-7741-MAAD,” Dorrin read. “Hapes Cluster forward-deployment cache, dated four days before Shedu Maad. Standard wartime logistics.”

“That’s the public version.”

“I know.” Dorrin scrolled farther. The screen flickered, and a second layer of data surfaced — same shipment ID, same date, different routing. A quarantine-survey classification that didn’t belong on a military manifest. A destination Ben had never heard of.

“Iresh,” Dorrin said. “That’s not a military destination. That’s—” He paused, pulling up a secondary reference. “Survey outpost. Decommissioned before the war. There’s nothing there.”

“There’s something there now.”

Dorrin kept scrolling. The encryption marker appeared at the bottom of the manifest — a personal seal, not an institutional one, the kind that said I authorized this myself and I don’t want anyone else reading it.

Ben recognized the marker before his mind caught up with his stomach.

“That’s Jacen’s encryption,” he said.

Dorrin looked up. “You’re sure.”

“I’m sure.”

The inspector didn’t ask how Ben knew. He filed it — Ben could see him filing it, the same way he’d filed the orange badge and the Jedi name and the fact that a dead records officer had contacted a Jedi Knight through a channel that shouldn’t exist. Dorrin was building a picture. He wasn’t asking for the pieces yet.

“This is evidence of concealment,” Dorrin said. “A duplicated manifest, a hidden destination, a personal encryption marker on a wartime shipment. It proves one of Jacen Solo’s last orders had two lives. It doesn’t prove murder.”

“But it’s why she died.”

“Yes.” Dorrin leaned back from the terminal. “And it’s not enough to tell us who killed her. We need someone who can read what this audit trail means.”

Ben was already thinking the same thing. The fragment was a key, but the lock was somewhere else — in the logistics chain, the authorization patterns, the institutional memory of how GAG had moved material and covered its tracks. He didn’t know the system well enough to trace it. He’d been fourteen. He’d been Jacen’s student, not his logistics officer.

“I can run a records check,” Dorrin said. “Cross-reference the authorization patterns against known GAG personnel with manifest-access privileges. It won’t be fast.”

“How long?”

“An hour. Maybe less if the system cooperates.” Dorrin’s mouth twitched — not quite a smile. “It won’t cooperate.”

Ben nodded. “I’ll wait.”

The hour passed in the corridor outside the records office, where the fluorescents hummed and the Jedi Temple sat on the skyline and Ben did not look at it. He leaned against the wall and watched the door and thought about the shape of the Force residue in the room behind him. Precise. Intimate. Controlled. Someone had learned what Jacen had taught. Someone had used it to kill a woman whose only crime was paying attention.

He didn’t name the familiarity. He didn’t have a name yet. But it sat in his chest, a stone he’d swallowed years ago and forgotten until now.

The door opened. Dorrin stood in the frame with a datapad in his hand and an expression that meant the records check had found something.

“Talon Veyr,” he said. “Former GAG field agent, reassigned to logistics and records after an operational incident. Worked under Commander Rane for eighteen months before his arrest. Convicted four years ago on charges involving manifest-authorization patterns that match the structure of your fragment.”

Ben straightened. “Where is he?”

“Tarn Velos Coastal Detention Works. Maximum security, ocean-side. He’s been there since his conviction.” Dorrin held out the datapad. “His case file is sealed, but the cross-reference pulled his personnel record. He knew the system. He knew the authorization chains. And he knew Elya Rane.”

Ben took the datapad. The face on the screen was younger than he’d expected — late twenties in the booking image, dark hair, the kind of expression that suggested the camera had interrupted something he’d rather have been doing. The file listed him as a non-violent offender, his sentence as open-ended, and his institutional record as unremarkable except for a note about disciplinary incidents involving unauthorized use of facility communications equipment.

“He’s been trying to contact someone,” Ben said.

“He’s been trying to contact everyone. Appeals, records requests, messages to former colleagues. Most of them bounced. The ones that didn’t were ignored.” Dorrin paused. “If you want to talk to him, you’ll need to file a formal consultant request through GA Justice. It’ll take a day to process, minimum. And it’ll flag on systems that haven’t been touched since the war.”

“Flag how?”

“The kind of request that wakes old GAG-adjacent alert protocols. Someone will notice. Someone always does.”

Ben looked at the datapad. At the face. At the name that meant nothing to him yet — Talon Veyr, former GAG, convicted of something the file didn’t explain, sitting in a prison on Tarn Velos with the knowledge Ben needed to read what Elya had died to preserve.

The legal route was the only path that didn’t blow the investigation open. It was also the path that announced to anyone still watching old GAG channels that someone was asking questions about Jacen Solo’s last orders.

He didn’t know what that would cost. He did the responsible thing anyway.

“File the request,” he said.

Dorrin nodded. “I’ll start the paperwork. You should know — once this goes into the system, you’re not quiet anymore. Someone will see it. Someone will ask why a Jedi Knight is visiting a GAG convict on Tarn Velos.”

“I know.”

“Do you have an answer?”

Ben thought about the Force residue in the room behind him. The shape of it. The way it had felt familiar in a way he didn’t want to examine too closely.

“I will,” he said.

Dorrin held his gaze for a moment longer. Then he turned back toward the office, datapad in hand, already pulling up the request forms on his terminal.

Ben stayed in the corridor. The datapad was still in his hand, Talon Veyr’s face still on the screen, and the Jedi Temple was still on the skyline through the window he wasn’t looking at. He had what Elya saved. He had a name, a location, and a reason. He had a case that was moving.

And underneath the forward momentum, quiet and certain, was the knowledge that he was going to have to explain to Dorrin why he knew GAG authorization chains faster than he should. Not today. Soon.

Dorrin’s first records check had pointed toward an old GAG personnel and prosecution trail Ben could not interpret alone.
