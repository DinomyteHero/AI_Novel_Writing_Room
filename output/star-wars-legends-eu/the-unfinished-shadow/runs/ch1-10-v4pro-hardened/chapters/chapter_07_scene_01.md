The alarm shifted pitch.

Ben heard it before his brain fully caught up: a drop in frequency, the tone sliding from the steady pulse of a standard security alert into something lower, faster, off-kilter. The storm surge was still hammering the sea wall, the ventilation still humming with its slightly wrong note, but the alarm had changed, and that change meant trouble.

Through the transparisteel partition, Talon went still. Not the way he had when Ben mentioned Elya; that had been a reaction. This was cleaner than that. His shoulders squared, his hands came off the table, and his eyes went to the door with the flat, operational focus of a man who had stopped being a prisoner and started being a survivor.

“That’s wrong,” Talon said.

“The alarm?”

“The pattern.” Talon listened, head canted slightly, the way Ben had seen veteran pilots listen to an engine about to fail. “That isn’t lockdown. That isn’t drill. That’s—”

The pitch shifted again. A third tone slid under the first two, higher, sharper, and Ben felt the hair on his arms lift. Not the Force. Just the body’s animal answer to a sound that did not belong.

“That’s an evacuation override,” Talon said. “Someone’s forcing storm protocol through the security system. The sea wall alarms.”

Ben was already on his feet. “Storm protocol is for weather.”

“It’s for anything that needs prisoners moved inland. Flooding. Structural failure. Attack.” Talon rose too, chair legs scraping durasteel, his body already angled toward the door on his side of the partition. “Someone’s pulling us out of the visitor wing. That isn’t procedure. Procedure is lockdown until the threat assessment clears. You don’t evacuate prisoners during a security alert unless—”

“Unless the alert is the cover.”

Talon looked at him. For one second, the humor vanished completely—no deflection, no testing grin, just a man who had survived four years in a maximum-security prison by reading every threat before it hit and was now reading one he couldn’t see.

“Skywalker,” he said. “Why are you really here?”

“I told you.”

“No. You told me about Elya and the fragment and the investigation. You didn’t tell me why someone would trigger an evacuation override in a coastal prison during a storm the same day you show up asking questions about Tess’s old operations.” Talon’s voice stayed fast, but not joking. It had the clipped pace of someone running calculations out loud. “You filed a visitor request through GA Justice. That went into the system. Someone saw it. Someone who’s been watching old GAG case trails, someone who knew what Elya found and didn’t want anyone else finding it. And now there’s an alarm going off that isn’t in the standard response matrix and we’re about to be moved somewhere that isn’t the visitor wing.”

Ben heard the guard station wake up—boots on durasteel, a voice on a comlink, the clatter of equipment being yanked from lockers. Whatever was happening, it was moving fast.

“How bad?” he asked.

“I don’t know yet.” Talon moved toward the door on his side of the partition, then stopped, one hand on the frame. “But they aren’t evacuating the whole prison. The pattern is local. East wing, visitor block, processing.” He turned back to Ben. “They’re evacuating us.”

The door on Ben’s side opened. The intake officer from processing stood there—face professionally blank and shoulders tight, a comlink in one hand and a security baton in the other.

“Sir, we need to move you to the inland shelter. There’s a storm-surge warning—”

“That isn’t a storm-surge warning,” Talon said through the partition. “Check the alarm pattern. That’s an override, not weather. Someone’s routing the evacuation protocol through the storm system.”

The officer looked at him, then at Ben, then at the comlink in her hand. “I have orders—”

“Your orders are wrong.” Talon’s voice was flat, certain, the voice of someone who had memorized every alarm pattern in the facility and knew exactly what each one meant. “The storm protocol triggers a two-tone alert with a thirty-second interval. This is three-tone with a fifteen-second interval. That’s an evacuation override. Someone’s using the storm system to move prisoners without authorization.”

The officer hesitated. Ben could see her running the calculation—the prisoner who knew the alarms better than she did, the visitor who wasn’t a records auditor, the orders that had come through too fast for standard procedure.

“How do you know that?” she asked.

“Because I’ve been here four years,” Talon said. “And I’ve heard every alarm this place has. That one’s wrong.”

The alarm shifted again, a fourth tone higher and more urgent. Down the corridor, someone shouted. Boots pounded on durasteel, moving away from the visitor wing.

“They’re clearing the guard station,” Ben said. “Who gave the evacuation order?”

“I don’t—” The officer glanced down at her comlink. “It came through the duty office. Priority override. I didn’t—”

“Who’s in the duty office?”

“I don’t know. The shift commander was supposed to—” She stopped. Her face changed. “The shift commander went off-duty an hour ago. The relief hasn’t checked in yet.”

Talon met Ben’s eyes through the transparisteel. The joke was gone. The restlessness was gone. What was left was the field agent who had survived Tess’s operations and Elya’s records office and four years in a maximum-security prison by knowing when the system was being turned against him.

“They aren’t evacuating us,” Talon said. “They’re isolating us. Clear the wing, move the guards out, leave the visitor room sealed. Whoever triggered that alarm wants us cut off from the rest of the prison.”

“Or wants us somewhere specific.”

“Same thing.” Talon looked at the officer. “You need to get to the duty office and verify the override. Right now. If the shift commander didn’t authorize it, somebody’s running a false protocol through the storm system.”

The officer stared at him for a long second. Then she turned and ran.

Ben watched her go. The corridor was emptying: guards pulling back toward the main hub, the visitor processing desk abandoned, the security camera in the corner still sweeping its usual pattern as if nothing had changed. The storm surge hammered the sea wall. The alarm kept cycling through its bad pitch.

“Skywalker.” Talon’s voice had gone quiet, the flat register of a man who had stopped calculating and started deciding. “You came here to ask me about a manifest. You’re about to get more than that. Whatever’s happening, it’s happening because you’re here. Someone saw your visitor request and moved faster than the system could protect you.”

“I know.”

“Do you know what that means?”

Ben looked at him through the transparisteel—the man who had been Tess’s protégé, Elya’s subordinate, a convict on a charge that didn’t quite explain the full shape of what he’d done. A man who had just read an alarm pattern in four seconds and identified a false evacuation protocol before the guards could finish their orders.

“It means my legal cover is gone,” Ben said. “And I’m going to need your help.”

Talon held his gaze for a moment. Then he smiled—not the sharp, testing smile from before, but something smaller and more honest.

“Good,” he said. “Because I’ve been waiting four years for someone to ask the right question, and you just did.”

Ben heard it then—the sound beneath the alarm. Footsteps, too many and too synchronized for prison staff. The specific plastoid creak of body armor that didn’t match any correctional-spec gear he’d seen in the processing station. The smell reached him a half-second later: salt water, machine oil, and the faint chemical tang of new armor plating.

“Talon,” he said. “Get away from the door.”

Talon was already moving, backing toward the far wall of his side of the visitor room, his hands coming up in the loose, ready posture of someone who had been in fights before and knew exactly how little good bare hands would do against body armor. “How many?”

“Five. Maybe six.” Ben let the Force open his perception—not reaching, just receiving. The corridor outside the visitor wing became a map of pressure and intent. Six bodies, moving in a loose breach formation. Two holding at the junction. Four coming toward the visitor room door. “They’re not prison staff.”

“I gathered that.”

“Body armor doesn’t match the facility spec. Weapons are—” He paused, reading the shapes through the wall, the cold metal signatures of blaster rifles. “Military-grade. Suppressed. They’re not here for a riot.”

“They’re here for me,” Talon said. His voice stayed calm in a way that wasn’t calm at all. “Someone saw your visitor request and decided to pull me out before I could tell you anything useful. That’s a snatch, Skywalker. Dressed up as a prison transfer, probably with paperwork that’ll look legitimate for about six hours. By the time anyone verifies the authorization, I’ll be in a holding cell that doesn’t exist on any registry.”

Ben’s hand moved toward his belt before he consciously chose to reach. The lightsaber was there, concealed under his jacket, the familiar weight of it a cold reassurance against his hip. He didn’t draw it yet. Drawing it meant the end of his cover, the end of the investigation’s legal framework, the end of any pretense that he was here as a records auditor.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

“Ben,” Talon said. His voice had gone very quiet. “Whatever you’re going to do, do it now.”

The door blew inward.

Ben’s lightsaber was in his hand before the smoke cleared, the blue blade igniting with a snap-hiss that cut through the alarm’s wrong pitch. The first Greyline operative through the door was big, armored, moving with the trained economy of someone who had done this before. His blaster rifle was already tracking toward Talon’s side of the partition.

Ben moved.

The Force answered before thought—a shove that caught the first operative in the chest and sent him crashing back into the man behind him. Both went down in a tangle of armor and weapons. The third operative cleared the doorframe, rifle coming up, and Ben was already there, lightsaber sweeping in a tight arc that caught the blaster bolt and sent it into the ceiling. The second deflection hit the rifle’s power pack, the blade kissing the housing just long enough to short the energy cell. The weapon died with a flat crack.

“Jedi!” someone shouted—not fear, not surprise, the callout of a professional updating his team on a tactical change.

Ben didn’t answer. He was already moving again, the Force flowing through him in the clean, wordless way it always did when the fight started. The fourth operative came through the door, and Ben caught him with a Force-pull that yanked the rifle from his grip and sent it clattering down the corridor. The man lunged for a sidearm, and Ben put a telekinetic wall between them—not a hard shove, just a barrier, the man’s momentum stopping cold as if he’d run into durasteel.

“Stay down,” Ben said.

The man stayed down.

Behind him, Ben heard the partition door cycling—Talon, moving fast, using the chaos to get out of the kill box. Smart. The visitor room was a trap with only one exit on Talon’s side, and the Greyline team had been coming straight for it.

“Left side!” Talon shouted.

Ben spun. The fifth operative had come through a secondary door—the staff entrance, the one the intake officer had used—and was raising a blaster carbine toward Ben’s back. Ben’s lightsaber came around in a rising block that caught the bolt and held it, the energy sizzling against the blade, and then he was closing the distance, two steps, three, the Force giving him speed that the operative’s armor couldn’t match. A strike to the weapon—not the man, the weapon—and the carbine’s barrel glowed orange for a half-second before the focusing crystal cracked and the whole assembly died.

The operative dropped the ruined carbine and reached for a vibroblade. Ben kicked it out of his hand before the hilt cleared the sheath.

“I said stay down.”

The man’s eyes stayed hard behind his helmet visor—professional, calculating, already rethinking his approach. But he stayed down.

Ben straightened, breathing hard, the lightsaber humming in his grip. The corridor outside the visitor room was a scatter of disabled operatives—two unconscious from the initial shove, one clutching a broken wrist, two more disarmed and pinned against the walls by the lingering pressure of Ben’s Force barrier. The sixth man, the one who had been holding at the junction, was gone. Running or repositioning. Ben filed the absence and moved on.

The alarm was still cycling. The storm was still pounding. And in the red emergency light of the prison corridor, Ben Skywalker stood with his lightsaber drawn and his legal cover in ashes around him.

“Clear,” he said.

“Clear,” Talon echoed from behind him. He had come through the partition door and was standing with his back to the wall, a security baton in one hand—taken from the abandoned guard station, Ben guessed—and his eyes moving across the downed operatives with the flat, analytical expression of someone cataloguing threats and finding the explanations too thin. “Six-man breach team. Military armor, no insignia. Suppressed weapons. They weren’t here to kill me.”

“I noticed.”

“If they wanted me dead, they’d have put a bolt through the transparisteel and walked away. This was a pickup. Someone wants me alive and somewhere else.”

Ben deactivated his lightsaber but didn’t put it away. The blade’s absence left the corridor darker, the red emergency strips along the floor throwing long shadows against the walls. “Greyline.”

“Probably.” Talon nudged one of the unconscious operatives with his foot, rolling him onto his side. The armor was clean, professional, unmarked. “This isn’t prison-issue. This isn’t even local security. This is contractor gear, and it’s new. Someone spent money on this operation.”

“Someone who saw my visitor request.”

“Someone who saw your visitor request and had a snatch team prepositioned close enough to reach Tarn Velos before the storm cleared.” Talon looked up, and his expression was strange—not frightened, not angry, something closer to professional respect mixed with deep, cold fury. “You kicked over a nest, Skywalker. Someone’s been watching this prison for a long time, waiting for anyone to show up asking about Tess’s old operations. And you walked in with a GA Justice badge and a Jedi lightsaber and gave them exactly the excuse they needed.”

Ben heard boots in the distance—more of them, moving fast, the sound echoing through the prison’s stone corridors. The second wave. Or the first team’s backup.

“We need to move,” he said.

“Agreed.” Talon was already walking, not toward the main exit but toward the junction where the sixth operative had disappeared. “This way.”

“The exit’s the other direction.”

“The exit’s what they’re covering.” Talon didn’t slow down. “You think a professional snatch team leaves the main corridor unguarded? They’ll have a containment element at every exit between here and the landing pad. The logical escape route is the trap.”

Ben followed him, the lightsaber still in his hand, the Force still singing through his nerves in the aftermath of the fight. “You know another way.”

“I know this prison.” Talon turned left at the junction, moving fast through a secondary corridor lined with storage lockers and maintenance access panels. The emergency lighting was dimmer here, the red strips flickering. “Four years, Skywalker. You learn the spaces between the official maps. Maintenance routes, old storm tunnels, the service crawlspaces they sealed after the last structural audit. The kind of routes that aren’t in the evacuation plan because nobody wants to admit they exist.”

“And you think Greyline doesn’t know about them?”

“I think Greyline knows the official layout. I think they’ve got schematics, floor plans, the sort of intel you get from bribing a junior administrator or pulling the prison’s public safety filings.” Talon stopped at a maintenance door, running his fingers along the frame until he found the release catch. “They don’t know the things you learn by being here. By watching which doors the guards don’t check. By noticing which cameras have a two-second lag and which corridors the storm shutters don’t quite seal.”

The door opened onto a narrow stairwell, dark and smelling of salt and rust. Talon started down without hesitating.

Ben followed. The stairs were wet, the stone worn smooth by decades of sea air and maintenance neglect. The alarm was fainter here, muffled by the walls, but the wrong pitch still carried—a constant reminder that the prison’s systems were no longer under the prison’s control.

“Where does this go?” Ben asked.

“Sublevel two. Old storage. There’s a maintenance tunnel that runs under the east wing, connects to the storm drainage system. It’s not on any schematic filed after the first structural survey. The prison sealed it about thirty years ago, but the seals are garbage and the guards use it to move contraband.” Talon glanced back over his shoulder. “You’re taking this well.”

“Taking what well?”

“The part where I’m leading you into a sealed maintenance tunnel under a prison that’s currently being raided by private military contractors who want to kidnap me.” Talon’s voice had recovered some of its humor, but it was the dark, survival-born version—the jokes of someone who processed fear by talking through it. “Most people would be asking more questions.”

“I’m not most people.”

“No. You’re a Jedi who just disabled six armed operatives without killing any of them, and you did it in about forty seconds.” Talon reached the bottom of the stairs and pushed open another door, this one heavier, its hinges screaming. “That’s not nothing, Skywalker. That’s the sort of thing people notice.”

Ben didn’t answer. The sublevel was dark, the only light coming from the emergency strips along the ceiling—fewer of them here, spaced wider, some of them dead entirely. The air was colder, wetter, the salt smell stronger. Somewhere above them, the alarm was still cycling, and Ben could hear more boots on durasteel, more voices shouting orders.

“The tunnel,” he said. “Where’s the entrance?”

“East wall, behind the old filtration equipment. There’s a panel that looks like it’s part of the housing. It’s not.” Talon was already moving through the darkness, navigating by memory and the faint red glow of the emergency lights. “We get through the tunnel, we come out on the seaward side of the prison, below the storm wall. From there, we can—”

He stopped.

Ben felt it at the same moment—a shift in the Force, a pressure that wasn’t physical. Someone was coming. Not the Greyline team, not the prison guards. Something else. Something moving with the measured control of someone who knew they were being hunted.

“Lysa,” Talon said quietly.

Ben looked at him.

“The intake officer. The one who ran to check the duty office.” Talon’s voice had gone flat again, the humor draining out of it. “She didn’t come back.”

“No,” Ben said. “She didn’t.”

The presence was closer now, moving through the sublevel with the calm, unhurried pace of someone who knew exactly where she was going. Ben reached for the Force, letting it extend his perception, and what he felt was off in a way he couldn’t name immediately—not hostile, not aggressive, but controlled. Deliberate. The presence of someone who had been waiting.

The intake officer stepped out of the darkness between two pieces of filtration equipment. Her face was still professionally blank, but something had changed in her posture—the shoulders looser, the hands still, the comlink no longer in her grip. She looked at Ben, then at Talon, and her expression was the calm, satisfied look of someone whose job had just gotten much simpler.

“The duty office,” she said. “I checked.”

“And?” Talon’s voice was careful.

“There’s no shift commander on duty. There hasn’t been for two hours. The evacuation order came through an external channel with valid authorization codes.” She tilted her head slightly, the gesture almost curious. “Whoever triggered the alarm has access to the prison’s command protocols. That isn’t a bribe. That’s an infiltration.”

Ben’s grip tightened on his lightsaber. “Who are you?”

“I’m the person who’s been watching this prison for six months, waiting for someone to show up asking about Colonel Tess’s old operations.” She looked at Talon. “You’re Veyr. Elya Rane’s logistics officer. The one who got convicted for manifest fraud that didn’t quite add up.”

“That’s one way to put it.”

“It’s the way the file puts it. I’ve read the file.” She turned back to Ben. “And you’re Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight. Former GAG. You filed a visitor request through GA Justice about six hours ago, and about four hours ago, a Greyline snatch team started prepositioning assets around Tarn Velos. Someone in Greyline’s command structure has access to your movement data.”

Ben felt the case reorganize itself around this new information—not just a snatch, but one with inside access, someone who had been watching the prison, watching the GAG case trails, watching him. The Vigil’s reach was longer than he’d thought. Faster.

“You’re not prison staff,” he said.

“I’m a contractor. Private sector. My employer has an interest in making sure Greyline doesn’t walk out of here with Talon Veyr.” She smiled slightly—the first expression that looked genuine. “You can call me Lysa. And right now, I’m the only person in this building who knows where the Greyline containment teams are positioned.”

Talon looked at Ben. “She’s been waiting for us.”

“She’s been waiting for someone,” Ben said. “We’re the someone.”

“The Greyline team you disabled was the first wave,” Lysa said. “There’s a second team moving in from the landing pad, and a third securing the main exit. They’ve got the prison’s command protocols, which means they can lock down any door, seal any corridor, and route any security response away from their operational area. You can’t fight your way out of this. There are too many of them, and they know the building almost as well as you do.” She looked at Talon. “Almost.”

“What do you want?” Ben asked.

“I want to get Veyr out of here before Greyline puts him in a hole that doesn’t exist. My employer wants the same thing. We can argue about why later.” She pulled a datapad from her jacket, the screen casting pale blue light across her face. “I’ve got the Greyline team positions. I’ve got the prison’s maintenance schematics—the real ones, not the public filings. And I’ve got a route that gets us to the seaward storm wall without passing through any of their containment zones.”

“Why should we trust you?”

“You shouldn’t. But right now, I’m the only person offering you a way out that doesn’t involve fighting through three squads of private military contractors while the prison’s own security systems are working against you.” She met Ben’s eyes. “You’re good, Skywalker. I saw the feed from the visitor wing. You disabled six operatives in under a minute without killing any of them. That’s impressive. But it’s not going to be enough. Not against what’s coming.”

Ben looked at Talon.

Talon held his gaze for a long moment. Then he shrugged—the loose, resigned shrug of someone who had spent four years making bad choices in bad situations and was about to make another one.

“She’s right about the schematics,” he said. “The route I was going to take goes through a junction Greyline definitely has covered. I was hoping they’d missed it. They probably didn’t.”

“And the tunnel?”

“Gets us out. But it’s slow, and if they’ve got the storm drainage mapped, they’ll have someone waiting at the exit.” Talon looked at Lysa. “Your route. Where does it go?”

“Through the old laundry facility. There’s a service elevator that goes down to the sea wall maintenance level. It’s not on any schematic filed in the last twenty years. Greyline won’t know it exists.”

“And from there?”

“From there, we get outside the storm wall and we improvise.” Lysa’s smile widened slightly. “You’re good at improvising, Veyr. It’s in your file.”

Talon looked at Ben. “She’s got a file on me.”

“I noticed.”

“This is the part where you make a decision, Skywalker. You’re the Jedi. You’re the one who came here with a legal cover and a GA Justice badge and a plan that involved asking me questions and going home. That plan is dead. What’s the new one?”

Ben looked at the lightsaber in his hand—the blue blade dark now, the hilt cool against his palm. He thought about Dorrin, waiting for a report that would never come. He thought about the manifest fragment, the duplicated shipment, the destination Iresh. He thought about Elya Rane, dead in her records office, her mind opened drawer by drawer by someone trained to do exactly that.

And he thought about Talon, standing in the dark sublevel of a prison that had just been turned against him, still making jokes, still reading the situation faster than anyone else in the room.

“We get out,” Ben said. “We figure out what Greyline knows. We find out who’s running this operation and we stop them before they can bury whatever Elya found.”

“That’s not a plan. That’s a goal.”

“It’s the only goal that matters right now.” Ben looked at Lysa. “Show us the route.”

She nodded and turned, leading them deeper into the sublevel. The emergency lights flickered. The alarm kept cycling through its bad pitch. And somewhere above them, in corridors that had been cleared of guards and sealed by protocols that didn’t belong to the prison, a Greyline snatch team was moving through the visitor wing, cataloguing their disabled comrades and reporting back to a commander who was about to learn that the Jedi they’d been told to expect was not the records auditor his file claimed.

Ben followed Lysa into the dark, Talon at his side, the lightsaber still in his hand. His legal cover was gone. His investigation was burning behind him. And the only path forward was through a sealed maintenance tunnel with a woman he didn’t trust and a convict who had just become the most valuable asset in a conspiracy he was only beginning to understand.

The partnership’s terms were being set in the dark, in the salt-smelling cold, before either of them had named what this was. Ben didn’t name it now. He just walked, and let Talon walk beside him, and trusted that the man who had read the alarm before he did would read the next threat the same way.

Behind them, the prison kept screaming its wrong alarm into the storm.
