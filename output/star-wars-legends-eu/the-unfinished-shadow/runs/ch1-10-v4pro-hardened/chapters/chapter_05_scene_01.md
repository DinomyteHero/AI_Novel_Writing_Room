The cross-reference room smelled of ozone and old dust, the stuff that settled into climate-control filters and never quite left. Three of the four holoprojectors were dark. The fourth threw a grid of personnel records into the air between them, names and assignment codes stacked in pale blue columns that turned slowly as Dorrin worked the terminal.

Ben sat across from him, the fragment sealed in its evidence sleeve on the desk between them. The ALLIED PARTY badge clipped to his collar caught the projector light and threw a thin orange reflection on the wall. He’d stopped noticing it an hour ago. Mostly.

“Elya Rane’s supervision records,” Dorrin said. “Postwar assignment logs, personnel evaluations, transfer authorizations. Everything she touched in the eighteen months before the GAG decommissioned.”

He followed the flow without meaning to, his eyes jumping to the authorization chains before Dorrin had finished highlighting them. The structure was familiar in a way that sat wrong in his stomach.

“There.” Ben pointed at a name three lines from the bottom. “Talon Veyr. He’s flagged under her supervision chain.”

Dorrin’s stylus paused above the datapad. He didn’t look up. “You read that faster than I did.”

The silence after that was brief but specific, and Ben let it settle.

“Show me the supervision window,” he said.

Dorrin pulled the record open. Talon Veyr’s personnel file expanded into the display — a younger face in the booking image, dark hair, an expression that suggested the camera had interrupted something he’d rather have been doing. The file listed his assignment history in compressed blocks—field operations, eighteen months under Maro Tess, then a reassignment notation Ben had to read twice.

“Reassigned from field duty to records and logistics,” Dorrin said. “Operational incident. The file doesn’t specify, but the date matches a GAG action on Coruscant — civilian casualties, containment failure. Veyr was Tess’s protégé until then.”

“Until he wasn’t.”

“Until he wasn’t.” Dorrin scrolled farther. “After reassignment, he worked under Commander Rane for eighteen months. Same records environment, same authorization protocols. She supervised his transition from field work to logistics.”

Ben leaned closer. The display showed Talon’s assignment chain in the compressed shorthand of institutional record-keeping — dates, codes, supervisor notations that looked neutral on the surface and carried whole histories underneath. Elya Rane had signed off on his quarterly evaluations. She had approved his access to the manifest-authorization systems. She had written, in the final notation before his arrest, *“Veyr understands the transfer architecture better than anyone in this office. Recommend retention despite disciplinary concerns.”*

“She kept him on,” Ben said.

“She argued for him.” Dorrin’s voice stayed level. “The disciplinary concerns aren’t specified here. Let me cross-reference the prosecution files.”

The projector flickered. A second data stream overlaid the personnel records: postwar prosecution files, the sort that had flooded GA Justice in the years after Shedu Maad. Most of them were sealed. This one wasn’t, or not entirely; the cross-reference had pulled the public-facing conviction summary and the internal audit trail that had led to the charges.

Ben read it in pieces. Manifest-authorization fraud. Transfer-chain manipulation. A pattern of altered shipment records that had diverted material into channels the prosecution had never fully identified. The conviction had stuck, but the file’s language was careful — careful enough to mean the investigators had known they were only seeing part of the picture.

“The authorization patterns,” Ben said. “Show me the audit trace.”

Dorrin brought it up. The display resolved into a side-by-side comparison: the manifest-authorization markers from Talon’s conviction file on the left, the audit trace from Elya’s recovered fragment on the right. The structures matched. Not perfectly, different shipments, different dates, different routing codes, but the underlying logic was the same. The way the authorizations layered. The way the quarantine-survey classification had been used to disguise a military shipment. The way the transfer chain had been split to hide the destination.

“That’s not coincidence,” Dorrin said.

“No.”

“This is the same records environment. The same authorization protocols. The same method of concealment.” Dorrin leaned back from the terminal. “Veyr was convicted for doing what someone did to this manifest. Which means he knows how it was done.”

“And he knows how to read it.” Ben was already moving ahead. “He worked under Elya. He worked in the same systems. He’ll recognize the pattern — what was altered, what was hidden, what the original routing was supposed to look like before someone duplicated it.”

“He’s not a decoder.”

“He’s a witness. The value is his lived operational knowledge. He knows what this records environment looks like from the inside.”

Dorrin was quiet for a moment. Then he closed the comparison window and pulled up a new form on his terminal — the GA Justice visitor request protocol, the one that would take a day to process and flag on systems that hadn’t been touched since the war.

“You’re sure,” Dorrin said.

“I’m sure he’s the right person. Whether he’ll talk to me is a different question.”

“That’s not what I meant.” Dorrin turned to face him. The projector light caught the inspector’s face from the side, throwing half of it into shadow. “You’re sure you want to do this through channels. Once I file this request, it’s in the system. Anyone watching old GAG case trails will see it. Anyone with an interest in keeping Talon Veyr quiet will know someone’s asking questions.”

“I know.”

“There’s no un-filing it.”

“I know that too.”

Dorrin studied him. Ben could feel the inspector building the file — the way he’d tracked the authorization chains too fast, the way he’d recognized Jacen’s encryption marker, the way he’d known which workstation to check without being told. Dorrin was a professional. He wasn’t asking yet, but he was writing the questions down.

“You were in the GAG,” Dorrin said. It wasn’t a question.

“I was fourteen.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I’ve got.”

The silence stretched. Dorrin’s stylus tapped once against the datapad — a single click that might have been punctuation or might have been a decision. Then he turned back to the terminal and began filling out the request form.

“Tarn Velos Coastal Detention Works,” he said. “Maximum security, ocean-side. The visitor protocol requires seventy-two hours’ notice for non-legal personnel. I can flag it as investigative urgency, but that only buys you about a day. You’ll be on a shuttle tomorrow morning if the system cooperates.”

“If.”

“It won’t cooperate.” Dorrin’s mouth twitched — not quite a smile. “But I’ve been filing requests through this system for fifteen years. I know which forms to attach and which supervisor to copy. It’ll go through.”

Ben watched him work. The form was dense: institutional fields stacked one after another, each one needing a specific code or authorization reference. Dorrin moved through them with the practiced efficiency of someone who had spent his career inside the machinery. His stylus never hesitated. His fingers found the right keys without looking.

“Visitor name,” Dorrin said. “Ben Skywalker. Affiliation?”

“Jedi Order.”

“That’s not going to help. The Order has no institutional standing on Coruscant.”

“I know. Put ‘allied-party consultant.’ You already have the badge on file.”

Dorrin entered it. “Purpose of visit?”

“Investigative consultation. Material witness interview.”

“Relationship to inmate?”

“No prior relationship.”

Dorrin paused. “He’s going to ask why you’re there.”

“I’ll tell him the truth.”

“Which part?”

Ben looked at the display. Talon Veyr’s face was still there, frozen in the booking image, the expression caught somewhere between irritation and something harder to name. A man who’d been Tess’s protégé, then Elya’s subordinate, then a convict on a charge that didn’t quite explain the full shape of what he’d done. A man who knew the transfer architecture better than anyone in Elya’s office. A man who’d been trying to contact someone for four years and had gotten nothing back but silence.

“The part where Elya Rane is dead,” Ben said. “The part where she hid a manifest fragment with Jacen Solo’s encryption on it. The part where someone killed her for finding it, and I need to know what she found.”

“That’s a lot of truth.”

“It’s what I’ve got.”

Dorrin finished the form. The terminal chimed once, a soft, procedural sound, and the request vanished into the system. Somewhere in the GA Justice network, a routing algorithm picked it up and carried it toward Tarn Velos. Somewhere else, in systems that had been dormant since the war, an alert flag woke up and registered that someone was asking questions about a GAG convict whose case file had been sealed for four years.

Ben didn’t know that. He wouldn’t know it until the prison alarm changed pitch in a chapter he hadn’t reached yet. Right now, sitting in the cross-reference room with the fluorescents humming and the projector throwing pale blue light across the desk, he felt only the clean satisfaction of having done the right thing.

“Request filed,” Dorrin said. “You’ll have confirmation within the hour. The shuttle terminal is three levels down — civilian transport to Tarn Velos, departures every two hours starting at 0600.”

Ben stood. The fragment was still on the desk in its sealed evidence sleeve. He picked it up, feeling the slight weight of it in his palm — a dead woman’s last act, preserved in heat-degraded plastic and a gap behind a power coupling.

“Inspector.”

Dorrin looked up.

“Thank you.”

Dorrin didn’t answer. He was still watching Ben with that same professional patience, the file still open in his head, the questions still unasked. Ben could have explained. He could have told Dorrin about being fourteen in the GAG, about Jacen’s training, about the way certain systems still felt familiar in a way he didn’t want to examine too closely. He could have justified his speed with the records, his recognition of the encryption marker, his instinct for where Elya would have hidden the chip.

He didn’t. He let the silence hold, and he walked out of the cross-reference room with the fragment in his hand and the ALLIED PARTY badge still clipped to his collar.

The corridor outside was empty. The fluorescents hummed at the same frequency they’d been humming for an hour. The recycled air tasted the same — old plasticrete, heat-stressed electronics, the stale breath of a building that had been processing paperwork since before the war ended. Ben walked toward the turbolift, past darkened offices and closed doors, past a window that looked out onto the Coruscant skyline where the old Jedi Temple still sat like a question no one was asking.

He didn’t look at it.

The turbolift took him down three levels. The shuttle terminal was quiet at this hour: a few civilian travelers, a GA Justice clerk on her way home, a maintenance droid polishing the same section of floor it had been polishing when Ben arrived. He bought a ticket for the 0600 departure to Tarn Velos and found a bench near the boarding gate.

The fragment was still in his hand. He turned it over once — the damaged chip, the corrupted sectors, the duplicate manifest that proved one of Jacen’s last orders had two lives. Elya had hidden it behind a power coupling. She’d sent Ben a message through a dead channel asking him to come alone. She’d been afraid, and careful, and dead before he’d even received the alert.

He was doing the right thing. He was following procedure. He was taking the legal route to Tarn Velos, with Dorrin’s case authority and Luke’s quiet support and a formal visitor request already working its way through the system. He was being careful.

The clean feeling settled over him as he sat there — a procedural satisfaction that almost covered the unease he couldn’t name. Almost.

The shuttle departure board flickered. TARN VELOS — ON TIME — 0600.

Ben tucked the fragment into his jacket and waited for morning.
