The routing indicator pulsed twice, and then the holo-image resolved.

Luke Skywalker was seated in what looked like a ship’s common room. Ben caught the edge of a bulkhead, the corner of a dejarik table, and light from some source he couldn’t see washing over his father’s face in a pale gold sheet. The offworld lag put a faint wobble in the image, a quarter-second delay between Luke’s mouth and the sound of his voice. It made him seem farther away than he was.

“Ben.”

Not a question. Luke said his name the way he always did — like he was glad to hear it and the name itself opened a door. He wore a plain tunic, no robe, and there was more gray in his hair than Ben remembered. The lines at the corners of his eyes had gone deeper since the last time Ben had seen him in person.

Ben sat on the edge of his bunk. The amber alert still pulsed on the holocomm unit beside him, a steady blink he’d stopped looking at but couldn’t stop catching out of the corner of his eye. The message was still open. His hand no longer covered the sender code, but he could still feel the shape of that gesture in his fingers.

“I need you to look at something,” Ben said.

Luke waited. The lag made the silence feel longer than it was.

Ben forwarded the message. He watched his father’s eyes move across text Ben had already memorized — GAG STILL ACTIVE. ONE OF JACEN’S LAST ORDERS HAS TWO LIVES. COME ALONE. CORUSCANT. E.R., and tried to read the quality of the pause that followed.

Luke’s expression didn’t shift. That was the first thing Ben noticed. Not the controlled blankness of someone managing a reaction, but the stillness of someone who had already taken the measure of it and was thinking two steps ahead. His father had always been good at that. It had been reassuring when Ben was young. Now it felt like a door shutting before he’d finished knocking.

“The channel,” Luke said. “You recognized it.”

It wasn’t a question.

“Old GAG infrastructure,” Ben said. “Dead-drop protocol. Should have been decommissioned six years ago.”

“And the sender?”

“Initials only. E.R. I don’t know the name.”

Luke nodded, a small motion the holo-lag stretched into something slower. “You’re sure it’s real.”

Ben opened his mouth to say yes, then stopped. He wasn’t sure. He wanted to be sure, which was not the same thing. The message had landed in his quarters on a channel that didn’t exist anymore, signed by someone he’d never met, telling him that Jacen’s last orders were still alive. He wanted it to be real because if it was real, it mattered, and if it mattered, then the past six years of being told the past was past weren’t the whole story.

“I’m sure enough to call you,” he said.

Luke held his gaze through the holo. The lag made it feel like he was looking at Ben from the bottom of a well.

“Tell me the rest.”

Ben told him. The duplicate manifest: one of Jacen’s last orders, a shipment with two destinations where the public record showed only one. The fragment, hidden somewhere Ben hadn’t recovered yet and preserved by someone who’d known what she was looking at and had been afraid enough to use a dead channel to send for help. The message’s insistence: come alone.

Luke listened without interrupting. When Ben finished, the silence stretched long enough for Ben to hear the soft hum of his quarters — the ventilation, the distant chime of the evening bell from the courtyard below, the faint crackle of the holo-connection keeping itself together across the distance between Shedu Maad and wherever his father was.

“It’s sensitive,” Luke said at last. “But it’s not proof.”

Ben’s shoulders tightened. “It’s enough to act on.”

“It’s enough to verify.” Luke’s voice stayed calm, warm, the register of someone who’d spent decades learning that urgency wasn’t the same as clarity. “It’s not enough to take to the Council.”

“I wasn’t planning to take it to the Council.”

“I know.” Luke leaned back a little, and the movement made the holo flicker. “But if this becomes an official Jedi investigation, it has to go through Saba. And an official Jedi move into old GAG territory — Ben, you know what that would do. Too many people survived the postwar years by pretending they hadn’t helped build it. The moment the Order opens a formal inquiry, every one of them will know someone is looking. The trail will scatter before there’s anything to hold.”

Ben heard the logic. He understood it. GAG hadn’t been just Jacen — it had been an institution, with personnel and records and political sponsors who had spent the years since the war quietly making sure their names stayed buried in the right archives. An official Jedi investigation would be a flare over every one of those graves. Anyone watching old GAG frequencies would disappear. Anyone holding old GAG records would destroy them. Whatever E.R. had found would be gone before the Order finished convening the first hearing.

He understood all of it. It didn’t make the urgency any smaller.

“So we do nothing,” he said.

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said it’s not proof. You said we can’t move officially. That’s the same thing with more words.”

Luke didn’t flinch. He also didn’t answer right away, and the pause wasn’t the pause of someone hunting for the right thing to say. It was the pause of someone who’d already found it and was waiting for Ben to be ready to hear it.

“You’re not only alarmed,” Luke said quietly. “You’re drawn to this.”

Ben’s jaw set. “Someone is probably dead. Someone who used an old GAG channel to ask me for help. If I’m drawn to that, it’s because I should be.”

“I’m not criticizing you.”

“You’re managing me.”

“I’m listening to you.” Luke’s voice stayed level, warm, unshaken. “There’s a difference.”

Ben looked away, toward the window where the late afternoon Hapan light was stretching across the floor in long gold bars. Toward the training mat, the old blade rack, the holoframe on the shelf he hadn’t looked at directly since he’d walked in. His mother’s face was in that frame. He knew it without looking. He always knew.

“This isn’t about Jacen,” he said.

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You were thinking it.”

The lag pulled Luke’s silence into something that felt like a held breath. When he spoke again, his voice was quieter. “I was thinking that you were fourteen when you learned how to recognize a dead-drop channel. I was thinking that someone used that training to find you, and that you knew what the channel was before you checked it. That’s not an accusation, Ben. It’s an observation.”

Ben’s hand tightened on the bunk edge. The metal was cool under his fingers. He could feel the amber pulse still firing on the holocomm, a rhythm that had started before he made the call, steady and patient and impossible to ignore.

“If I go to Coruscant,” he said, “and this is real — if someone’s dead and the manifest is what she said it was — then waiting for proof means letting whoever killed her cover their tracks.”

“Yes.”

“And if I don’t go, and the trail goes cold, then whatever Jacen hid stays hidden. And someone who tried to do the right thing dies for nothing.”

Luke looked at him for a long moment. The holo-light made his eyes look tired in a way Ben didn’t quite remember — farther away, even when they were fixed right on him. There were lines at the corners of his mouth that hadn’t been there a few years ago. His pauses had gotten longer, and the space between his sentences carried a weight Ben couldn’t name and didn’t ask about.

He noticed the distance. He didn’t ask.

“Verify quietly,” Luke said. “Go to Coruscant. Find out what she found. And if it’s larger than it looks — if this is more than one dead channel and one frightened records officer — come back. We’ll figure out the next step together.”

He didn’t say no. Ben understood that this was the most permission Luke could give without making the case official. It wasn’t the answer Ben wanted. It was the answer he’d known he was going to get.

“Quietly,” Ben repeated.

“Quietly. You’re not a formal investigator. You’re a Jedi Knight following up on a message from someone who asked for help. If you’re wrong, the damage stays contained. If you’re right—” Luke paused, and the lag made it feel like a door held open. “If you’re right, you’ll have something concrete to bring back. Something the Order can act on without scattering the trail.”

Ben nodded. The tension in his shoulders hadn’t gone away, but it had shifted into something more useful — purpose, direction, the clean edge of knowing what came next.

“There’s one more thing,” Luke said.

Ben looked up.

“Whoever sent that message used your old service marker. She knew how to find you through a channel that shouldn’t exist anymore. That means she was in GAG, or close enough to GAG to have access to its routing protocols. And if she’s dead —” Luke’s voice didn’t change, but something in his eyes sharpened. “— then whoever killed her may know she sent the message. Be careful who you trust.”

“I know.”

“I know you know. I’m saying it anyway.”

The corner of Ben’s mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but close. “That a father thing?”

“It’s a Luke thing. I’ve been doing it since before you were born.”

The holo flickered. The offworld lag stretched the moment, and for a second Luke’s image blurred at the edges, light smearing across his face like a hand dragged over a holoframe. Then it steadied, and he was looking at Ben again, tiredness still in his eyes, warmth still there too.

“Call me when you land,” Luke said. “And Ben —”

Ben waited.

“It’s all right if it’s personal. That doesn’t make it wrong.”

Ben didn’t answer. He didn’t trust his voice. He nodded once, short and sharp, and ended the call before Luke could see anything else.

The holo-image collapsed. The amber pulse kept firing on the comm unit, steady and patient, and Ben sat on the edge of his bunk in the sudden silence and let it settle around him like cold water.

Nothing in the room had changed: the training mat, the blade rack, the holoframe he still wasn’t looking at. The Hapan light had shifted another degree across the floor, gold deepening toward amber, and the evening bell was ringing again in the courtyard below.

Ben stood. He crossed to the small locker beside his bunk, pulled out his travel pack, and started to pack.

He moved with the efficiency of someone who’d learned to leave places quickly: a change of clothes, his lightsaber clipped to his belt where it belonged, a datapad with the message still loaded and the sender code still glowing faintly under the amber pulse. Credits. A spare comlink. The things he would need if he was walking into a crime scene with no official standing and no backup and no proof.

He told himself he was going because of Elya. Because someone had asked for help, and Ben Skywalker had spent six years being told his past was damage, and here was a chance to make it something else. Here was a chance to use what Jacen had taught him — the dead-drop protocols, the threat-assessment reflexes, the way of reading a room like a tactical map — for something that wasn’t a war crime.

It was a good reason. It was true. It wasn’t the whole truth, and he knew it, and he packed anyway.

Amber pulse still firing, light still shifting, the holoframe stayed where it was, his mother’s face turned toward the wall, and Ben didn’t look at it as he sealed the pack and slung it over his shoulder.

He was going to Coruscant, alone, to find out what E.R. had died for, and whether Jacen’s last orders were still alive, and whether the past everyone else had agreed to let rest was finished with him yet.

The door clicked shut behind him. The quarters were empty. The amber pulse kept firing in the dark.
