The maintenance door grated open onto a service corridor that stank of salt and cold machine oil. Talon went through first, one hand already up to stop Ben before the door had even finished dragging aside.

“Camera,” he said. “Left corner. Twelve-second sweep. Wait.”

Ben stopped, the corridor narrow, its pipes wrapped in fraying insulation, emergency strips washing everything in a dim red that made the rust look like old blood. Storm noise hammered through the walls from every side, a steady low thunder that vibrated through his boots.

“Twelve seconds from when?” Ben asked.

“From when I said wait.” Talon kept counting under his breath, eyes on the corner. “Four years, Skywalker. Four years of watching which cameras the guards check and which ones they don’t. You think I’m going to get shot in a service corridor because you couldn’t stand still for twelve seconds?”

“I’m standing still.”

“You’re talking. That’s not the same thing.” Talon’s count hit its mark, and he moved. “Now.”

The corridor ran thirty meters before it reached a T-junction. Talon took the left branch without slowing, and Ben fell in behind him, lightsaber still in hand but unignited. The hilt was cold against his palm. The Force sat at the edge of his awareness, a low, uneasy tug in places like this—not danger yet, but the shape danger liked to wear before it showed itself. Above them, behind them, somewhere in the prison’s upper levels, Greyline was still moving. Ben could feel them as a diffuse smear, a disturbance that hadn’t quite turned into a threat.

Talon reached the junction and stopped again, palm flat against the wall. “This is the part where I tell you the plan, you tell me it’s a bad plan, and then we do it anyway because it’s the only one.”

“What’s the plan?”

“Sublevel three. Waste runoff channel under the east wing. It ties into the old storm drainage system. The prison sealed it about thirty years ago, but the seals are garbage and the guards use it to move things they don’t want logged.” Talon glanced back at him. “Contraband, mostly. Sometimes people. The channel lets out on the seaward side, below the storm wall. From there we can work our way around the perimeter to the landing pad access road.”

“And the Greyline teams?”

“They’ll have the main exits covered. Visitor wing too. They might have sublevel mapped if they got the real schematics.” Talon’s words came fast, each one running into the next as he thought and talked at the same speed. “What they won’t have is four years of watching which cameras lag by two seconds and which maintenance hatches the guards don’t check because the hinges scream and nobody wants to file the work order. That’s the advantage, Skywalker. That’s the thing I’ve got that they don’t.”

Ben heard what Talon wasn’t saying: the advantage was thin and shrinking, and Greyline had already shown they could read the prison’s systems. Every minute they spent in these corridors gave Greyline another chance to shut a door.

“How far to the runoff channel?” he asked.

“Three more junctions. Maybe four minutes if the hatches cooperate. Maybe ten if they don’t.” Talon was moving again, quick but not panicked, the pace of someone who knew exactly how fast he could go without making mistakes. “The third junction has a camera with a two-second lag. The maintenance hatch after that is supposed to be locked, but the lock’s been broken since before I got here. The guards know about it. They don’t fix it because fixing it would mean admitting they know about it, and then they’d have to explain why they never filed the report.”

“You’ve thought about this.”

“I’ve thought about a lot of things. Most of them involve getting out of this building before someone decides I’m more useful dead than alive.” Talon turned another corner, his hand brushing the wall automatically as he went. “The problem with being the person who knows where the bodies are buried is that eventually someone wants to make sure you’re one of them. I’ve spent four years waiting for someone to ask the right question. What I didn’t plan on was the question coming with a snatch team attached.”

They reached the first junction. Talon raised his hand again, and Ben stopped. The corridor ahead was darker, the emergency lights spaced farther apart, some of them dead altogether. Salt and wet concrete hung in the air, threaded with rust and a faint chemical bite from industrial cleaner.

“Camera’s on a twenty-second cycle,” Talon said. “We’ve got about eight seconds before it sweeps back this way. When I say go, you go. Don’t run. Running trips the motion sensors on the upper levels. Just walk like you’re supposed to be here.”

“Am I supposed to be here?”

“No. But the trick is to walk like you are anyway.” Talon’s eyes stayed on the camera housing, his lips moving slightly as he counted. “Go.”

They crossed in eight seconds, and Talon didn’t stop talking the entire time.

“The lag is a firmware issue. They were supposed to patch it three years ago, but the patch meant taking the whole wing offline for six hours, and the warden didn’t want to deal with the paperwork. So now we have a two-second gap between what the camera sees and what the monitor shows, and if you know exactly when to move, you can be gone before anyone watching the feed knows you were there.” He shoved open a maintenance hatch, the hinges shrieking, and held it for Ben. “That’s the thing about institutions, Skywalker. They’re full of people who know exactly what’s broken and exactly how to use it. The only difference between me and the guards is I’m not pretending the broken things aren’t broken.”

Ben stepped through. The corridor beyond was narrower still, the ceiling lower, pipes overhead wrapped in insulation gone gray with age. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. The storm pressed against the structure, and he could feel the building shifting under it, tiny tremors of metal and stone settling against the wind.

“The guards know about the lag,” he said.

“Of course they do. They use it to take longer breaks. Camera shows an empty corridor for twenty seconds, monitor shows it for eighteen, and if you time your smoke break right, nobody ever sees you leave your post.” Talon was already at the next junction, already counting. “The people who built this place thought they were building a prison. What they actually built was a system full of people who learned how to live inside it. That’s not a flaw. That’s the whole thing.”

Ben followed him through the second junction, through another maintenance hatch, down a short flight of stairs that smelled of salt and rust and something organic he didn’t care to identify. The alarm was fainter here, muffled by the walls, but the wrong note still carried—a constant reminder that the prison’s systems were no longer under prison control.

“The runoff channel,” Ben said. “How bad is it?”

“It’s a waste channel. It smells like waste. It’s narrow, it’s dark, and it’s probably full of things I don’t want to think about. But it gets us out, and right now that’s the only thing that matters.” Talon glanced back over his shoulder. “You’re taking this well.”

“Taking what well?”

“The part where I’m leading you through a sealed maintenance tunnel under a prison that’s currently being raided by private military contractors who want to kidnap me, and you’re not asking whether I know what I’m doing.”

“Do you know what you’re doing?”

“Mostly.” Talon’s voice had the dark, survival-born humor Ben was starting to recognize—the jokes of someone processing fear by talking through it. “The parts I don’t know, I’m making up. The parts I’m making up, I’m doing very confidently. That’s the other thing about institutions. Confidence is half the job. The other half is knowing which cameras have a two-second lag.”

They reached the third junction. Talon stopped, hand up, and Ben saw his shoulders tighten.

“What?”

“The camera’s off.”

Ben looked at the housing. The small red indicator light was dark. “Is that normal?”

“No.” Talon’s voice went flat. “That camera’s been on a twenty-second cycle for four years. It doesn’t go off. It doesn’t get maintenance. It just runs.”

Ben reached for the Force, pushing his awareness outward, and felt it—the disturbance he’d been tracking was closer now, sharper, moving in a pattern that didn’t fit a search. It moved like someone who knew where he was going.

“They’re ahead of us,” he said.

“Yeah.” Talon was already turning, already recalculating. “The junction past this one leads to the runoff channel access. If they’ve got someone there, we’re cut off.”

“Can we go around?”

“Not without going back through the junction we just passed, and if they’re reading the camera feeds, they’ll have seen us cross it.” Talon’s fingers tapped a rhythm against the wall. “They’re not just searching. They’re not just blocking exits. They’re—”

He stopped.

Ben felt it at the same moment: another shift in the Force, another team moving, this one cutting across the secondary route Talon hadn’t even named. The adjustment was too fast, too exact. Someone was reading the prison’s sensor grid in real time, anticipating them before they moved.

“They’re herding us,” Talon said.

Ben looked at him.

“The camera’s off because they don’t need it anymore. They know where we are. They know which routes I know. And they’re closing the ones that don’t lead where they want us to go.” Talon’s voice stayed flat, but his hand had gone still. “There’s only one route left they haven’t covered.”

“Which one?”

“The sea wall. There’s a maintenance gantry that runs along the outside of the storm wall, about forty meters above the surf. It’s exposed. It’s wet. In this storm, it’s probably suicidal.” Talon looked at him, calm in the way of someone who had just done the math and hated the answer. “And it’s the only way out they’re not blocking.”

“Because they want us to take it.”

“Because they want us on the sea wall. I don’t know why. Maybe there’s a team waiting at the other end. Maybe they’ve got a sniper with a clear line on the gantry. Maybe they just want us outside the building where the prison’s security systems can’t interfere with whatever they’re planning.” Talon was moving again, turning down a side corridor Ben hadn’t noticed, pace faster now. “What I know is the route I planned is gone, and the route they’re leaving us is the worst possible option in a storm, and we’re going to take it anyway because the alternative is standing here until they close the last exit.”

Ben followed into a darker corridor, the emergency lights dead completely, the only illumination coming from the faint blue spill of his lightsaber. He’d ignited it without thinking, the blade throwing long shadows against the walls. The storm was louder now, rain drumming harder, and Ben could feel the cold seeping through the metal, the salt air growing stronger as they moved toward the sea.

“The gantry,” he said. “How long?”

“Maybe two hundred meters. Maybe more. Depends which section they’ve left open.” Talon was talking faster now, the complaints surfacing like a pressure valve opening. “It’s going to be wet. It’s going to be cold. The wind’s probably strong enough to knock a grown man off his feet, and the surf’s going to throw spray forty meters up, and if there’s a team waiting at the other end, we’re going to have to fight them on a metal catwalk in the middle of a storm with no cover and no backup and no way to retreat.”

“You’re complaining.”

“I’m always complaining. It’s how I stay calm.” Talon shoved open another hatch, this one heavier, the metal screaming against its frame. “If I stop complaining, that’s when you worry. That’s when it means I’ve stopped thinking and started panicking. Right now I’m complaining, which means I’m thinking, which means I’m going to get us out of this.”

The hatch opened onto a narrow stairwell, the steps wet and worn, the walls streaked with salt. Storm noise hit them full force here—not just rain, but surf roaring below, wind howling through the gantry supports, metal groaning under pressure. Ben tasted salt on his lips.

Talon started up the stairs without hesitation.

Ben followed. The tactical inventory ran through him the way it always did before a fight—the Force reaching outward, mapping the space ahead, the gantry’s length, the storm’s violence, the lack of cover. Lightsaber ready, the blade a steady blue in the dark. Talon’s footing on wet metal, his balance in the wind, his speed on the exposed catwalk. Storm visibility—maybe twenty meters, maybe less, rain and spray cutting the world down to a narrow strip of motion and sound.

He had already said yes. He’d said yes the moment Talon named the sea-wall route and kept moving, the moment the math resolved into a single path forward. The partnership’s terms were being set in the dark, in the salt-cold air, before either of them had named what this was.

Ben didn’t name it now. He just climbed and let Talon climb ahead of him, and trusted that the man who had read the cameras and the lag and the herding pattern before anyone else in the building would read the next threat the same way.

The stairs ended at a heavy durasteel door, salt-crusted and beaded with moisture. Talon put his shoulder to it, and the wind tore it open before he could push.

The sea wall hit them like a fist—wind and spray and the roar of surf forty meters below, the gantry stretching out into the storm, a narrow ribbon of metal suspended over black water boiling under the waves. The rain came sideways, stinging Ben’s face, and the cold landed at once, the kind that got into bone in seconds and stayed there.

Talon stepped onto the gantry, one hand gripping the rail, his body angled into the wind. “I want you to know,” he shouted over the storm, “that this is exactly the situation I was hoping to avoid.”

“You said the sea wall was the only option.”

“It is. Doesn’t mean I have to like it.” Talon was already moving, careful on the wet metal, his complaints carrying on in a steady stream the wind tore apart before Ben could catch most of them. “Four years. Four years of memorizing camera cycles and guard rotations and maintenance schedules, and the one thing I didn’t plan for was a private military contractor who could read the sensor grid faster than I could read the exits.”

Ben followed him onto the gantry. The metal slicked under his boots, the rail cold and wet, the wind shoving at him from every direction at once. Below, surf smashed against the sea wall, throwing spray high enough to mix with the rain. The lightsaber burned steady blue in the gray, the only color left in a world gone flat.

The partnership was real. Neither of them had said it. Neither of them needed to.
