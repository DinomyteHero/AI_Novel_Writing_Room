The morning training salle at Shedu Maad was never empty, but it was never all the way full, either. The Order had been here three years now, and the rhythms had settled into something permanent without ever tipping into home. Hapan stone drank the sound of bare feet on mats, swallowed the snap of training remotes, and turned every grunt of effort into something polite. High windows admitted light that had already passed through three layers of Consortium architecture before it reached a Jedi’s skin.

Ben Skywalker worked through the fourth form at half speed, letting the lightsaber’s weight pull his shoulders through the sequence. The blade was set low, training intensity rather than combat, and its hum was the only thing in the room that felt like it belonged to him.

Two other knights occupied the far end of the salle. He knew them both. Javeth Chorin, a Cerean about five years older than him, was working a remote drill with the focused economy of someone avoiding a bigger problem. Beside him, Tessa Rill, human and sharp-eyed, good with a blade and better at pretending she wasn't, ran through a series of one-handed deflection patterns. They’d nodded when Ben came in. Friendly. Easy. The way you nodded at someone you respected but didn’t quite know how to talk to.

Ben finished the fourth form and moved into the fifth. His body knew the sequence better than his mind did. That was the point. That was always the point.

“Skywalker.”

He looked up. Javeth had caught his remote and was wiping sweat off his forehead with the back of his wrist. “You want to run a partner drill? Tessa’s been hitting the same deflection for twenty minutes and I’m starting to take it personally.”

Tessa didn’t look over. “I’m refining.”

“You’re sulking.”

“I’m refining with emotional discipline.”

Ben lowered his blade. The training setting left a faint green afterimage when he moved it too fast. “What kind of drill?”

“Blade-to-blade. First contact, then reset. I need someone who won’t let me get sloppy.”

He didn’t say someone who trained with Jacen. He didn’t need to. The shape of the request was familiar: Ben was good enough to push them, young enough not to intimidate, and his history meant they’d be careful with him in ways they wouldn’t be with each other.

Ben nodded. “Fine.”

They squared off in the center of the salle. Javeth’s blade was blue, set to the same training intensity. The Hapan stone was cool under Ben’s feet. Light through the windows had shifted a few degrees since he’d started—mid-morning now, the angle gone flat and bright.

Javeth came in fast, a straight thrust that Ben deflected without thinking. Reset. A low sweep, a high feint, a reversal that nearly caught Ben on his off side. Reset. The rhythm built. Ben let his body handle the responses while his mind drifted, not far, just a half-step back, the way it did when he was training with people who weren't family.

He was better than Javeth. He knew it, and Javeth knew it, and neither of them would say it because that wasn’t how the Order worked. But the gap was there, and it was the sort that made people careful. Not in the drill—Javeth was pushing hard, testing him—but in the spaces between drills. In the way people chose their words. In the way they remembered, halfway through a sentence, who his father was, who his mother had been, who had trained him at fourteen.

Ben caught Javeth’s blade on a rising block and held it there for a beat longer than necessary. “You’re dropping your left shoulder.”

“I know.”

“Then stop doing it.”

Javeth grinned, quick and surprised, and reset. “Yes, Master Skywalker.”

“That’s my father.”

“I know that too.”

Tessa had stopped her deflection drill. She was watching them now, her blade still humming at her side. “He’s right about the shoulder. You’ve been doing it since last tenday.”

“I’ve been doing it since I was a padawan. It’s structural.”

“It’s lazy,” Ben said.

Javeth laughed. It was real, unguarded, and for a moment the salle felt like it was supposed to—three Jedi in the same room, pushing each other, the formality of the Hapan architecture softened by shared effort.

Then Javeth said, “Jacen used to say the same thing about my footwork,” and the temperature in the room dropped.

It wasn’t dramatic. Nobody flinched. Nobody looked away. But the space where the laugh had been closed over, and the silence that replaced it was the kind that knew exactly what it was covering.

Javeth’s face tightened. He hadn’t meant to say it. Ben could see him replaying the sentence, hearing the name land in the room, watching the shape of it settle between them.

“Sorry,” Javeth said. “I didn’t—”

“It’s fine.”

Ben said it before Javeth could finish. The words came out automatically, the way they always did, smooth and practiced and just fast enough to shut the door before anyone could push it open. He’d been saying it’s fine about Jacen for six years. He’d gotten good at it.

Tessa was looking at the floor. Javeth was looking at his blade. Ben was looking at the high windows, at the light coming through them, at anything that wasn’t the careful expressions on their faces.

He didn’t resent them. That was the thing. They were good people. They were trying. They just couldn’t stop remembering who he was, and he couldn’t stop being the thing they remembered.

“I’m going to get something to eat,” Ben said. “You should fix your shoulder.”

He deactivated his blade and walked out of the salle before anyone could say anything else.

The refectory was in the eastern wing, where the Hapan architects had given up trying to make Jedi eat in formal dining halls and settled for a long, low room with windows on three sides and tables that could be pushed together or pulled apart depending on the day’s needs. The morning meal was still out: bread, fruit, some kind of egg dish the kitchen droids had been experimenting with for two months without ever quite getting right.

Ben took a plate and a cup of caf and sat at the end of one of the long tables, where he could see the door and the windows and the serving line all at once. It wasn’t a conscious choice. It was just where he sat.

The refectory was about half full. Knights and masters, a few senior padawans, a cluster of younglings at a corner table who were supposed to be eating and were instead conducting some elaborate negotiation involving fruit slices and a stolen datapad. Ben watched them for a moment. The younglings didn’t know who he was. They knew his name, probably—everyone knew his name—but not what it meant. They hadn’t been alive during the war, or they’d been too young to remember. To them, Ben Skywalker was just another Jedi Knight, maybe a little younger than most, maybe a little better with a blade, but not someone whose history required a particular kind of silence.

He was still watching the younglings when Amelia sat down across from him.

She moved quietly for someone who’d grown up in a palace. Ben hadn’t heard her approach, which meant either she’d learned to walk silently or he’d been more distracted than he thought. Both were possible.

“You’re brooding,” she said.

“I’m eating.”

“You’re eating broodily.”

Amelia was seventeen, or maybe eighteen—Ben could never remember exactly, and she seemed to enjoy the ambiguity. She had her mother’s coloring and her father’s way of looking at people like she was already three steps ahead of whatever they were about to say. She was visiting Shedu Maad for the week, a regular thing she did every few months, and the refectory always got a little warmer when she was in it. Not because she was charming, though she was. Because she was family, and family moved through the Order’s spaces with an ease that full-time residents couldn’t quite manage.

“How long are you here?” Ben asked.

“Through the end of the week. Mother has some things to attend to, and I’m supposed to be observing the training curriculum.” She took a piece of fruit off his plate without asking. “So far I’ve observed that the egg dish hasn’t improved.”

“It’s gotten worse.”

“I didn’t think that was possible.”

“Neither did the kitchen droids.”

Amelia grinned. It was quick and sharp, there and gone. “You look like you’ve been in the salle since dawn. Have you talked to anyone who isn’t holding a lightsaber?”

“I talked to you.”

“I meant before me.”

Ben took a sip of caf. It was too hot. He drank it anyway. “Javeth and Tessa. We ran a drill.”

“And?”

“And Javeth’s left shoulder is still a problem.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Amelia looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she looked away, toward the younglings and their fruit-slice negotiations, and let the silence stretch until it became ordinary again. That was one of the things Ben appreciated about her. She knew when to push and when to let the silence do the work.

“You could come to the capital next time,” she said. “Mother always says you’re welcome.”

“I know.”

“But you won’t.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Ben set his cup down. The caf was still too hot. The refectory was still too bright. The younglings had apparently reached some sort of agreement, because the datapad had been returned and the fruit slices were being redistributed in a pattern that suggested complex reparations.

“I’m fine here,” Ben said.

Amelia didn’t answer. She took another piece of fruit off his plate and ate it slowly, her expression unreadable.

“You know,” she said eventually, “most people just say thanks when someone invites them somewhere.”

“Thanks.”

“That’s not what I meant either.”

“I know.”

She smiled again, smaller this time, and stood up. “I have a briefing in twenty minutes. Try to eat something that isn’t caf.”

“I will.”

“You won’t.”

She left the way she’d arrived—quietly, easily, a brief pressure on his shoulder as she passed that might have been affection or might have been a reminder that he was still sitting alone at the end of a table in a room full of people who cared about him and didn’t know how to show it.

Ben finished his caf. The younglings finished their negotiations. The morning light shifted another degree through the windows, and the refectory emptied out around him until he was the only one left.

His quarters were in the residential wing, a single room with a window facing the interior courtyard. It was smaller than the quarters he’d grown up in on Ossus, before the Order had relocated, before the war, before everything. But it was his, and he’d learned to appreciate the quiet.

The holocomm alert was firing when he walked in.

He stopped in the doorway. His hand went to his belt before he remembered he wasn’t carrying a weapon in his own quarters. The alert was a steady amber pulse on the private unit beside his bunk—not the Jedi comms channel, not the general Order frequency, but a secondary routing he hadn’t used in years.

He knew the channel before he checked it; his body knew it. The pulse was degraded, the signal carrying the telltale compression artifacts of old GAG infrastructure, and the part of his brain trained to recognize threat vectors before his conscious mind caught up was already cataloguing the details: routing through standard HoloNet relays, not Jedi encryption, origin point masked behind a dead-drop protocol that should have been decommissioned six years ago.

He crossed the room, sat on the edge of his bunk, and did not open the message.

Amber light pulsed: on, off, on, off. Ben watched it the way he’d watched the high windows in the salle—looking at something that wasn’t the thing he was actually seeing.

Someone still had the old routing. Someone had his GAG service marker. Someone had sent a message through a channel that didn’t exist anymore, and it had found him here, in the heart of the Jedi Order’s new home, three years after everyone had agreed the past was past.

He opened the message.

The text was short. Plain. Sent through a low-priority relay that had degraded the formatting but left the words intact:

*GAG STILL ACTIVE. ONE OF JACEN’S LAST ORDERS HAS TWO LIVES. COME ALONE. CORUSCANT. E.R.*

Ben read it twice. Then a third time. The words didn’t change. The channel didn’t change. The amber pulse kept firing, a steady rhythm that felt like a heartbeat in a room that was too quiet.

*GAG still active.* That wasn’t possible. The Galactic Alliance Guard had been disbanded after the war, its infrastructure dismantled, its personnel scattered, its records purged. Ben had watched it happen. He’d been one of the reasons it happened.

*One of Jacen’s last orders has two lives.* The duplicate manifest. The hidden destination. Whatever Elya Rane had found in the logistics archives at 0233 this morning, it had been real enough to send a message through a dead channel and sign it with initials Ben didn’t recognize.

*Come alone.*

He looked at the sender code. His hand moved before he told it to—flat palm, covering the identifier on the screen, the old reflex of someone who’d learned at fourteen that information was dangerous and visibility was worse. His fingers pressed against the holodisplay, smearing the light, and he sat there with his hand over the code like he was hiding it from someone who wasn’t in the room.

He noticed the movement. He did not remove the hand.

The amber pulse kept firing underneath his palm. The message was real. The channel was real. Someone had used his old service marker to find him, and whoever she was—E.R., a name he didn’t know, a voice he’d never heard—she’d told him the one thing that would get his attention: Jacen’s last orders weren’t finished.

Ben sat on the edge of his bunk with his hand over the sender code and let the knowledge settle. It settled badly. It settled like a weight he’d been carrying without noticing and had just remembered was there.

He needed to tell someone. Not the Order—not yet. Not Saba, not the Council, not the machinery that would turn this into a formal investigation before he’d even seen the evidence. He needed to tell someone who would understand what the message meant before he had to explain it.

He needed to tell Luke.

His hand was still over the code. He looked at it—his own hand, his own fingers, the pale smear of light leaking between them—and felt the old GAG reflex still running, still working, still fitting the shape of a room it should have left six years ago.

He didn’t remove the hand. He reached for the comm with his other one and keyed his father’s private frequency.

The call connected. The routing indicator pulsed once, twice, searching for a signal that was somewhere between Shedu Maad and wherever Luke Skywalker was right now.

Ben waited. His hand stayed where it was. The amber light kept pulsing underneath it, and the message stayed open on the screen, and the past that everyone else had agreed to let rest opened its eyes and looked directly at him.
