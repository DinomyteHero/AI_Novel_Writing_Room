The chrono on the wall read 0233. Commander Elya Rane had been staring at the same two records for forty-one minutes, and the longer she looked, the less she could pretend this was a filing error.

Her terminal was the only light in the narrow third-floor office. Five dark workstations stretched toward the door in two neat rows, their screens black, their chairs shoved in at the exact angle the overnight cleaning crew left them. Climate control hummed steadily in the walls. Tonight she heard every cycle of it.

The public manifest was routine. She had pulled it three days ago as part of a backlog audit, the kind of task that landed on her desk because no one else wanted wartime logistics records and because Elya Rane had spent fifteen years becoming the person who would. Quarantine/survey shipment QS-7741-MAAD, logged four days before the Battle of Shedu Maad, authorized by the Galactic Alliance Guard's medical logistics division, destination a forward staging depot that had been overrun forty-eight hours after the shipment cleared. The cargo listed standard field-hospital supplies. The routing chain was dull. The signatories were dead, transferred, or administratively dissolved when the GAG was decommissioned six years ago.

She had nearly closed it. Dead shipment, dead logistics chain, no living officer to flag it for, no audit request waiting on the other end. But something in the authorization timestamp had snagged her attention — a gap of fourteen seconds between the encryption seal and the routing entry, too small for a human clerk to have entered anything, too precise for a system lag.

So she had pulled the raw transfer log. And the raw transfer log had a duplicate.

Not a copy. Not a clerical double-entry. A second manifest wearing the same shipment identity — QS-7741-MAAD, same quarantine/survey classification, same cargo declaration — with a different destination buried under three layers of GAG internal routing. The encryption marker was Jacen Solo's personal seal.

She checked it four times. The metadata was unambiguous: the duplicate had been encrypted by Jacen Solo, personally, four days before he died at Shedu Maad. The public shipment had gone to the staging depot and been destroyed. The hidden shipment had gone somewhere else.

The tightening behind her sternum was not panic. It was the earlier thing, the thing that came before you admitted you had already decided.

Elya drew her hands back from the terminal and laid them flat on the desk. The chrono read 0238. Climate control cycled again. The fluorescent bar over her workstation buzzed at a pitch that made her teeth ache if she thought about it, so she didn't.

For six years she had not thought about a lot of things, including the wrong approval field. She had not thought about that in months, maybe longer if she was honest. But it was there now, rising through the procedural calm the way old corrections always did when you were alone at 0238 with a dead man's encryption marker burning on your screen.

Ben Skywalker had been fourteen. She had seen his name in the personnel routing field — the one that authorized field-unit attachment for GAG operations, the one that put children into war zones with a clean administrative signature — and she had moved it. Quietly. No note, no flag, nothing that would leave a record of who had done it or why. Just a records correction. A name shifted from one field to another, from active routing to administrative hold, and if anyone had ever noticed, they had not asked.

She had not been able to bear one more child being turned into a clean bureaucratic answer, and fourteen was the same age her son would have been, if the orbital bombardment had come three days later. The same age her son would have been, if the orbital bombardment had come three days later.

Elya closed her eyes. Opened them. The terminal was still there. The duplicate manifest was still there. Jacen Solo's encryption marker was still there, bright and absolute, and she was the only person in the galaxy awake with proof that one of his last orders had two lives.

She reached for her keyboard.

The GAG dead-drop protocol was obsolete. It should have died with the rest of the operational infrastructure six years ago. But Elya had spent fifteen years in post-conflict logistics, and one of the first things you learned was that decommissioned did not mean dead. It meant no one was paying attention. It meant the old channels still existed, still accepted traffic, still routed to the service markers they had been built to recognize — because no one had issued the shutdown command, or because the shutdown command had been filed in the wrong approval field, or because the people who knew how to issue it were dead or transferred or administratively dissolved.

She composed the message in the old GAG format. She used the identity-address tied to Ben Skywalker's service marker — the one he had carried as a fourteen-year-old field asset, the one that should have been purged when the Guard was disbanded. She kept it short.

*GAG is still active. One of Jacen's last orders has two lives. Come alone. — E.R.*

Her hands moved on muscle memory. Her brain lagged half a beat behind them, that familiar sensation of making a records correction before anyone could call it a choice. She sent the message before she could think about what sending it meant.

The chrono read 0244.

She sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, watching the confirmation pulse on her screen. Climate control hummed. The fluorescent bar buzzed. The recycled air tasted of old plasticrete and heat-stressed electronics, the same way it had on every late shift for fifteen years.

Then she pulled the data chip from her terminal.

It was warm. She held it in her palm for three seconds, feeling residual heat through her skin, then crossed to the dark workstation at the end of the row — the decommissioned one, the one that had been offline since before she transferred to this office, the one no one ever touched because it was easier to leave it there than file the removal paperwork.

She wedged the chip into the gap behind the power-coupling housing.

Not obvious. Not a safe. Just the place she used when she had spent fifteen years learning where people looked and where they didn't — the blind spot behind infrastructure, the space between the thing that mattered and the thing that powered it. Residual heat from the coupling would partially degrade the chip over time. She knew that. She was counting on it. A damaged fragment was harder to read — and harder to find, if someone searched by procedure instead of instinct.

She straightened.

The door-seal clicked.

Elya turned.

A man stood between her and the corridor. She had not heard footsteps. She had not heard the door open. Only the seal disengaging — a sound she had heard ten thousand times, the soft pneumatic release of an interior office door — and then he was there, and the absence of footsteps was worse than a shout would have been.

He was young. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair, cut short but not military. A face that would have been unremarkable if not for the stillness in it — the way he held himself, the way his hands rested at his sides without tension or hurry. He wore a plain dark jacket, no insignia. His eyes moved across the office once, cataloguing, then settled on her.

"Commander Rane," he said.

His voice was quiet. Not soft — quiet, the way someone sounded when they had spent years rehearsing the exact words of an old dismissal.

Elya's hands were at her sides. She did not remember lowering them. Her terminal still glowed behind her, the duplicate manifest still open, Jacen Solo's encryption marker still burning on the screen. She did not look at it. She did not look at the dark workstation at the end of the row. She looked at the man between her and the door and thought, very clearly: There is no other exit.

"I don't believe we've met," she said.

"No." He did not move. "You found something tonight."

It was not a question. The tightening behind her sternum shifted into something colder, not panic, not yet, but the thing that came right before it. The thing that made your hands steady and your voice calm because your body had already understood what your mind was still refusing to name.

"I found a clerical error," she said. "Routine audit backlog. Nothing that needs attention at this hour."

"You sent a message."

The chrono read 0251. Nine minutes since she had sent the dead-drop. Nine minutes, and this man was standing in her office, and he knew.

"I don't know what you're referring to," she said.

He took a step forward. Not toward her — toward the terminal. His movement was unhurried, exact, the way someone moved when aligning tools at exact angles. Elya stepped aside without meaning to. Her body made the calculation before her mind did: let him see the screen, let him think the screen is what matters, keep him away from the dark workstation at the end of the row.

He stopped at her terminal. Looked at the two manifests. Looked at the encryption marker. His expression did not change.

"You sent a message," he said again. "To Ben Skywalker. Using an obsolete GAG dead-drop protocol tied to his old service marker."

Elya's mouth was dry. She kept her hands visible. She kept her voice level. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"You told him the GAG is still active. You told him one of Jacen's last orders had two lives. You told him to come alone." He tipped his head a fraction, not quite looking at her. "You signed it E.R."

Climate control cycled. The fluorescent bar buzzed. Elya could hear her own heartbeat, steady pressure behind her ears, and thought: think in fields. Think in records. Keep it bureaucratic. Keep it abstracted. Buy one more second by staying in the vocabulary of logistics and not the vocabulary of dying.

"The dead-drop protocol was decommissioned six years ago," she said. "Any message sent through it would be undeliverable."

"You know that's not true."

"I know the shutdown order was never properly filed. I know the routing infrastructure still exists. I know a message sent through it would reach its destination if the destination marker was still active." She paused. "I also know that Ben Skywalker's service marker should have been purged when the GAG was disbanded."

"It wasn't."

"No," she said. "It wasn't."

The man looked at her. His eyes were a color she couldn't name in the fluorescent light — pale, maybe, or just washed out. Nothing threatening in his expression. Nothing angry. Just the same stillness, the same quiet, the same sense of someone who had spent years rehearsing the exact words of an old dismissal and had finally found someone to say them to.

"You preserved something," he said. "A fragment of the duplicate manifest. You hid it."

Elya did not move. Her face did not move. She had spent fifteen years in post-conflict logistics, and one of the things you learned was how to keep your face still when someone was reading your records.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said for the third time.

He raised his hand.

It was not a threatening gesture. It was not abrupt. It was the sort of motion someone made when reaching for a datapad, or adjusting a light, or doing any of the thousand small things people did with their hands in an office at 0252 in the morning. Except his hand stopped in the air between them, palm toward her, fingers slightly curled, and Elya felt something press against the inside of her skull.

Not pain. Not at first. Pressure — precise, intimate pressure, like someone had laid a thumb on a memory she had not touched in years and was waiting to see if she flinched.

The wrong approval field. Ben Skywalker's name. The moment she had moved it, quiet and fast, before anyone could call it a choice.

The pressure shifted. Went deeper. It slid through the procedural layers of her mind — the audit backlog, the quarantine/survey classification, the fourteen-second gap between the encryption seal and the routing entry — and she understood, with a clarity worse than fear, that he was not reading her terminal. He was reading her.

"You sent the message to Ben Skywalker," he said. His voice remained quiet. Calm. "You preserved a fragment of the duplicate manifest. You hid it somewhere in this office."

Elya tried to think in fields. Tried to keep it bureaucratic. The pressure was building behind her eyes now, a dull ache that spread down her spine and into her chest, and she thought: records correction. Transfer error. Routing anomaly. Think in fields. Think in records. Do not think about the dark workstation at the end of the row. Do not think about the gap behind the power-coupling housing. Do not think about the chip.

"The manifest," he said. "It points toward Iresh."

She had not thought about Iresh. She had not allowed herself to think about Iresh. But the word was there now, rising through the pressure like a bubble through oil, and she could not stop it.

"Yes," she heard herself say.

"What did you tell Skywalker?"

"That one of Jacen's last orders had two lives. That he should come alone. Nothing else." The words came out without permission. Her voice sounded distant, as if someone else were speaking from the far side of the office. "I didn't tell him about Iresh. I didn't tell him about the duplicate destination. I told him enough to come."

The pressure shifted again. Deeper. She felt it push past the procedural layers into something older — the orbital bombardment, the son she had not been able to save, the fourteen-year-old boy whose name she had moved out of a field because she could not bear one more child being turned into a clean bureaucratic answer. The pressure paused there, registering the connection, then moved on.

"The fragment," he said. "Where is it?"

Think in fields. Think in records. The dark workstation. The power-coupling housing. The gap. The chip. Do not think about the chip. Do not think about the gap. Do not think about the dark workstation at the end of the row.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

The pressure became pain.

It was not sudden. It was not violent. It was a gradual, systematic escalation — the way a record was corrected, the way a name was moved from one field to another, the way a child was turned into a clean bureaucratic answer. Elya felt her knees buckle. She caught herself on the edge of her terminal, fingers white on the casing, breath coming in short, sharp pulls that did not feel like her own.

"You hid it," he said. "Where?"

Do not think about the chip. Do not think about the gap. Do not think about the dark workstation at the end of the row. Do not think about the chip. Do not think about the chip. Do not think about the—

She thought about the chip.

The pressure found it.

She felt him register the image — the dark workstation, the power-coupling housing, the gap, the chip wedged into the space behind the infrastructure. She felt him reach for it, and felt him stop.

He could see it. He could see where she had hidden it. But he could not reach it — not through her, not through the pressure, not through whatever method he was using to pull the memory out of her. The image was there, clear and specific, but the location was not. The location was a records instinct, not a documented procedure. The location was fifteen years of knowing where people looked and where they didn't. The location was the blind spot behind the infrastructure, and he could not read it because he had never learned to think like a records clerk.

"The fragment," he said again. "Where?"

Elya's hands were shaking. Her vision blurred at the edges. The pressure was everywhere now — behind her eyes, down her spine, in her chest, in her throat. She could feel her heart fighting it, a frantic, uneven rhythm in a body being pushed past what it could survive.

"I don't know," she said.

It was true. She did know the chip was in the gap behind the power-coupling housing, but not where that was in any way he could extract, not in a way that became a location he could search. The knowledge was procedural, not spatial. It was the kind of knowledge you got from fifteen years in the same office, touching the same equipment, learning the same blind spots until the blind spots became instinct and the instinct became invisible.

He pushed harder.

Elya felt something give. Not physically — something deeper, something structural, something that had been holding her together since the orbital bombardment and the wrong approval field and the fourteen-year-old boy whose name she had moved because she could not bear one more. She felt it crack. She felt the pressure rush through the crack. She felt herself begin to come apart.

Think in fields, she thought. Think in records. The GAG is still active. One of Jacen's last orders has two lives. Come alone. I moved his name out of the wrong field. I couldn't bear one more. I couldn't bear one more. I couldn't—

The chrono read 0300.

Commander Elya Rane died at her terminal, fingers still hooked in the casing, eyes still open, face still set in the expression of someone who had spent fifteen years making records corrections before anyone could call them choices.

The man who had killed her stood over her body for a long moment. His hand was still raised, fingers still curled, expression still unchanged. Then he lowered his hand. He looked at the terminal — the two manifests, the encryption marker, the confirmation pulse from a dead-drop message that should never have been deliverable. He looked at the dark workstations in their double row. He looked at the decommissioned unit at the end of the row, its screen blank, its chair pushed in at the exact angle the overnight cleaning crew left it.

He did not search the office. He had learned what he had come to learn: Ben Skywalker's name, the Iresh destination, the existence of a fragment pointing toward something Jacen Solo had hidden in the final days before Shedu Maad. The fragment itself was beyond his extraction — a gap in his knowledge, a blind spot behind the infrastructure — but he knew it was here. Somewhere in this office, hidden by a records clerk who had spent fifteen years learning where people looked and where they didn't, there was a damaged data chip containing the one piece of the duplicate manifest he had not been able to pull from her mind.

He would not find it tonight. He knew that. The hiding place was procedural, not spatial — the kind of knowledge that died with the person who held it. He could tear the office apart, pull every terminal, search every gap, and still miss it, because he did not think like a records clerk and never would.

He turned. Walked to the door. The seal clicked open, and he stepped through, and the door closed behind him without a sound.

Climate control hummed. The fluorescent bar buzzed. The chrono on the wall read 0302.

Commander Elya Rane's terminal was still lit. The duplicate manifest was still open. Jacen Solo's encryption marker was still burning on the screen. The dead-drop confirmation still pulsed, a steady green light that meant the message had reached a service marker that should have been purged six years ago.

And behind the power-coupling housing of the dark workstation at the end of the row, wedged into a gap no one would search because no one had ever learned to think like a records clerk, a damaged data chip held the one piece of the duplicate manifest that Aevyn Soris had not been able to extract.

The fragment survived.
