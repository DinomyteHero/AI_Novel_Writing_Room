The transit hub smelled of recycled air and fried dough, the two scents mingling into something greasy and faintly sweet that stuck in the back of Ben’s throat. He led Talon through the arrivals corridor without speaking, his eyes snagging on exits by reflex: the maintenance hatch behind the third vendor stall, the emergency pressure door set into the wall near the turbolift bank, the security station with its single bored officer who hadn’t looked up from her datapad in the forty seconds they’d been in view.

Talon was checking cameras. Ben caught the tilt of his head, the way his gaze kept climbing to the corners where duraplast met ceiling. Four visible from the arrivals corridor. Two more in the main concourse. Cheap civilian hub, cheap security: local recording, thirty-day wipes, enough coverage to make people feel watched and not enough to matter.

Neither of them said anything about it. Ben had never discussed that shared room-scanning habit with anyone aloud. He’d learned it at fourteen in the GAG and never stopped doing it, and Talon was doing it beside him as if they’d trained together for years instead of hours.

They were still wet from the storm. The orbital shuttle had been standard issue: civilian passengers, civilian flight plan, nobody asking questions about two men boarding at a coastal survey station with no luggage and no change of clothes. The recycled air had dried them some, but Ben’s tunic still clung damp at the collar, and Talon’s prison-issue jacket sat heavy on his shoulders, making him look smaller than he was.

The main concourse opened ahead, a long, low-ceilinged stretch lined with vendor stalls and plastoid benches, lit by neon strips that buzzed hard enough for Ben to feel in his teeth. The crowd was thin this late—travelers asleep on benches, a few more buying food from stalls that should have been closed, a Rodian family arguing in low, sharp tones near the turbolifts. Nobody asked names here. Ben had spent enough of his youth in places like this to know how to disappear inside them.

He picked a bench near the back wall, angled so they could see both exits and the security station. Talon sat without comment. His face was drawn, the usual sharpness ground down by exhaustion, and he didn’t sweep the room again after he sat. That was the first sign something had shifted. Talon always scanned.

Ben left him there and went to the nearest food stall—a Sullustan outfit selling flatbread wraps and something that smelled like spiced nerf. He bought two wraps and two cups of caf that tasted like it had been sitting on the heater since the last shift change. He brought them back and set one wrap and one cup beside Talon without a word.

Talon looked at the food. Looked at Ben. Then took the wrap and ate.

No joke about the caf. No show of not needing it. No deflection at all. He just ate the way someone did when he hadn’t eaten enough and didn’t have the energy to pretend otherwise.

Ben drank his caf and watched the concourse. The Sullustan vendor was shutting down her stall. The Rodian family had finished arguing and was heading for the turbolifts. The security officer still hadn’t looked up from her datapad.

His comlink buzzed.

Encrypted frequency. GA Justice. Ben set his caf down and answered audio only; a hologram in the middle of a transit hub was the sort of thing people remembered.

“Skywalker.”

“Ben.” Dorrin’s voice came through official and strained, the voice of a man who had been on calls all night and expected to be on them all morning. “You’re on Coruscant.”

It wasn’t a question. Ben didn’t ask how Dorrin knew. The orbital shuttle filed manifests. GA Justice had access to them. Dorrin had probably been watching for Ben’s name since Tarn Velos locked down.

“I’m on Coruscant,” Ben said.

A pause. Ben heard the faint click of a stylus—Dorrin taking notes, pretending to take notes, or giving his hands something to do while he chose his next words.

“The Tarn Velos incident is being classified as a security breach with external complicating factors,” Dorrin said. “That’s the Bureau’s language. It means they haven’t decided whether to issue a warrant for you or pretend you were never there.”

“Which way is it leaning?”

“Toward warrant. The Greyline contractor has filed a formal complaint. They’re claiming you assaulted their personnel and interfered with a lawful security operation.”

“They were running a snatch operation under false procedural cover.”

“I know.” Another stylus click. “I’ve reviewed the visitor wing footage. What’s left of it. The storm took out three exterior cameras, and the interior feeds have gaps the prison’s technical staff are calling weather-related interference.”

Ben heard what Dorrin wasn’t saying. Somebody had scrubbed the footage. Not Dorrin—he didn’t have that level of access—but someone with a reason to make the official record incomplete.

“The prisoner you extracted,” Dorrin continued. “Talon Veyr. Convicted on manifest fraud and unauthorized data access, serving a twelve-year sentence at Tarn Velos Coastal Detention Works. Currently listed as escaped during the security breach. The Bureau has issued a fugitive alert.”

“I’m aware.”

“Are you aware that assisting a fugitive is a felony under GA Justice code?”

“Yes.”

“And that your allied-party access authorization has been suspended pending review?”

“I figured as much.”

Another pause. Longer this time. The stylus had stopped.

“I’m building a file,” Dorrin said. His voice dropped half a step—still official, but closer now, the distance between investigator and person narrowing. “I don’t know what’s going to be in it yet. I don’t know if it helps you or hurts you. But I’m building it, and I’m going to be thorough, and I want you to understand that before you tell me anything else.”

Ben measured his reply. The caf had gone cold. The concourse had gone quiet. Talon had finished his wrap and was sitting with his eyes half-closed—not asleep, just still in a way Ben hadn’t seen from him before.

“I understand,” Ben said.

“Good.” The stylus clicked once more. “I’ll contact you when I have questions. Don’t leave Coruscant.”

The channel cut.

Ben set the comlink on the bench. Talon opened his eyes.

“That sounded fun.”

“He’s building a file.”

“Of course he is.” Talon’s voice was flat, stripped of its usual rhythm. “He’s a GA Justice investigator. Building files is what they do. The question is whether the file says ‘Ben Skywalker, fugitive’ or ‘Ben Skywalker, person who was right about something inconvenient.’”

“He doesn’t know yet.”

“They never do.” Talon picked up his caf and drank. Made a face. “This is terrible.”

“It’s been on the heater since the last shift change.”

“That explains it.” He set the cup down. For a moment he just sat there, looking up at the neon strips, and Ben could see the exhaustion in the way he held his shoulders—not coiled for action, just carrying a load he’d run out of room to set down.

“Iressa Vossk,” Talon said.

Ben waited.

“Former GAG Manifest Integrity Analyst. She was in records and logistics, same division as Elya, same division as me after I got pulled from the field. Her specialty was authentication—she could look at a manifest and tell you whether the routing was real or whether someone had dressed a lie up in the right paperwork.”

“Can she prove the duplicate was deliberate?”

“She can prove it was possible. The duplicate manifest used a cloned shipment identity with a quarantine-survey disguise. That’s not a clerical error. That’s someone with access to the manifest-authorization system and enough operational knowledge to know which shipments wouldn’t be cross-checked.” Talon rubbed at his eyes with the heel of his hand. “Iressa can reconstruct the authorization chain. She can show where the clone diverged from the original. If anyone can prove this was deliberate concealment and not a filing mistake, it’s her.”

“Where is she?”

“Coruscant. Last I heard, she was with a civilian archive contractor—Civitas Archive Systems. Respectable job, respectable life, the sort of thing people build after the war when they want everyone to forget they ever worked for GAG.” A pause. “She won’t want to talk to me.”

“Why not?”

“Because talking to me is what got Elya killed.”

The words landed flat, without emphasis. Talon didn’t look at Ben when he said them. He was still watching the neon strips, his face blank in the way Ben was learning to read: valve shut, humor gone, the bare structure left behind when a man stopped performing because he didn’t have the energy for it.

“She contacted me first,” Ben said. “Elya did. Through a dead GAG channel. She told me one of Jacen’s last orders had two lives. You didn’t get her killed. Someone else did.”

“I know.” Talon’s voice stayed low. “I know that. I also know she wouldn’t have been at that crime scene if I hadn’t spent four years telling her someone was going to come asking about Tess’s old operations. I told her to watch for it. I told her to be ready. And when someone finally came asking, she was ready, and now she’s dead.”

Ben didn’t answer. There wasn’t one that made it better.

“The Greyline snatch team,” Talon said. “They were prepositioned. They had access to the prison’s command protocols. They knew we were in that visitor wing before the alarm even triggered. That’s not a routine security response. That’s an active containment operation.”

“I know.”

“Which means the lie that ruined me—the manifest fraud, the authorization chain, the conviction—isn’t old history. Someone is still running it. Someone is still protecting it. Someone sent a private military contractor to extract me from a prison because I talked to a Jedi about a six-year-old manifest fragment.” Talon turned his head and met Ben’s eyes. “I want to know who.”

The words hung in the recycled air. Ben felt the weight of them settle—not gratitude, not warmth, not the clean logic of a man joining a cause he believed in. This was colder. The arithmetic of a man who had just learned his ruin was still somebody’s active operation, and who wanted to know by whom, and who was willing to stay in the line of fire to find out.

“You’re hunted,” Ben said. “Legally compromised. The Bureau has issued a fugitive alert. If you stay on Coruscant, you’re staying in the line of fire.”

“I know.”

“If we do this, there’s no clean way out. No institutional cover. No legal protection. Just the case and whatever we can prove before somebody stops us.”

“I know that too.”

Ben held his gaze. Talon didn’t look away.

“Then we find Iressa Vossk,” Ben said.

Talon nodded once. He didn’t say thank you. Ben didn’t expect it. The partnership that had formed in the storm and the gantries and the skiff’s cramped cabin had just become something else—not clean recruitment, not gratitude, but the colder logic of two people who had both been touched by the same lie and wanted to know who was still telling it.

Ben’s comlink buzzed again. Different frequency this time—private, encrypted, the channel his father used when he didn’t want the call logged anywhere official.

Ben answered. “Dad.”

“Ben.” Luke’s voice was quiet, tired in a way Ben noticed and didn’t ask about. “I’ve spoken to the Bureau. They’re not issuing a warrant yet. I called in a few favors.”

“How many do you have left?”

“Enough.” A pause. “Not many. But enough for this.”

“I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

“For making you use them.”

The silence on the other end was the kind Ben had heard before from his father—not anger, not disappointment, just the careful pause of a man choosing words that would land without wounding.

“You made a choice,” Luke said. “I don’t know yet whether it was the right one. But you made it, and you’re standing by it, and I’m not going to let the Bureau bury you before we find out what’s actually happening.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me yet. The Bureau’s patience has limits. So does mine. I need you careful, Ben. More careful than you’ve been.”

“I will.”

Another pause. Ben heard ship systems in the background, the low hum of a hyperdrive. His father was on a ship, doing damage control from a distance, calling in favors Ben hadn’t known he still had, holding a shape that had no institutional frame and doing it anyway.

“I love you,” Luke said. “Be safe.”

“I love you too.”

The channel closed.

Ben sat with the comlink in his hand for a moment. Talon was watching him—not staring, just watching, the way he’d watched Ben in the skiff after the sea wall dropped behind them.

“Your father,” Talon said. “He does that a lot? Calls in favors to keep you out of legal trouble?”

“Not often.”

“How often is not often?”

Ben thought about it. “This is the first time.”

Talon took that in. Then he picked up his cold caf and drank the rest in one long swallow, grimacing at the taste.

“Iressa Vossk,” he said. “Civitas Archive Systems. I can find the address, but she’s not going to open the door for me. You’ll have to do the talking.”

“I can do that.”

“She’s going to be angry. She built a respectable life. She’s not going to want to burn it for a dead woman and a convicted fugitive and a Jedi with no institutional cover.”

“I know.”

“And she’s going to want payment. Iressa always wants payment. She calls it a consultation fee. It’s how she pretends she isn’t doing favors for people who are going to get her killed.”

“We’ll pay her consultation fee.”

Talon almost smiled. Not quite—the valve was still shut, the humor still offline—but something moved at the corner of his mouth, a ghost of a rhythm that might come back later, once the exhaustion eased and the danger was less immediate.

“You’re going to be paying a lot of consultation fees before this is over,” he said.

“Then we’d better find out who’s funding this operation.”

Talon nodded. The flicker faded. The weight settled back into his shoulders.

“Iressa can tell us that too,” he said. “If she’s still alive.”

The neon strips buzzed overhead. The security officer still hadn’t looked up from her datapad. The concourse stayed quiet, anonymous, full of people who didn’t want to be noticed. Ben sat on the plastoid bench with his cold caf and his fugitive partner and the name of the next lead, and felt the threshold he’d crossed settle into something colder and more specific.

He had a partner. He had a lead. He had a much larger problem than he’d started with.

And somewhere in the city above them, a former GAG analyst was going about her respectable life, not knowing that two hunted men were about to knock on her door and ask her to burn it all down.
