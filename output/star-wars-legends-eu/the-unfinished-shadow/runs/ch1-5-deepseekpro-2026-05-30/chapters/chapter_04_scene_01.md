The third-floor corridor smelled of old plasticrete with something chemical beneath it, a sharp processed tang that told Ben the scene had already been worked over before he got there. He passed two uniformed Coruscant Security officers, and neither stopped him. That meant Dorrin had cleared his name at the checkpoint. The Bureau of Post-Conflict Logistics was a narrow building jammed between a data-archival annex and a municipal power substation, the sort of address that happened because somebody had needed office space in a hurry after the war and nobody had bothered to move out.

A window at the corridor’s midpoint caught the mid-morning light. Through it, across the gap between the Bureau and the next tower, the upper spires of the vacated Jedi Temple rose against the pale Coruscant sky. Sunlight struck the stone at an angle that made the empty windows look shut.

Ben noticed it the way you notice a word you’ve decided not to say, and kept walking.

Inspector Cale Dorrin waited outside the office door. Compact, dark-skinned, early fifties, he had the stillness of someone who had spent most of his career in rooms where people were deciding how much to tell him. His GA Justice credentials hung from a lanyard around his neck. His hands rested at his sides, not folded, not tucked away, a man who had been standing in corridors for too long to waste energy on fidgeting.

“Skywalker,” he said.

Not a greeting. A confirmation.

“Inspector Dorrin.”

Dorrin did not offer a hand. Ben did not expect one. Luke’s quiet word to someone who knew someone at Justice had opened the door, but it hadn’t made Dorrin happy about it. Ben could read that in the set of his shoulders: a professional making room for an irregularity because the evidence supported it, and not by much.

“Your father’s office said you’d be coming,” Dorrin said. “They didn’t say why you’d want to.”

“The woman who died sent me a message before it happened. She asked for my help.”

“You knew Commander Rane personally?”

“No.”

Dorrin waited. The silence wanted more. Ben understood the measure of it. He’d been sized up before, by people with better reasons and worse intentions, and he knew how to let a silence stretch until the other person filled it. He’d learned that from someone whose name he was not going to think about in this corridor.

“She used an old routing protocol to reach me,” Ben said. “From before the war ended. She thought I’d understand what it meant.”

“And did you?”

“I understood enough to get on a ship.”

Dorrin studied him for another beat, then turned and pressed his palm to the door’s access panel. The lock clicked open.

“The body’s been removed,” he said. “Forensics finished their first pass two hours ago. You get ten minutes, and you don’t touch anything the techs haven’t cleared. If you see something I need to know about, you tell me before you move it. Those are the limits.”

“I understand.”

“You understand them,” Dorrin said, “or you accept them?”

Ben met his eyes. “Both.”

The office was smaller than he’d expected. Six workstations sat in two rows, five of them dark, chairs shoved in and terminals blank. The sixth was angled toward the near wall, its screen still lit with a faint amber glow from a system not yet powered down. The chair was turned a little away from the desk, as if the person sitting there had twisted toward the door before she died.

A dark smear marked the edge of the terminal casing. Dried blood, brown-black under the harsh fluorescent overhead. The air carried the chemical residue of forensic processing — solvent and something sharper under it, the ghost of whatever the techs had used to lift prints and seal evidence bags.

Ben stopped just inside the doorway. His eyes moved first to the corners of the room, the sightlines, the single door behind him. The habit fired before he could stop it, counting exits and mapping angles of approach. He’d done it since he was fourteen. He’d never broken it, and he’d stopped trying.

Then he opened himself to the Force, and the room turned.

A living presence left a trace in the Force — not a memory, not a ghost, but an impression, the shape of a person pressed into the space where they had been. When someone died naturally, the trace faded gently, like a note held and released. When someone died violently, it stayed jagged, the Force still ringing with what had happened.

This was different.

The silence where Elya Rane had been was absolute: not fading, but removed, precise, surgical, as if someone had cut her out of the room and sealed the edges. There was no panic in the residue. No struggle. No desperate flare of a mind fighting back. Just the clean, intimate wrongness of something systematic done to a person who had no way to stop it.

Ben went still. His breathing slowed. He knew the shape of this. Knew it without wanting to, before he had a name for it, the way you know the weight of a door before you push it open. The Force-impression was controlled, deliberate, almost clinical — not rage, not passion, not the sloppy violence of someone who wanted to hurt. Someone had come into this room and done something to Elya Rane that she could not fight off, and they had done it with precision.

He pulled back from the impression. Not all the way — he still needed to stay open enough to sense what Elya had left — but enough to put a little distance between himself and the thing that had been done here. His heart was going too fast. He forced it down.

“You all right?”

Dorrin stood in the doorway, watching him. The question was professional, not personal, but his face said he knew the difference between shock and recognition.

“Fine,” Ben said. “The Force residue is strong. She didn’t die easily.”

“She didn’t die messily, either. No defensive wounds. No sign of a struggle. The preliminary report says cardiac arrest, but the tech who wrote it put a question mark next to it.” Dorrin paused. “You’re telling me the Force says otherwise.”

“I’m telling you someone was in this room with her, and whatever they did, she couldn’t fight it.”

Dorrin took that in without comment. His face stayed flat, but Ben saw his hand shift at his side, the small adjustment of a man filing away information that didn’t fit any category yet.

“Ten minutes,” Dorrin said. “Starting now.”

Ben moved farther into the room. He didn’t go to the terminal first, or the chair, or the smear on the casing. He went to the wall behind the workstation, where the power conduits ran down in a metal casing painted the same pale gray as the rest of the office. The casing was scuffed near the floor, the paint worn through in a pattern that suggested someone had pried at it recently.

He knelt. The gap behind the casing was narrow, barely wide enough for a hand, and the air that came out of it was warm from the lines running behind the wall. He reached in, fingers brushing dust and cable sheathing and the rough edge of the wall’s interior.

Something smooth touched his fingertips. A data chip, smaller than his thumb, wedged in the seam where the casing met the wall. He worked it free carefully, feeling the heat that had warped the casing slightly, the faint tackiness of plastic that had been too close to a power coupling for too long.

He stood with the chip in his palm, small and dark and damaged, and felt nothing like triumph. Only the flat confirmation that Elya Rane had been good at her job, and it had mattered.

“Dorrin.”

The inspector crossed the room. He looked at the chip in Ben’s hand, then at the gap behind the power casing, then back at Ben.

“That’s not where evidence usually hides.”

“She didn’t put it in a safe,” Ben said. “She put it where someone who knew her would look. Someone who knew how she thought about records.”

“And you knew how she thought about records?”

“No. But I knew she was scared, and running out of time, and she still found a way to hide something the person who killed her didn’t find.” He held out the chip. “That’s not luck. That’s instinct.”

Dorrin took it. He turned it over, studying the heat-warped casing, the partially corrupted contact strip. “This is degraded. Might not be readable.”

“It’s readable enough. She wouldn’t have hidden it otherwise.”

“You’re confident about that.”

“I’m confident she knew what she was doing.”

Dorrin didn’t answer. He crossed to the terminal — the one still lit, still angled toward the door — and slotted the chip into the reader. The screen flickered. For a moment nothing happened, and Ben felt the cold possibility that the heat had cooked the data clean through.

Then the fragment resolved.

It wasn’t a complete file. Corruption had chewed out whole sections — strings of garbled characters, blank spaces where data should have been, a timestamp that cut off mid-sequence. But what remained was enough. A shipment manifest, dated four days before the Battle of Shedu Maad. A quarantine/survey classification Ben recognized as a wartime disguise protocol. A destination field that read IRESH — CIVILIAN RELIEF SURVEY, and beneath it, in a secondary routing layer that shouldn’t have existed, a duplicate authorization tagged with an encryption marker Ben hadn’t seen in three years.

Jacen Solo’s personal cipher.

The encryption was still active. The marker was unmistakable — the same signature pattern that had sealed GAG’s most sensitive wartime orders, the ones that bypassed the standard chain and went straight to Jacen’s inner circle. Ben had seen it a hundred times when he was fourteen, sitting in briefings he wasn’t supposed to be in, reading documents he wasn’t supposed to read.

Dorrin studied the screen. “That’s a GAG encryption marker.”

“Yes.”

“Personal-level. Not standard fleet.”

“Yes.”

“And you recognize it.”

Ben said nothing. He didn’t need to. Dorrin was already looking at him differently, the same professional assessment with something new behind it, the quiet recalibration of a man who had just realized the Jedi in his crime scene knew more about the evidence than he’d admitted.

“It’s Jacen Solo’s marker,” Ben said. “He used it for orders that didn’t go through the regular chain. The ones he wanted kept off the books.”

“Off the books from GAG.”

“From everyone.”

Dorrin let that settle. His expression did not change, but his silence said he was rearranging the pieces, fitting Ben into a different category than the one he’d assigned at the door.

“The fragment shows a duplicated shipment identity,” Dorrin said, turning back to the screen. “Public version went to a Hapes field hospital. The hidden duplicate — this one — was routed somewhere called Iresh under a quarantine disguise. The encryption means somebody at the highest level didn’t want it found.”

“Jacen didn’t want it found.”

“Jacen Solo is dead.”

“The encryption isn’t.”

Dorrin tapped the screen, scrolling through the partial data. “This doesn’t tell us what was in the shipment. It doesn’t tell us who authorized the duplicate. It doesn’t tell us why Commander Rane died for it.” He straightened. “It tells us somebody went to a lot of trouble to hide a wartime logistics order, and three years later somebody killed a woman to keep it hidden.”

“Not nothing.”

“No,” Dorrin said. “Not nothing. But not enough.”

He ejected the chip and held it up. “This is evidence now. It goes into the chain. I can run it against the Bureau’s records and see if the manifest pattern matches any other cases. But if the encryption is Solo’s personal marker, I can’t break it. I don’t have the clearance, and even if I did, the cipher’s probably tied to a key that was decommissioned after the war.”

“So we need someone who can read it.”

“We need someone who can read the audit trail behind it. The manifest itself is a dead end. But the authorization chain — who signed off, who routed it, who buried the duplicate — that’s a trail. And trails lead to people.”

Dorrin moved to the terminal and began typing. His fingers were quick and exact, the rhythm of someone who had spent years pulling records out of systems that didn’t want to give them up. The screen shifted through several layers of Bureau interface, then into a search protocol Ben didn’t recognize.

“I’m running the manifest pattern against GA Justice’s conviction records,” Dorrin said without looking up. “If anyone’s been prosecuted for similar authorization fraud — cloned shipments, quarantine disguises, off-book routing — it’ll flag here.”

Ben waited. The fluorescent light hummed overhead. The chemical smell from the forensic work was fading, replaced by the warm-plastic scent of the terminal and the faint electric tang of the power conduits behind the wall. Through the door he could hear the murmur of voices in the corridor — Coruscant Security officers talking quietly, the distant sound of a door opening and closing.

The search results filled the screen in a series of lines, each a case file with a name and date and disposition. Dorrin scrolled through them, his face blank, his eyes moving with the speed of someone who had learned to read patterns in data before he understood what they meant.

Then he stopped.

“Here,” he said. “Talon Veyr. Convicted two years ago — fraud, unauthorized routing, obstruction. The manifest pattern in his file is close to what we’re looking at. Cloned shipment identity, quarantine disguise, off-book authorization chain.”

Ben stepped closer to the screen. “Where is he now?”

“Tarn Velos Coastal Detention Works. Medium-security, long-term. He was prosecuted by the same office that handled the post-war GAG cases.” Dorrin tapped another key, and a personnel file opened beneath the conviction record. “Before the conviction, he worked in GAG logistics. Records and routing. And before that —” He paused, reading. “Before that, he was assigned to Commander Elya Rane’s section. She was his supervisor for eighteen months after a reassignment.”

Ben felt the case shift. Small movement, but enough to change what came next. A name, a location, a reason. The fragment alone meant nothing. But Talon Veyr had worked under Elya, and his conviction involved the same sort of manifest fraud, and he was alive and reachable in a way Elya wasn’t anymore.

“He can read it,” Ben said.

“He can read the audit trail. Whether he’ll is a different question.” Dorrin straightened from the terminal. “Tarn Velos is a GA Justice facility. To get access to an inmate, I’d have to file a formal request — investigative interview, material witness, something that justifies the visit. The request goes through the same system that flagged his original conviction. It’ll generate paperwork. Notices. Records.”

“Which means people will see it.”

“Which means anyone who’s been watching the old GAG cases will know somebody’s asking questions about Talon Veyr.”

Ben understood what Dorrin was telling him. The legal route was the only path that wouldn’t blow the investigation wide open — and it was also the route that would wake old alert systems. Filing the request would announce that the case existed. It would warn whoever had killed Elya that someone was following the trail.

He didn’t know that yet. He stood in the harsh fluorescent light of the records office, the damaged chip in evidence, Talon Veyr’s name burning on the screen, and he did the responsible thing.

“File the request,” he said.

Dorrin looked at him. “You understand what that means.”

“I understand that we need somebody who can read the fragment, and he’s the only name we have. If filing the request wakes something up, it wakes something up. We’ll deal with it when it does.”

The inspector held his gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded once and began typing the request into the terminal.

Ben looked at the screen, at the name and the location and the conviction record, and felt the forward lean of a case clicking into its next stage. Beneath that, quieter, was the realization that he was going to have to explain to Dorrin why he knew GAG authorization chains faster than he should. Not today. But soon.

He had what Elya saved. He still didn’t know what it cost to have asked for it.
