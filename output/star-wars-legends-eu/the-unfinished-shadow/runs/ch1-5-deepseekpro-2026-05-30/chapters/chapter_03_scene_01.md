The call connected on the third tone.

Luke's holo resolved above the desk in blue-white relay static, his image flickering once before it settled. Warm light glowed somewhere behind him, but the room itself stayed out of focus, the angle too tight to make out more than a suggestion of diplomatic quarters and someone else's schedule. The kind of place with polite doors and no privacy.

"Ben." Luke's voice carried the half-second lag of a signal crossing too many relays. "I was hoping you'd call."

Tired, and not the ordinary kind.

Not the ordinary tired that came from a long day or a late Council session. Something farther back than that. His eyes seemed set deeper than Ben remembered, the lines at the corners cut a little harder. He was looking straight at Ben, attentive and present, but there was something in the attention Ben couldn't name. Like Luke was seeing him from a little farther away than the holo distance explained.

Ben noticed. He didn't ask.

"I've got something," he said. "It's not official yet. It might not be anything."

Luke waited. The silence was listening, not managing, at least not yet, and Ben felt the familiar pressure of having asked for his father's attention and now needing to earn it.

He laid it out: the degraded-channel flag, the dead-drop protocol that should not exist, the message from someone signing E.R., using his old GAG service marker, saying one of Jacen's last orders had two lives and she couldn't stop it alone. Then the fragment she'd mentioned — a duplicate manifest, personally encrypted, pointing to a world called Iresh that Ben had never heard of.

He gave Luke all of it, the words coming out clean and ordered, the way he'd learned to deliver a briefing when he was fourteen and the person listening could have him reassigned if he sounded uncertain. He didn't sound uncertain now; he sounded like someone putting evidence on a table and waiting for a verdict.

Which was the problem. He'd handed Luke the power to say stop, and he'd done it before he realized he was doing it, and now he had to sit through the relay lag and watch his father's face for the exact silence that told him which way this was going.

Luke didn't interrupt. He let it all land, then stayed quiet for a beat longer — not the pause of someone deciding what to say, but the pause of someone who already knew and was choosing the cleanest way to say it.

"Who else knows about the message?"

The first question, quiet and precise. Ben felt something in his chest tighten.

"Just me. She sent it to my service marker, not the Order's routing." He hesitated. "I don't think she had time to send it to anyone else."

Luke took that in without comment. His holo-image wavered, a flicker of interference catching the edge of his expression before it settled again. When he spoke, his voice was still calm, still careful, the voice of a man who had learned a long time ago that urgency didn't make answers arrive faster.

"The fragment she mentioned. Do you have it?"

"No. She said she'd hidden it. I don't know where."

"So right now you have a message from a dead-drop channel, a fragment you haven't seen, and a destination that doesn't appear in any public record."

"I have a woman who's probably dead," Ben said. "And a logistics order Jacen encrypted personally four days before Shedu Maad. That's not nothing."

"I didn't say it was nothing."

Luke's tone didn't shift. It didn't need to. The correction was in the steadiness of it — the way he let Ben's edge run past without catching. Ben felt the heat in his own voice and didn't push again.

"I'm not saying you're wrong to be alarmed," Luke said. "I'm saying this is sensitive, and it's not proof yet. If the Order moves officially — if Saba convenes the Council and opens an inquiry into a GAG operation that was supposed to be dead three years ago — we don't just investigate. We announce. We put GAG back in the news. We give every former officer who survived by pretending they'd helped nothing a chance to start covering their tracks before we have anything concrete to hold."

Ben knew the argument. He'd known it before Luke made it, which was part of why it landed the way it did — not as new information but as the confirmation of a door closing he'd seen coming from the moment the call connected.

"GAG wasn't just Jacen," Luke said. His voice stayed quiet, careful, but there was weight under it now. "Too many people built it. Too many people staffed it. Too many people woke up the morning after the war ended and started erasing their own names from the routing lists. An official Jedi move scatters them. You know that."

"I know that."

"Then you know why I'm asking you to verify quietly."

The word landed. Quietly. Not stop. Not wait for the Council. Ben heard the gap in what Luke had said and understood it for what it was: the most permission Luke could give without making the case official, and the most caution he could apply without telling his son to stand down from something that might be real.

It should have been enough. On paper, it was enough. Ben could go to Coruscant, find the fragment, confirm whether Elya was alive or dead, and come back with something concrete. That was the smart play. That was the play Luke was offering.

Ben didn't want the smart play. He wanted Luke to say go — to validate the urgency, to short-circuit the process, to tell him to move. He wanted his father to look at the same evidence and feel the same cold drop in his stomach Ben had felt when the degraded-channel flag fired. He wanted the case to feel as obviously urgent to Luke as it did to him.

And Luke was looking at him with those tired eyes, farther away than they should have been, and Ben understood that Luke saw the case clearly. He just didn't see only the case.

"You're drawn to this," Luke said.

It wasn't a question.

Ben's hand moved toward the holoframe on his desk — the one he didn't look at directly during calls, the one angled so the image wasn't visible from the pickup. His fingers stopped an inch short of the frame. He pulled his hand back.

"It's a lead," he said. "Someone reached out to me specifically. Someone who knew the old routing, who knew my service marker, who trusted me enough to send a message that could get her killed. I'm not going to let that sit in my inbox and do nothing."

"I'm not asking you to do nothing."

"Then what are you asking?"

Luke didn't answer right away. The gap stretched — not the technical lag of the relay, but the human lag of a man choosing his words. His image flickered again, and in the half-second of distortion Ben saw something in his father's face he couldn't name. Not doubt. Not hesitation. Something older than either.

"I'm asking you to be careful," Luke said. "Verify what you can. Come back if it's larger than it looks. Don't make it official until you have something that can't be explained away."

"And if it's already larger than it looks?"

"Then you come back, and we figure out the next step together."

Together. The word should have landed as reassurance. It landed as management — the careful inclusion that meant Luke was still treating this as something Ben needed supervision for, still framing the case as a problem to solve rather than a thing Ben had been handed directly by someone who had no one else to trust.

Ben knew he was being unfair. He knew Luke was right — the institutional logic was sound, the caution was warranted, the risk of scattering the trail before there was proof was real. He knew all of that, and he still wanted Luke to be wrong, because if Luke was right then Ben was just a junior investigator following a lead his father had approved, and the case wasn't personally meaningful in the way Ben already felt it was.

He didn't say any of that. He sat in the quiet of his quarters, Hapan sunlight angled low across the floor, the holoframe on his desk catching the edge of his vision and tugging at something he refused to look at directly.

"Verify quietly," he said. "Come back if it's larger."

"Yes."

"That's not a no."

Luke's expression didn't change, but something in the stillness shifted. "No," he said. "It's not a no."

Ben nodded. The movement was short, more a jerk of his chin than real agreement. He reached for the comm controls, and his hand was steady, and the steadiness felt like a lie he was telling both of them.

"I'll let you know what I find."

"Ben."

He stopped. His hand rested on the cutoff, not quite pressing.

"Be careful," Luke said. "Not just about the case."

The words hung in the air between them — the holo-image flickering, Luke's tired eyes holding Ben's across the relay lag and the institutional distance and the three years of careful normalcy that had never quite covered the shape of what came before. Ben heard what Luke wasn't saying. He heard it clearly.

He didn't answer. He cut the call.

The holo collapsed. The room was quiet again — the low hum of climate control, the distant sound of voices from the courtyard below, the angled light still painting its slow arc across the floor. Ben sat at the desk for a long moment, his hand still on the comm controls, the dead-channel alert still scrolling across the display in characters that belonged to a routing protocol the galaxy had agreed to forget.

Then he stood. He crossed to the closet and pulled his go-bag from the shelf — automatic movement, the old GAG reflex of packing light and fast and without asking why. A change of clothes. A datapad. The private holocomm, still keyed to his personal frequency. He didn't own a blaster. He'd stopped carrying one after the war.

The holoframe on the desk caught the light as he turned. His mother's face, frozen in a moment he'd chosen years ago and never changed. He didn't look at it. He'd learned not to look at it when he was about to do something she would have told him to think through twice.

He was going to Coruscant. He told himself it was because of Elya, because a woman who had trusted him enough to use a dead man's channel deserved someone who would show up. Because the fragment existed and someone needed to find it. Because Jacen's last orders still had two lives attached, and one of them might still be alive.

All of that was true. None of it was the whole reason.

The first thing in a long time that felt like it might matter. He hated that it did. He needed it to be real.

He zipped the go-bag closed and reached for his jacket. Hapan sunlight was fading toward evening, and the courtyard voices had gone quiet, and somewhere in the Coruscant night a records office was waiting with a body and a fragment and a trail that started exactly where Ben's old life had ended.

He was going, and Luke knew he was going. Neither of them had said it directly, and that was the shape of the permission Ben was carrying: not a blessing, not an order, just the precise gap in what his father had said.

The door clicked shut behind him.
