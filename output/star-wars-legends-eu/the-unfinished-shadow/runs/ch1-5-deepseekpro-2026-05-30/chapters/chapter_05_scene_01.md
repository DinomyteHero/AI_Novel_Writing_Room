The GA Justice records facility sat three blocks from the Bureau of Post-Conflict Logistics, in a building that had once been a banking headquarters and still wore its old money like a scent. High ceilings, polished stone, the sort of quiet that had to be paid for. Ben went through security with a visitor badge clipped to his collar, ALLIED PARTY stamped across it in orange, and tried not to notice that the guards looked at the badge before they looked at him.

Dorrin was waiting in a cross-reference room on the fourth floor. Windowless and cold, the air tasted of recycled oxygen and the faint electrical bite of holoprojectors on standby. Three walls were banked with display units, dark for now. A single terminal sat on a desk in the center, and beside it a chair Dorrin had clearly occupied for hours—his jacket hung over the back and a half-empty cup of caf cooled next to the datapad.

“You’re early,” Dorrin said.

“Traffic was light.”

Dorrin gave him a once-over that was not hostile, just measuring, and nodded toward the chair across from his own. Then he keyed the holoprojectors awake. Light flooded the room—personnel files, assignment logs, supervision records, all carrying the GAG header Ben hadn’t seen in three years and recognized in half a second.

“I pulled everything the Bureau had on Commander Rane’s old section,” Dorrin said. “Assignment logs, supervision records, transfer authorizations. The postwar prosecution files are cross-linked where the cases overlap.” He paused with one hand resting on the terminal. “This is going to take a while.”

“Then we should start.”

Dorrin looked at him, not unfriendly, just taking stock, and began scrolling.

The first hour was drudgery. Ben had known it would be. Investigation work was mostly sitting in rooms like this one, reading documents that did not want to yield, waiting for a name or a date or a routing code to catch the light. He’d learned that at fourteen, sitting in briefings he wasn’t supposed to be in, and the lesson had stuck.

Elya’s assignment logs were thorough. She’d run a tight section—fifteen personnel at its peak, handling records and routing for GAG’s logistics operations across three sectors. The logs showed transfers in and out, performance reviews that stayed polite and precise, a commendation from six months before the Battle of Shedu Maad that mentioned “exceptional attention to authorization integrity.”

Dorrin read the commendation aloud. “Authorization integrity. That’s the sort of phrase that gets people killed later.”

“Or promoted.”

“Same thing in GAG.”

Ben didn’t argue. He was watching the personnel list scroll past, names and dates and transfer codes, and his eyes caught a pattern before his brain finished catching up. A cluster of transfers out of Elya’s section, all within a three-month window, all carrying the same authorization signature.

“Go back,” he said.

Dorrin stopped. “Where?”

“Three entries up. The transfer authorization—that’s Maro Tess’s signature.”

Dorrin pulled the record. The authorization was clean, properly filed, every code where it belonged: Colonel Maro Tess, reassigning personnel out of field duty and into records and logistics, eighteen months before the war ended.

“You know Tess?” Dorrin asked.

“By reputation.” Ben kept his voice level. “He was GAG operations. Senior. The type of officer who didn’t leave a paper trail unless he wanted one.”

“This is a paper trail.”

“This is him wanting one.”

Dorrin absorbed that without comment. He pulled the next record, and the next, and then stopped. His hand hovered over the display, and Ben saw the name a beat before Dorrin spoke it.

“Talon Veyr. Reassigned from field operations to Commander Rane’s records section under Tess’s authorization.” Dorrin tapped the screen and brought up the supervision file. “He worked under Elya for eighteen months. Performance reviews are solid—above average for logistics work, notes about aptitude for pattern recognition in routing documentation.”

Ben leaned forward. “Pull his prosecution file.”

“I was getting there.”

The file opened in the air between them: a GA Justice conviction record, two years old. Talon Veyr, charged with fraud, unauthorized routing, and obstruction. The manifest patterns in the evidence summary were close to what Ben had seen on Elya’s damaged chip. Cloned shipment identity. Quarantine disguise protocol. Off-book authorization chain.

“It’s the same pattern,” Ben said.

“It’s a similar pattern. That’s close enough.”

“Close enough that he’ll recognize what’s on the fragment.”

Dorrin didn’t answer at once. He was reading the conviction record, his eyes moving down the screen with the same quick, exact rhythm Ben had noticed in Elya’s office. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quieter.

“His transfer-chain conviction—the one that put him in Tarn Velos—it involved manifest authorization patterns that match the audit trace on your fragment. Not exact, but close.” He straightened from the terminal. “He worked under Elya. He was convicted for the same kind of fraud the fragment represents. If anyone can read what’s on that chip, it’s him.”

“He’s not a decoder,” Ben said. “He’s not going to break the encryption. But he knows the records environment. He knows what the authorization chain looks like from the inside. He can recognize the pattern.”

“That still makes him a witness.”

“That makes him our only witness.”

Dorrin let the silence settle. The holoprojectors hummed, the displays shifting as the system cycled its refresh. Ben could feel the Inspector watching him, not the way the guards at the checkpoint had, but something closer to professional recalibration. A man who had just realized the Jedi in his records room knew more about GAG authorization chains than he’d said.

“You read those patterns faster than I do,” Dorrin said.

It wasn’t a question.

Ben met his eyes. “I was in GAG.”

“I know. You were fourteen.”

“That didn’t stop them from teaching me how to read a manifest.”

Dorrin held his gaze another moment. Then he nodded once and turned back to the terminal. The moment passed without being settled, and Ben knew it hadn’t passed at all. Dorrin was filing it away, adding it to whatever private record he was building, and the next time Ben showed him something he shouldn’t know, the silence would be heavier.

“Tarn Velos is a GA Justice facility,” Dorrin said. “To get access to an inmate, I have to file a formal visitor request. Investigative interview, material witness designation. The request goes through the same system that flagged his original conviction.”

“Which means it generates notices.”

“Which means anyone watching the old GAG case files will know someone’s asking questions about Talon Veyr.”

Ben heard what Dorrin was telling him. The legal route was the correct route—the only route that didn’t involve breaking a convicted fraud offender out of a coastal detention works. It was also the route that would wake old alert systems, the kind set up during the war and never fully decommissioned, the kind somebody with access and interest could still be monitoring.

Filing the request would announce that the case existed. It would warn whoever had killed Elya that someone was following the trail.

“File the request,” Ben said.

Dorrin looked at him. “You understand what that means.”

“I understand that we need someone who can read the fragment, and Talon Veyr is the only name we have. If filing the request wakes something up, it wakes something up. We’ll deal with it.”

The Inspector didn’t argue. He turned to the terminal and began typing, fingers quick and exact, and Ben watched the request take shape on the screen: a formal GA Justice document, every field in its place, the kind of paperwork that moved through systems with the weight of law behind it.

Ben’s eyes tracked the authorization codes as they appeared. He caught the routing marker before Dorrin finished entering it, recognized the transfer protocol from the old GAG logistics chain, and said nothing. Dorrin’s hands paused for half a beat—a hesitation so small Ben almost missed it—then kept going.

Neither of them spoke.

The request filed with a soft chime. Somewhere in the GA Justice system, a notification flag tripped. Somewhere else, in a system Ben couldn’t see and didn’t know to look for, an alert lit up a board—a visitor query for Talon Veyr, filed by Inspector Cale Dorrin, cross-referenced to a sealed evidence record from the Bureau of Post-Conflict Logistics.

Ben didn’t know that yet. He stood in the cold fluorescent light of the cross-reference room, the fragment sealed in an evidence sleeve on the desk between them, and felt the clean relief of having done the right thing, almost unfamiliar.

“Thank you,” he said.

Dorrin ejected a datachip from the terminal and handed it across. “That’s a copy of the supervision records and the prosecution file. The visitor request will take a few hours to process. You should be cleared for Tarn Velos by the time you arrive.”

“You’re not coming?”

“I have a crime scene to close and a dead woman’s next of kin to notify. You can interview a prisoner without me.”

Ben took the chip. He collected the evidence sleeve with the fragment, then unclipped the visitor badge from his collar and set it on the desk. The orange ALLIED PARTY lettering caught the holoprojector light and held it.

“There’s a shuttle terminal three blocks east,” Dorrin said. “Direct route to Tarn Velos, leaves every two hours. You’ll make the next one if you walk.”

Ben nodded. He didn’t thank Dorrin again—the Inspector had already filed the thanks under professional obligation and moved on. That was fair. That was how investigations worked.

He walked out of the cross-reference room and down the corridor, past the security checkpoint where the guards were already changing shifts, and out into the Coruscant afternoon. The sky was the color of old durasteel, the traffic a constant roar three hundred levels up, and the shuttle terminal sat at the end of the block with its departure board cycling through destinations in clean blue light.

Ben bought a ticket for Tarn Velos. He boarded the shuttle with the fragment in his go-bag and the datachip in his pocket and the clean feeling still settled in his chest, a specific relief that he was doing everything correctly, following the rules, using the system the way it was supposed to be used.

The shuttle lifted off. The departure board cycled to the next destination.

And somewhere, on a board Ben couldn’t see, the alert his visitor request had triggered was already being read.
