The chrono on the wall read 0233. Commander Elya Rane had been staring at the two versions of the same wartime shipment manifest for eighteen minutes, and her body knew something was off before her mind caught up.

The public record sat on the left side of her terminal, a routine quarantine/survey shipment logged four days before the Battle of Shedu Maad, pushed through standard GAG logistics channels, flagged as medical cargo bound for a field hospital in the Hapes Cluster. Boring. Complete. The sort of record that sat untouched in the Bureau of Post-Conflict Logistics for three years because no one had a reason to open it twice.

The hidden duplicate sat on the right. Same shipment identity. Same quarantine/survey classification. Same departure timestamp. Different destination.

Elya had found it by accident, in a routine records reconciliation that had thrown a mismatch in the transfer-chain metadata. The sort of clerical ghost that usually meant a data-entry error, a misrouted authorization, tired fingers at 0300 during the war’s last week. She had opened a ticket. She had poured herself a cup of caf. She had expected to spend twenty minutes cleaning up someone else’s sloppy filing and then go home.

That was three days ago.

Now she sat alone in the third-floor records office with five dark workstations behind her, the climate control humming its steady midnight note, and looked at the personal encryption marker attached to the hidden duplicate. The marker was Jacen Solo’s. Not GAG-standard. Not departmental. His. The metadata showed the encryption had been applied personally, four days before Shedu Maad, using a cipher key that had been decommissioned with the rest of the Galactic Alliance Guard after the battle.

One of Jacen’s last orders had two lives. A public one that went to a field hospital in the Hapes Cluster. And a hidden one that went somewhere else.

Elya’s hand moved to her caf cup before she told it to. The liquid was cold. She drank it anyway, tasting old plasticrete and heat-stressed electronics in the recycled air, and let her eyes track across the destination field on the hidden duplicate for the dozenth time.

Iresh.

She had never heard of it.

Six years in Post-Conflict Logistics had put manifests from every theater of the war, every supply line, every medical evacuation route under her hands. She knew the names of worlds that never appeared on civilian starcharts, the way-stations and transshipment points and emergency landing zones that existed only in military routing tables. She had never seen Iresh on any of them.

The tightening behind her sternum was not panic. It was the earlier thing, the thing that came before you decided you had already decided. She had felt it once before, three years ago, sitting at a different terminal in a different office, staring at an approval field with Ben Skywalker’s name in it. Fourteen years old. Logged through GAG personnel routing as an active field asset. Someone had filed him like a piece of equipment.

She had moved his name out of that field at 0300 without authorization because she could not bear one more child being turned into a clean bureaucratic answer.

The same tightening now. The same certainty that if she did nothing, the system would do what systems did — absorb the anomaly, smooth the edges, file the duplicate away in a dead personnel record and wait for the retention timer to expire.

She reached for her terminal and began to compose.

The GAG dead-drop protocol was obsolete. It should have been decommissioned with the rest of the Guard’s operational infrastructure after Shedu Maad. But Elya had trained on it during the war, and muscle memory did not care about decommission dates. She built the message in layers — an identity-address tied to Ben Skywalker’s old service marker, a routing path that bounced through three inactive transfer nodes, an encryption wrapper that would read as junk data to anyone who didn’t know which GAG cipher variant she was using.

The message itself was four lines.

*GAG is still active. One of Jacen’s last orders has two lives. Come alone. — E.R.*

She read it twice. She did not add *please*. She did not add *I’m sorry*. She did not add *I moved your name once and I’m trying to make it matter one more time*. Ben Skywalker was a Jedi Knight now, not a fourteen-year-old in the wrong approval field. He would understand what the message meant or he wouldn’t. Either way, she was out of time to explain.

She sent it.

The terminal chimed once — a clean, procedural note. Transmission complete. The message was now in the dead-drop system, waiting for Ben’s service marker to ping the retrieval node. He might never check it. The marker was three years cold. But Elya had worked records long enough to know that people who survived the GAG never really stopped checking the old channels. Paranoia was a professional qualification.

She closed the dead-drop interface and turned to the manifest fragment.

The hidden duplicate was too large to send through the dead-drop — the metadata alone would have tripped every monitoring algorithm still running on the old GAG routing layers. She could not copy it to a datapad; the Bureau’s security protocols logged every external storage access, and she had already spent three days running queries that would look suspicious in hindsight. She could not leave it on her terminal; whoever had sealed this record would check there first.

Her records instinct chose before her conscious mind caught up.

The office had six workstations. Five were assigned to officers who had transferred out, retired, or been downsized in the post-war budget consolidations. Their terminals were dark. Their access permissions were revoked. But the physical workstations remained — durasteel frames, composite paneling, the standard Bureau-issue structural casing that ran along the underside of each desk to shield the power couplings from dust and spilled caf.

Elya knelt beside the workstation nearest the door. The casing was sealed with a standard maintenance latch, not security-rated, just meant to keep cleaning staff from accidentally unplugging anything. She worked it open with her fingernails, feeling the grit of three years of accumulated dust, and found the gap between the power coupling housing and the inner frame. Maybe four centimeters wide. Deep enough for a standard data chip.

She pulled the chip from her terminal. The manifest fragment was the only thing on it — she had purged her personal files yesterday, not letting herself think about why. She pressed the chip into the gap, felt it seat against the inner frame, and closed the casing.

The latch clicked. The dust on the panel was undisturbed except for a single fingerprint she wiped away with her sleeve.

She stood up. Her hands were moving on muscle memory, her brain a half step behind them — the familiar feeling of making a records correction before anyone could call it a choice. She had done this before: not hiding things from killers, but fixing things before someone noticed they were broken. Same procedural rhythm. Same quiet refusal to wait for permission.

The chrono read 0247.

She walked back to her terminal and sat down. She had not decided what she was waiting for — a response from Ben, a security sweep, the morning shift arriving in four hours — when the door-seal clicked.

Elya looked up.

A man was standing between her and the corridor. He had not been there a heartbeat ago. She had heard no footsteps, no turbolift chime, no voice in the hallway. Just the click of the seal and the sudden fact of him — medium height, dark hair, a face younger than she expected and older than it should have been, and a stillness that felt wrong in a way she could not name.

He wore civilian clothes. No badge. No uniform. No weapon she could see.

The absence of footsteps was worse than a shout would have been.

“Commander Rane,” he said.

His voice was quiet. Not a whisper — the voice of a man who had learned he never needed to raise it to be heard.

Elya’s hands were on her lap, below the desk, where he could not see them. She did not reach for the terminal. She did not try to run. The office had one door, and he was standing in it, and the windows were three floors up and looked out on nothing but the interior walls of the Bureau’s central processing shaft.

“I don’t believe we’ve met,” she said. Her voice came out steady. The procedural part of her brain — the part that had survived six years of post-conflict bureaucracy by never letting anyone see her flinch — was already cataloguing exits she knew did not exist.

“No,” the man agreed. “We haven’t.”

He did not introduce himself. He did not explain why he was there. He stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides and looked at her terminal, and Elya understood with a clarity that felt like cold water down her spine that he was not a colleague, not a security sweep, not someone she could talk past.

She kept her hands visible. She kept her voice level. She thought in fields, not bodies — keep it bureaucratic, keep it abstracted, buy one more second by staying in the vocabulary of records and not the vocabulary of dying.

“Is there something I can help you with? The office is closed until 0700, but if you have a records request—”

“You found the duplicate manifest,” he said. “Three days ago. You sent a message tonight. To whom?”

The question was calm. The calm was worse than a threat would have been.

Elya’s mouth opened. She had a procedural answer ready — something about data classification, about proper channels, about the Bureau’s policy on unauthorized access inquiries. The words were there, lined up in her head like a queue of transfer requests waiting for approval.

Then the pressure came.

It was not physical. It was not pain — not yet. It was the sensation of someone opening a door inside her mind and walking through it without knocking. An intrusion so precise, so intimate, that her body tried to recoil before her brain understood what was happening.

She had heard stories about what Jedi could do. She had processed the records of interrogations conducted during the war, the dry bureaucratic language that described techniques she had never expected to experience. The reports had used words like *extraction* and *compliance* and *information recovery*. They had not mentioned the feeling of someone else’s attention moving through her memories like a hand sorting through files.

*Think in fields*, she told herself. *Keep it bureaucratic. Keep it abstracted. He’s reading your terminal, not your mind. He’s reading your terminal, not your—*

“Ben Skywalker,” the man said.

The name landed in the quiet office like a datachip hitting a durasteel floor.

“You sent the message to Ben Skywalker. His old GAG service marker. You used the dead-drop protocol.” The man’s voice was still quiet, still calm, but something in it had sharpened — not anger, but focus. The attention of someone who had found what he was looking for. “You told him one of Jacen’s last orders had two lives.”

Elya’s hands were gripping the arms of her chair. She did not remember moving them.

“And you preserved something,” the man continued. “A fragment of the manifest. You didn’t send it with the message. You hid it.” His head tilted slightly, the first movement she had seen him make that was not deliberate stillness. “Where?”

She did not answer. She did not think the word *casing* or *power coupling* or *dust*. She thought about the chrono on the wall — 0251 now, the digits advancing with the same steady rhythm they had kept for six years of late-night shifts and routine reconciliations and quiet choices made at 0300 while everyone else was asleep. She thought about the hum of the climate control. She thought about the taste of cold caf.

The pressure increased.

It was not pain. Pain would have been easier to resist. This was something else — the sensation of her own memories being turned over, examined, sorted into piles she could not see. The dead-drop message. Ben’s name. The moment she had knelt beside the workstation and worked the casing open with her fingernails. The gap between the power coupling and the frame.

“Iresh,” the man said.

The word was soft. Almost reverent.

“The duplicate manifest points toward Iresh. You found the destination. You sent it to Ben Skywalker.” He was silent for a moment, and when he spoke again his voice was lower still — not whispering, but close. “You moved his name once before. Three years ago. An approval field. You removed him from it because he was fourteen.”

Elya’s breath caught. She had not thought about that. She had been careful not to think about that. But the pressure was inside her memories now, sorting through the old choices like a clerk reviewing archived files, and she could feel him finding the moment she had sat at a different terminal in a different office and decided that one child was enough.

“You wanted it to matter,” the man said. “The old choice. You wanted it to mean something one more time.”

She did not answer. She could not answer. The pressure was everywhere now, filling the spaces between her thoughts, and she was losing the ability to separate what she was choosing to remember from what he was taking.

*Think in fields*, she told herself again, but the words were slipping. *Think in fields. Not bodies. Fields. Records. Transfer chains. Proper channels. The Bureau’s policy on—*

The pressure pushed deeper.

She felt something give.

It was not physical. It was the sensation of a door she had been holding shut finally swinging open, and everything behind it spilling out at once — the manifest, the encryption marker, the dead-drop routing path, Ben’s service marker, the gap in the casing, the dust on her fingers, the chrono reading 0233 and then 0247 and then 0251 and then—

The man was standing closer now. She had not seen him move.

“The fragment,” he said. “Where?”

Elya’s lips formed words. She did not choose them. They came out of her mouth like data scrolling across a terminal screen, automatic and unstoppable.

“Workstation,” she heard herself say. “Near the door. Power coupling housing. The gap behind the inner frame.”

The pressure did not ease. It kept moving through her, sorting and cataloguing, and she understood with a clarity that was worse than fear that he was not going to stop. He was going to take everything she had — every memory, every choice, every quiet correction made at 0300 — and she was going to keep giving it to him because her body had forgotten how to refuse.

The chrono read 0257.

She thought about the dead-drop message, already gone, already beyond his reach. She thought about Ben Skywalker’s service marker, waiting in the old routing nodes for a ping that might never come. She thought about the fragment in the casing, hidden by instinct rather than procedure, and whether he would find it now that she had told him where to look.

She thought about the wrong approval field, three years ago, and the fourteen-year-old boy whose name she had moved out of it because someone had to.

The pressure pushed past what her body could hold.

Elya Rane died at 0300, sitting at her terminal in a narrow internal office with five dark workstations behind her and a man she had never met standing between her and the door. She died as herself — practical, tense, and briefly human. She did not deliver a final speech. She did not name her fear. She died the way she had worked: quietly, competently, with her hands still gripping the arms of her chair and her eyes still fixed on a terminal that showed nothing but the public version of a wartime shipment manifest, routine and boring and complete.

The man stood over her for a long moment. Then he turned to the workstation near the door.

The casing came open easily — a standard maintenance latch, not security-rated. He found the gap between the power coupling housing and the inner frame. He found the data chip.

He also found the dust.

The chip was there, exactly where she had said it would be. But when he slotted it into the terminal and called up the manifest fragment, the data was damaged. Not encrypted — physically degraded, the storage medium compromised by heat from the power coupling it had been pressed against. The fragment was partial. Garbled in places. Enough to confirm the Iresh destination, enough to confirm Jacen Solo’s personal encryption marker, but not enough to reconstruct the full transfer chain.

The woman had hidden the chip in a gap she knew would damage it. Whether by instinct or intent, she had chosen a hiding place that would preserve the fragment’s existence while destroying its contents.

The man stood in the quiet office for a long moment, the damaged chip in his hand and the dead woman at her terminal and the chrono on the wall advancing past 0300 into the empty hours before morning.

Then he left.

The door-seal clicked behind him. The climate control hummed its steady midnight note. The five dark workstations sat silent and undisturbed. And in the gap behind the power coupling housing, a smear of dust marked the place where a records officer had made one last correction before anyone could call it a choice.
