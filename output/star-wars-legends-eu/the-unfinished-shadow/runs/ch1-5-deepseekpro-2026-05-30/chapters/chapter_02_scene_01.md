The morning training salle at Shedu Maad caught light the way Hapan architecture always did: cool, indirect, spilling from high windows cut into pale stone. It made everyone look a little more composed than they felt. Ben Skywalker had been living in the complex for two years and still noticed the light. On Coruscant, light came from wherever you pointed it. Here, it arrived like a diplomatic courtesy.

He worked through the second half of his solo drills with the economy of someone who had been doing them since he was old enough to hold a lightsaber. The blade hummed its green note, steady and familiar, and his body moved through the sequences without asking his brain for permission. That was the point of drills. It was also the problem.

Two younger Jedi were sparring at the far end of the salle: a Twi'lek woman Ben knew by sight and a human man whose name he had learned twice and forgotten twice. Their rhythm was good, competitive without being reckless, and the talk between passes had the easy shorthand of people who had trained together long enough to stop being polite. Ben caught a fragment, something about a supply requisition and someone's questionable taste in starfighter modifications, and registered the tone more than the content. Included, without being invited in. The room's background noise, warm and indifferent.

He shut down his blade and wiped sweat from his forehead with the back of his wrist. The recycled air in the salle tasted faintly of stone dust and the ozone tang of recent saber work. Through the high windows, the Hapan morning was a pale wash of gold and grey. Shedu Maad's star was smaller than Coruscant's, or farther away; Ben had never bothered to check which.

"Your footwork's still compensating for a blade half a meter shorter than the one you're holding."

Ben turned. Amelia Solo leaned in the salle doorway, arms crossed, one shoulder against the stone frame. She wore civilian clothes, a fitted tunic in deep blue, Hapan-cut, too fine for training-salle dust, and her expression had the warm amusement of a cousin who had already decided to tease him.

"I'm not compensating," Ben said. "I'm adapting."

"That's what compensating means."

"It means I'm doing it on purpose."

Amelia's mouth twitched. She pushed off the doorframe and walked into the salle, her boots quiet on the stone. The Hapan guards at the corridor junction, two of them in palace-house livery, polite and absolute, did not follow her in. They never did. Amelia moved through Shedu Maad's security protocols the way Ben moved through saber drills: without thinking, because she'd grown up inside them.

"I thought you were on Coruscant this week," Ben said.

"Change of plans. Mother had a thing." The vagueness was practiced, as all of Amelia's vagueness was practiced: not evasive, just comfortable. The ease of someone who had learned early that some questions were better left alone. "I'm here until end of cycle. Thought I'd see if you were still doing that thing where you train alone instead of asking anyone to spar."

"I like training alone."

"You like not having to explain why you're better than everyone your age and don't want to talk about it."

Ben clipped his lightsaber to his belt. "That's a very specific diagnosis."

"I've had years of practice." Amelia's smile stayed warm, but it was the warmth of someone who knew she was right and was being generous about it. "Come on. I'm hungry and the refectory's serving something that might be eggs."

---

The refectory ran along the complex's southern edge, a long room of pale stone and high ceilings where the morning light came in at an angle that made the tables look like they belonged in a painting. The room was half-full: Jedi, support staff, and a scattering of Hapan administrative personnel who had learned to eat at the same hours as the Order, whether from efficiency or diplomatic habit. The noise sat at the low, pleasant level of a community that had not yet remembered what it was supposed to worry about today.

Ben collected a tray and found a seat at one of the long tables. Amelia sat across from him, already peeling a piece of fruit with the focused efficiency of someone raised to eat quickly between obligations.

The eggs — if they were eggs — were pale yellow and tasted faintly of spice. Ben ate without paying much attention to the food. His attention was on the room, as it always was: the exits, the sightlines, the conversational rhythms. He didn't mean to do it. The habit had installed itself during a year he didn't like thinking about and had never bothered to uninstall.

"Kenth Dorvis asked about you yesterday," Amelia said around a mouthful of fruit. "Wanted to know if you were taking any missions."

"Dorvis doesn't ask about me. Dorvis asks about my father and uses my name as a polite opening."

"That's a little unfair."

"Dorvis asked me once if I'd ever considered a diplomatic track. He said it with the tone people use when they're trying to find a safe job for someone they think might break."

Amelia's expression flickered — not quite a wince, but close. "He means well."

"Everyone here means well." Ben took another bite of eggs. "That's the problem."

He hadn't meant to say it. The words came out before he could decide whether they were true, and once they were in the air between them he couldn't take them back. Amelia looked at him for a beat longer than comfortable, her fruit forgotten in her hand, and Ben saw the quick assessment behind her eyes — whether this was a conversation he wanted to have or a door he had opened by accident.

She chose wrong. Or right, depending on how you measured it.

"Ben—"

"Skywalker!"

The voice came from further down the table — a young Jedi Knight named Tylo Vann, broad-shouldered and generally well-meaning, the sort of person who filled silences because silences made him nervous. He was leaning forward on his elbows, halfway through a story Ben had not been following, and his face had the bright, open expression of someone about to say something he thought was harmless.

"You did a tour with the GAG, right? During the war? I was telling Risha about the old fleet deployments and I couldn't remember whether you were attached to the *Anakin Solo* or—"

The temperature in the room dropped.

No one gasped. No one turned to stare. But the conversational rhythm around their table stumbled — a beat too long, a subject change too quick — and suddenly the people nearest Tylo Vann were very interested in their breakfasts. Risha, the Twi'lek from the training salle, found something fascinating in her caf. The human man beside her developed a sudden need to check his datapad.

Tylo realized what he'd done a heartbeat late. His face went through three expressions in quick succession — confusion, recognition, horror — and then settled into the careful blankness of someone who knew he'd stepped in something and was trying not to make it worse by moving.

"I didn't mean—" he started.

"It's fine," Ben said.

His voice came out steady. Pleasant, even. The voice of someone who had been asked an ordinary question and was giving an ordinary answer. He'd practiced it for years, and it still worked, and he still hated how well it worked.

"I was attached to the *Anakin Solo*," he said. "For most of it. Logistics and reconnaissance."

The words were true and empty. They gave Tylo something to nod at, which he did, with the too-quick relief of someone being offered an exit. "Right. Right, of course. Logistics. I should have remembered."

He shouldn't have remembered. He shouldn't have asked. But the moment was already closing, the conversational wound already sealing over, and Ben could feel the room's collective decision to move on like a shift in air pressure. Someone at the next table laughed at something. Someone else asked for the salt. The background hum resumed its easy rhythm, and Ben was left sitting in the silence that followed his own answer, jaw tight, eyes fixed on a point just past Tylo's shoulder where the morning light was doing something uncomplicated against the stone.

Amelia hadn't looked away. She was still watching him with an expression too careful to be pity and too knowing to be comfort.

"You do that a lot," she said quietly.

"Do what?"

"Make it easy for them."

Ben picked up his fork. The eggs had gone cold. "They're trying to be kind. The least I can do is let them."

He ate the rest of his breakfast without tasting it. Amelia let the silence stand, which was either the kindest thing she could have done or the most Hapan, and Ben wasn't sure there was a difference.

---

His quarters were on the third level of the residential wing, a narrow room with a high window and a bed that was a little too short for him. He had never asked for a different one. The quarters came with the rank, and the rank came with the name, and asking for something better would have felt like admitting he noticed the difference.

He closed the door behind him and stood for a moment in the quiet. The recycled air tasted of stone and the faint mechanical sweetness of the climate control. Through the window, the Hapan morning had brightened into something more assertive — gold light on pale stone, the distant sound of voices from the courtyard below. Normal sounds. Ordinary morning sounds. The sort that were supposed to mean you belonged somewhere.

His private holocomm was on the desk where he had left it, a compact unit keyed to his personal frequency. The Jedi comm channels routed through the Order's secure network — standard protocol, monitored for priority traffic, the system that flagged messages by clearance level and sender identity. Ben's private unit was not on that network. It was an old model, civilian-grade, the sort of thing you bought when you wanted a line that didn't go through the Order's routing nodes.

He had bought it six years ago. He had never replaced it.

The alert came when he was halfway across the room, and it wasn't the standard chime. It was a degraded-channel flag — a burst of static and a low, repeating tone that Ben hadn't heard in three years and had never expected to hear again. The sound hit him before the recognition did, a cold drop in his stomach that arrived a full second ahead of his brain.

He stopped moving.

The holocomm was three paces away. The alert was still firing, the degraded-channel flag scrolling across the small display in characters that belonged to a routing protocol the GAG had decommissioned at the end of the war. The signal wasn't coming through Jedi comms. It wasn't coming through any channel the Order monitored. It was coming through old GAG infrastructure — the dead-drop network, the identity-address system tied to former service markers, the ghost of a logistics chain that should have been purged from the HoloNet years ago.

Someone still had the routing.

Ben crossed the room in four steps and sat down at the desk. His hands were steady. His breathing was steady. The part of his brain that had learned to stay calm under pressure was already cataloguing the details — degraded channel, obsolete protocol, routing through standard infrastructure rather than Jedi comms — and filing them in the drawer marked deal with this later. The rest of his brain was still catching up to what his body already knew.

A dead channel didn't fire by accident. Someone had sent a message to his old GAG service marker. Someone who knew it existed. Someone who knew he would recognize the protocol.

He opened the message.

The text was short. Plain. The sort of message you sent when you didn't know how much time you had.

*Ben —*

*GAG is still active. One of Jacen's last orders has two lives and I can't stop it alone. Come to Coruscant. Come alone. Use the old routing if you need to reach me.*

*E.R.*

Ben read it twice. Then a third time. The words didn't change.

GAG was still active. Not the public record — the public record said the Galactic Alliance Guard had been disbanded at the end of the war, its personnel scattered into other services, its operations terminated by executive order. The public record was wrong. Someone had kept a piece of it running, and that piece had two lives attached to it, and a woman named E.R. was asking him to come alone.

His hand moved before he registered the movement.

It covered the sender code on the display — palm flat against the screen, blocking the characters from view. The motion was quick and automatic, the old cover-your-tracks reflex, GAG-shaped and still running after three years of peace. He noticed it a moment later, his hand pressed against the holocomm like he was hiding evidence from someone who wasn't in the room, and he didn't remove it.

He sat there, palm against the sender code, and let the stillness settle around him.

The message was real. It was specific. It had come through a channel that should not exist from someone who had used his old service marker to find him, and the woman who sent it was in danger — probably already dead, if Ben was being honest with himself, because people who used dead-drop protocols to send messages like this usually didn't survive long enough to send a second one. And she had asked for him. Not the Order. Not the Jedi. Him.

The first thing in months that felt like it mattered.

He hated that it did. He needed it to be real.

The holocomm's display flickered under his palm. The sender code was still there — a string of characters he didn't recognize, a routing path that would take time to trace, a name reduced to initials. E.R. He didn't know an E.R. He had never served with anyone who used those initials. But she knew his service marker, and she knew the dead-drop protocol, and she had trusted him enough to send a message that could get her killed.

Or she had no one else to send it to.

Ben lifted his hand from the display. The sender code was still there, waiting. He looked at it for a long moment, and then he reached for the comm controls and keyed in a frequency he knew by heart.

Not the Order's channel. Not Saba's. His father's.

The case was personal before it was institutional. He had decided that before he knew he was deciding it, and now the decision was made, and the ease he'd been trying to build at Shedu Maad — the careful mornings and the pleasant answers and the practiced move of making it easy for everyone else — was already over.

The call connected. The holo flickered, resolving into the familiar blue-white glow of a waiting channel, and Ben sat in the quiet of his quarters with the dead-channel alert still scrolling across his display and the weight of old GAG history settling back onto his shoulders like a coat he had never quite learned to take off.
