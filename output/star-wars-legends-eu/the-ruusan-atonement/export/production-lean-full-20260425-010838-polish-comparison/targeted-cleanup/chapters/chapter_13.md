# Chapter 13

## Scene 13.01

Kolven filled the viewport like a world that had never learned how to be dangerous. Green spread across the northern continent in broad, easy sweeps, cut by blue rivers flashing in the sunlight. Thin white cloud sat over it all, the mild, scattered kind that promised a clean landing and a decent forecast. Ben had seen a hundred worlds from orbit just like this—agricultural colonies, retirement settlements, places where a broken irrigation pump was front-page news.

Desh’s voice came from the cockpit station behind him, flat and stripped of expression.

“Surface temperature readings don’t match the visual spectrum.”

Ben didn’t turn. “How far off?”

“Four degrees at the equator. Eleven at the poles. The north continent should be in late-autumn cooling based on axial tilt. Sensors say it’s twenty-two standard and climbing.” A beat. “The vegetation’s absorbing the wrong wavelengths. Whatever’s green down there isn’t doing photosynthesis the way it should.”

Sera moved up beside Ben’s shoulder. She’d been quiet since Maetha’s, and that silence had weight, as if she’d taken something apart and disliked what she’d found inside. “Show me the settlement.”

Desh brought up the secondary display. Kolven’s main population center sat where two rivers met, a grid of streets laid out with pre-Ruusan efficiency, buildings still standing, roofs intact. From orbit it looked inhabited. Lived in. The kind of place where someone ought to be hanging laundry or yelling at a droid or cursing at a moisture vaporator.

“There’s nobody down there,” Ben said. It wasn’t a question.

“No comm traffic. No power signatures. No heat blooms from the residential grid.” Desh’s fingers moved across his board. “The infrastructure’s intact. It just isn’t running.”

Kael spoke from the back of the cockpit, voice dry and easy. “The birds?”

Desh glanced at his scan. “No avian biosigns. No animal movement at all above ground level. Insect populations are present, but the distribution is—” He stopped. “The distribution is wrong. They’re clustered in shapes that don’t match any normal foraging pattern.”

Ben felt tightness settle in his chest. The same tightness he’d felt at Maetha’s before the spike, the same warning his body had learned to give him before his mind caught up. Something here was broken, and it wanted his attention now.

“Take us down,” he said. “Landing zone at the settlement edge. We do this on the ground.”

---

The ramp lowered onto grass that bent the wrong way.

Ben saw it before his boot touched down—a patch of groundcover leaning east while the wind moved west, stalks curving against their own grain as if the air had weight but no direction. He stepped off anyway. The grass crunched under his heel with the sound of dead leaves, not living stems.

The air tasted flat. Not stale—the oxygen content was fine, the pressure was fine, every reading Desh had called out on approach sat within tolerance—but flat, the way water tasted after it had been boiled too long. Ben’s lungs took it in and his body rejected it in the same breath.

Sera came down behind him. Her boots hit the grass and she stopped, one hand lifting a fraction before she forced it back to her side. Her face gave away nothing, but Ben had learned to read her stillness the way he read Desh’s silences. She was braced.

Torin stepped off the ramp last. He walked three paces past the group and stopped with his back to them, facing the settlement. His shoulders were loose. His head tilted the same way it had at Maetha’s, that listening angle that had once made Ben want a wall between them.

Ben reached for the Force.

The reach was automatic—a decade of training made reflex, the thing he did the way other people blinked or breathed. His awareness opened the way it always did, searching for the familiar armature of living presence, the warm pull of the light, the cold warning of the dark, the thousand small cues that told a Jedi where to step, when to stop, and what waited around the corner.

It found nothing.

Not emptiness. The Force was there—he could feel it, a vast reservoir of energy and presence and potential pressing against him—but it had no shape. No direction. No axis he could use to orient himself. The ground of his sense simply wasn’t there, and his reach fell through the absence like a foot through a stair that had looked solid.

His balance lurched. He widened his stance by reflex, boots grinding into the wrong-bending grass, and for one long second he was eight years old in the Temple training halls again, reaching for the remote and finding only the sick drop that came when the Force slipped out of his grasp.

Except the Force hadn’t slipped.

It was right there. It was everywhere. It just had no structure his mind could hold.

“Ben.” Sera’s voice came from somewhere to his left. “What do you feel?”

He heard the question and knew she already knew the answer. She’d reached too. She’d found the same dead end.

“Presence,” he said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears—steady, but thin, the way a man sounded when he was concentrating on not falling. “Energy. No structure. No light or dark.”

“No axis,” Sera said quietly.

“No axis.”

Torin turned. His face was calm in a way that made Ben’s gut tighten before his brain could name why—not calm because he was managing fear, but calm as if he’d come home to a silence he’d been missing. “The Weave is present but unformed. Ankhural described this state in the third codex. The raw potential before the lattice imposes order.”

“Ankhural was wrong,” Sera said. Her voice had gone hard. “The lattice doesn’t impose anything. It is the Force’s own structure. This—” She gestured at the settlement, the grass, the flat sky. “This is damage.”

Kael hadn’t moved from the base of the ramp. He stood with his arms folded, weight back on his heels, face blank in the way that meant he was counting threats. “The non-Force-sensitive is about to say something none of you want to hear.”

Desh pulled his chrono off his wrist.

He didn’t speak. He held the chrono flat in his palm and watched the second hand. Ben watched too—the small red needle that should have been ticking forward in steady, predictable increments. It jumped. Two seconds forward, one back. Then a pause that lasted three heartbeats. Then four seconds in a blur.

Desh’s jaw tightened. He put the chrono back on his wrist and looked at Ben.

“Time’s not stable here,” he said. “The drift is measurable. I’m getting half a second variance per minute at minimum, and it’s accelerating closer to the settlement center.” He paused. “The plants are growing wrong because time is moving wrong. They’re not sick. They’re confused.”

Ben looked at the grass bending against the wind. At the settlement buildings standing intact and empty. At the sky that looked temperate and felt like nothing at all.

“Veraine did this,” he said. “This wasn’t the seal failing. This was a test.”

Sera nodded once. “She unwound a settlement world to prove she could. Twelve thousand people lived here.” Her voice stayed level, but Ben heard the weight under it—the weight of someone watching history repeat itself and knowing exactly how many bodies the first run had cost. “She counted them and proceeded.”

The silence after that was different from the silence of the unwoven Force. This was a human silence—five people standing on dead grass, each sealed inside his or her own perception, with nothing shared to hold them together. Ben couldn’t feel Sera in the Force. He couldn’t feel Torin, couldn’t feel the warm-jagged hum that had been a warning and a comfort at once. He couldn’t feel Kael at all—Kael had always been a tight knot of efficiency at the edge of Ben’s awareness, and now there was nothing.

He could see them. He could hear them breathe. But the thing that had let him know them—the thing that had let him trust his read on Torin’s calm or Sera’s stillness or Kael’s silence—was gone.

His moral intuition had gone silent.

He stood in the flat air on the wrong-bending grass and listened to the inside of his own head, and the thoughts that came were his. All of them. Uncushioned. Unsoftened. The ones he usually let the Force set at a better distance, the ones about Jacen and Mara and the last time he’d trusted someone who felt warm and certain and sure.

The ones about his father, and the comm channel he hadn’t opened, and the report he hadn’t filed, and the choice he made every day to carry this alone because carrying it alone was the only way he knew how to carry anything.

Desh was watching him. Not with a chrono in hand, not with data on his lips—just watching, the way he’d watched Ben in the GAG years, the way he’d watched him on the Steady Returns, the way someone watched a man about to make a decision and wanted to be there when it happened.

Ben met his eyes. Desh didn’t speak. He didn’t need to. The silence between them was the only thing in the unwoven zone that felt like it had structure.

Ben looked away first. He looked at the settlement, at the empty streets, at the buildings that had held twelve thousand people before Veraine had decided they were an acceptable cost. And in the silence where his moral intuition should have been, he heard himself think: *She’s heading for Veranthos. And I still haven’t called Luke.*

The thought was his. All of it. And the silence that followed was louder than any sound.

## Scene 13.02

They went into the breach in single file, and the Force broke apart around them.

Sera came second behind Ben. He heard her breathing falter before he saw her hands. The rhythm she’d held from the settlement edge—steady, counted, disciplined—caught on the third step past the perimeter. Not a gasp. A hitch. The sort of split-second stumble that came when a body expected pressure and found nothing.

Her hands clenched, both at once, like a duelist reaching for a second blade that never came. Muscle memory snapped at empty air. Ben watched her shoulders lock against an impact that wasn’t there, her weight dropping a beat late into a stance she should have hit without thinking. He could see the compensation. He could see the delay. Her jaw tightened, but she didn’t speak.

Ben’s own breath tightened in sympathy. The urge to move—to step in, to give her something to brace against—rose in his chest and had nowhere to go. There was nothing to meet. The force she was fighting never arrived.

Kael moved past her.

He didn’t slow. Didn’t flinch. His stride stayed loose, his breathing even, his weight falling exactly where his boots expected it to. He crossed the unwoven zone the way a man crossed a room he knew by memory, not carefully, not defiantly, just *there*, as if the missing structure was familiar to him instead of wrong.

Ben’s attention snagged on the back of Kael’s head. A question formed without words—something about the zone’s shape, about who belonged where, about why a Sith-trained conscript moved through Force-starvation like a man heading home. Ben felt the wrongness before he could name it. The wrongness of Kael’s ease. The wrongness of seeing someone not struggle while everyone else was trying not to drown.

He didn’t reach for the Force to check. The motion was automatic, ten years of training making it reflex—and the wrong move here. He knew that. He’d learned it at the settlement edge, and learned it again with every step deeper into the breach. But his body wanted the reassurance the way a hand wanted a handhold, and before he could stop himself he opened his awareness and reached.

The Force came back in pieces.

Not emptiness. Presence. Energy. The vast reservoir of something that should have been whole, only it arrived in shards. Multiple voices speaking at once, each only a fraction of the whole, none of them agreeing on direction or weight or meaning. A voice that might have been Sera’s discipline, stripped down to tension. A voice that might have been Torin’s warmth, jagged now, the tone broken into pieces that wouldn’t fit. A voice that was Kael’s tight efficiency, except it wasn’t tight now—it was loose, relaxed, the knot undone in a way that made no sense. Underneath all of them, a deeper voice Ben couldn’t place at all, something vast and patient and utterly still.

He stopped trying to assemble them.

The choice was deliberate. His body tightened against the absence—the vertigo of reaching for a rail and finding open air—then let go. His sternum ached with the effort of not grasping. He let the shards lie where they fell. He let the Force stay broken.

Desh’s voice came from behind him, flat and stripped to data.

“Chrono drift’s accelerating. Two-point-four seconds of variance per minute now. The gradient’s directional—pulling toward a center point about four hundred meters ahead.” A pause. “The plants are growing backward here. I’m looking at a sapling reversing into a seed.”

Ben turned. Desh stood with his chrono in his palm, his face unreadable in the way that meant he was processing something he didn’t like. But it was his eyes that caught Ben—not the numbers, not the tactical read, but something else. A sadness with no object. A grief that wasn’t attached to anything in particular, just there, hanging over him like heat before a storm.

Desh didn’t name it. He reported chrono drift and backward-growing plants and a directional pull, and his voice stayed level through all of it. But that sadness sat in his face like it had been waiting for him to arrive.

Ben looked away. He looked at the zone—at the grass bending the wrong way, at the sapling undoing itself into a seed, at air that tasted flat and sky that felt hollow. In the edge of his vision, Desh’s chrono readings lined up with what Ben’s body had been feeling since they crossed the perimeter.

The breach had a shape.

Not a boundary. A boundary was what the settlement edge had given them, a line where the Force thinned and then failed. This was deeper. This was the thing inside the line, the architecture of the wound itself, and it wasn’t formless. It held. It leaned toward a single point the way water leaned toward a drain, and that point wasn’t here.

It was Veranthos.

Ben’s breath caught. The knowledge hit as physical certainty—a weight settling into his chest, a direction his body understood before his mind had words for it. Veranthos. Not Kolven, not the settlement, not any of the other places Veraine had unwound. The breach was anchored to a specific world, and the ritual could only complete where the wound had been made. The vergence. The origin point. The place where Gavran had built his seal and Veraine had been buried inside it.

He didn’t say it. He didn’t need to. The data and the sensation lined up without language, without room for language. His body knew it first: *She’s heading for Veranthos. That’s where this ends.*

The second fact landed while the first was still settling.

He felt it in his bones—like a bridge cable humming hard before it snapped. The surviving anchors. Sera. Kael. Torin. The three living points in the containment net that hadn’t gone dark. They were drawing on one another.

Not separately. Not the way they had when Ben first sensed them in the stasis ring, each anchor carrying its own share of the load. This was different. This was coupled. Each drain fed the others, each depletion dragging the rest lower, the three of them locked into a spiral that fed itself. The cascade wasn’t slowing. It was compounding.

His hands clenched. The knowledge arrived with no words, no way to turn it into words. Pure embodied fact: the timeline had shrunk. The system was failing faster than anyone had believed, and the three people walking beside him were the system, and they were dragging each other toward collapse.

He looked at Sera. At the half-beat delay in her recovery, the way her body fought a pressure that wasn’t there. He looked at Kael. At the impossible ease, the breathing that hadn’t changed, the stride that belonged to someone who’d been here before. He looked at Desh. At the chrono in his hand and the sadness in his face and the grief without an object the unwoven zone had laid on him like a palm.

Then he looked at Torin.

Torin had stopped.

He stood at the center of the breach—the deepest point, where the Force shards were thinnest and the empty weight was thickest and the air itself seemed to lean away from him. His back was to the group. His shoulders were loose. His head was tilted at that listening angle Ben had learned to dread, the one that meant Torin was hearing something nobody else could hear.

He didn’t move.

Sera stopped. Kael stopped. Desh stopped. The four of them stood at the edge of the worst of it and watched Torin stand at its center, and the silence between them was the silence of the unwoven Force—no structure, no axis, no way to read what was happening inside the man who had once been warm and jagged and certain.

Ben reached. He knew it was useless before he did it—the Force was shards here, partial voices, nothing he could shape into a reading—but he reached anyway, the way a hand reached for a pulse, the way a son reached for a father’s presence in a dark room.

He found nothing.

Not emptiness. Not absence. Something else. Something that had been Torin’s signature—the warm-jagged hum, the weight of a presence that had made the group feel like a team—and was now something Ben couldn’t name. It hadn’t gone dark. It hadn’t gone cold. It had simply become unreadable, the way a face became unreadable when the person behind it stopped performing.

Torin turned.

His face was still. Not calm—calm was something you did, a choice, a control over fear. This was different. This was deep water that didn’t ripple because it was too heavy to move. His eyes found Ben’s and held them, and Ben looked into them and saw nothing he could name.

He didn’t know what he was looking for. He didn’t know what he was looking at. Torin’s face gave him nothing—no warmth, no cold, no signal his pattern-recognition could catch and call a read. The man who had been the most readable person in the group was now a closed door, and Ben stood on the wrong side of it with the Force in shards around him and his instincts gone quiet.

The silence held.

Then Sera’s voice cut through it, tight and controlled and level. “We’re leaving. Now.”

No one argued. Kael turned first, his stride still easy, still unnervingly smooth, still wrong in a way Ben couldn’t yet sort out. Desh pocketed his chrono and fell in behind him, the sadness still sitting in his face like weather. Sera waited for Ben to move, her hands still clenched, her body still braced against a pressure that wasn’t coming.

Ben looked at Torin once more. Torin met his eyes and gave him nothing.

They walked out of the breach.

The Force snapped back at the perimeter—a physical jolt, immediate and sharp, like pressure slamming into place when a hatch sealed. Ben felt it behind his sternum, relief so sudden it almost hurt. The Force returned in a rush, coherent and structured and *there*, the voices knitting into a single field, the axis reasserting itself like a spine straightening after being bent too long.

He could feel Sera again. The edge of her discipline, sharper now, vibrating with whatever she was holding down. He could feel Kael—the tight knot of efficiency back in place, as if the zone had never loosened it. He could feel Desh’s steady quiet, not Force-sensitive but still somehow grounding.

He could feel Torin.

And Torin’s presence was unreadable.

Not absent. Not dark. Not cold. Closed. Like a door shut. Like a channel sealed. Like a man who had decided something and wasn’t ready to be seen.

Ben looked at him. Torin stood at the edge of the perimeter, back to the breach, his face still carrying that deep-water stillness. He didn’t know what Ben was looking for. Ben didn’t know what he was looking at.

The Steady Returns sat on the dead grass behind them, ramp down, running lights on, waiting to carry them away from Kolven and toward Veranthos. Toward the only place the ritual could finish. Toward a cascade that was speeding up faster than anyone had believed.

Ben didn’t speak. He walked toward the ship, and the others walked with him, and the silence they carried was the silence of something seen and not named, something felt and not understood, something that had happened to Torin in the deepest part of the breach and was now sealed inside him like a secret.

## Scene 13.03

The Steady Returns broke atmosphere with the last of the breach still on Ben’s tongue—flat air, a hollow sky, the taste of something that should have been there and wasn’t. Kolven shrank in the aft viewport, a brown-green smear against the starfield, and the breach zone went with it. At the perimeter, the Force had snapped back like a hatch sealing. Ben felt it before he understood it: pressure behind the sternum, relief so sharp it might have been a wound closing too fast.

He opened the comm panel.

The routing glowed institutional blue. Luke’s frequency sat at the top of the queue, exactly where it always did, exactly where it had been for every check-in since Ben left the Temple. Thirteen minutes had gone by since the panel came live. Thirteen minutes of Kolven falling away and the Force stitching itself back together outside the viewport while Ben’s hand stayed on the armrest, not moving.

His breath stayed shallow. His pulse thudded high in his throat. What he’d learned in the breach still hadn’t settled in him—the cascade-acceleration, the array-dependency, the way the three surviving anchors had been pulling on one another like cables humming just before a snap. It sat in his chest as hard fact, a weight without words, and the comm panel sat in front of him with Luke’s frequency lit in blue and the transmit control two fingers away.

He didn’t reach for it.

Desh’s hands moved on the controls, steady and unhurried. The nav-computer chimed once as the course to Veranthos locked in. Sera stood behind Ben’s chair, her reflection caught in the viewport glass—still, silent, her hands loose at her sides in the way that meant she was holding something down. Torin had taken the jump seat aft, his face turned toward the starfield, his presence in the Force shut like a door Ben couldn’t pry open. Kael sat with his back to the bulkhead, eyes half-lidded, breathing even. The cockpit held the silence of five people who had walked into an unwoven zone and come back carrying different pieces of it.

The panel waited.

Thirty seconds. The blue didn’t change. Ben’s hand stayed on the armrest.

A minute. The Steady Returns cleared Kolven’s gravity well. Desh tapped the nav-console once, twice, trimming the course. The sound was small, ordinary, and it landed in the silence like a stone in still water.

Three minutes. Outside the viewport, the Force shimmered faintly against the starfield—restored, coherent, whole in a way Ben knew now was temporary. He could feel its structure, the threads holding, the architecture Gavran had built and Veraine was dismantling and the three people in this ship were still carrying inside their bodies. He could feel the acceleration. The compounding drain. The timeline tightening.

He could feel the two accounts building themselves in his mind, not as words but as shapes, as pressures, as the difference between one kind of weight and another.

The first account was Luke’s. Institutional. Framed through Jedi Order protocol. Filtered through a Master’s interpretation of what Ben should know and when. The call would pull the mission into Luke’s response window—Luke would ask, and Ben would answer, and the answers would become orders, and the orders would become a shape Ben hadn’t chosen. That carried the weight of every check-in he’d ever made, every report he’d ever filed, every time he’d handed over his perception and let someone else tell him what it meant.

The second account was the group’s. Raw. Unfiltered. Bound only by what they could trust each other to understand. Sera’s discipline and Torin’s closed door and Kael’s impossible ease and Desh’s steady hands and the grief-without-object the unwoven zone had laid on all of them. That carried the weight of what Ben had felt in the breach—the shards, the voices, the body-knowledge that didn’t become a report because it wasn’t a report. It was something he’d lived through. Something he couldn’t hand off.

His hands tightened on the armrest.

The choice wasn’t about leadership. It wasn’t about self-reliance as a concept, as a principle, as something he could name and defend. It was about who he could trust with the Force-reading he’d just lived through. It was about whether the thing he’d felt in the breach—the full unweaving, the cascade-acceleration, the array-dependency, the silent closed face of a man who had once been warm—could survive translation into a channel Luke would receive and interpret and act on.

It couldn’t. Ben knew that. The certainty sat in his sternum beside the relief of the restored Force and the ache of what that restoration had cost.

He closed the panel.

His hand moved from the armrest to the control. The institutional blue vanished. The channel went dark.

Ben’s shoulders eased a fraction—not relief, but the settling of a decision made. He felt Desh’s hands on the controls, unchanged, still steady. He felt Sera’s reflection in the viewport glass, looking at the panel, then away. Neither spoke.

The silence between them wasn’t empty. It held what they’d all seen in the breach—the backward-growing plants, the chrono drift, the sapling reversing into a seed, Torin standing at the center of the worst of it with his face unreadable and his presence sealed off. It held what Ben had just chosen not to report.

He looked out the viewport. The Force shimmered against the starfield, restored and coherent and whole from here, and Ben looked at it and realized he wasn’t sure he knew how to read it anymore. The shimmer looked like structure. It looked like health. It looked like the thing he’d been trained to trust since he was old enough to feel the Force at all.

It was costing three people in this ship everything they had left.

The unopened channel sat dark on his console. Ben looked at it. Desh saw. Sera saw. Torin’s closed presence sat in the jump seat aft like a question nobody knew how to ask.

The Steady Returns carried them toward Veranthos, and the silence went with them, and the dark panel was the last thing Ben saw before the chapter ended.
