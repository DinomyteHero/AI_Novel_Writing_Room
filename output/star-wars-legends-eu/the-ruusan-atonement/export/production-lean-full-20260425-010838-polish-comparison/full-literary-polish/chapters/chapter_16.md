# Chapter 16

## Scene 16.01

The comm panel lit with an incoming signal that didn’t match any civilian or Jedi protocol Ben recognized. On Desh’s secondary display, the encryption handshake scrolled by in characters from an older conversation.

Kael straightened in the jump seat. His attention had been on the starfield, the nav overlay, the silence he seemed to carry everywhere, but the protocol’s era signature hit him before it reached anyone else. His jaw tightened once, then eased.

“That’s hers,” he said.

Ben’s hand had already started toward the accept key. He stopped a finger’s breadth short and forced himself to wait. The urge to answer was physical, a lean in his chest that wanted to close the distance before distance could be used against him. He knew that feeling. He didn’t give in to it.

“Full encryption,” Desh said. His fingers were already moving on his datapad, breaking the signal into pieces with the same quick, precise motions Ben had seen him use on a dozen threat assessments. “Sith-era protocol. Brotherhood variant, if I’m reading the handshake right. She’s not hiding who she is.”

“She is announcing it,” Sera said. Under pressure, her voice went formal, each word cut to its essentials. “The protocol itself is the signature. She wants us to know the signal is authentic.”

“Or she wants us to think it is.” Desh never looked up. “Could be a lure. Somebody who knows enough about Brotherhood comm protocols to fake a handshake.”

“Who else would know enough?” Kael’s voice stayed quiet, the question flat and exact. He wasn’t arguing. He was stating what he’d already accepted and waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

The comm panel chimed again. Incoming audio.

Ben keyed the channel open.

The voice that filled the cockpit was calm, precise, and complete in a way that made every sentence sound measured twice. “I would prefer to speak rather than be spoken about. I suspect you would as well.”

Silence followed, the kind that happened when five people each had something to say and none of them wanted to start.

Desh broke it first. “She’s good. One sentence and she’s already set the terms.”

“She has not set anything,” Sera said. “She has asked. The difference is the trap.”

“The difference is she used Kael’s name in the handshake.” Desh turned his datapad so Ben could see the decrypted header. “She’s not broadcasting to the Steady Returns. She’s hailing Kael Drenn through a protocol he recognizes and asking to talk. That’s not a trap. That’s an invitation with a very specific guest list.”

Torin shifted against the cockpit’s aft bulkhead. He’d been quiet since the signal came through, watching the exchange with the same patient attention he brought to everything. When he spoke, his voice carried the warmth it always did, the storyteller’s cadence that made people lean in instead of brace.

“We should hear her.”

Sera turned toward him. “You would walk into a meeting with a Sith alchemist who has spent a thousand years planning her next move, on the strength of a polite invitation?”

“I would hear what she has to say before I decided whether to walk anywhere.” Torin’s tone never sharpened. “She reached out. That’s information. Refusing to receive it does not make us safer. It makes us less informed.”

“She reached out because she wants something,” Desh said. “The question is whether what she wants is something we want to give her.”

Ben listened as the argument moved around the cockpit and felt each position settle into place. Desh’s tactical caution was the read he trusted, the one that had kept them alive through operations where trust was the first thing to get somebody killed. Sera’s archival memory was the one he listened to; she knew Veraine’s era in ways the rest of them didn’t, and her instinct to treat the invitation as a trap was earned, not reflex.

Torin’s warmth was something else. Ben registered it without naming it, the way he had in the bunker on the first survey world, the way he had when Torin described the thin place as a relief instead of a wound. It was half of what made Torin effective and half of what made the skin on Ben’s arms go cool. He didn’t know why he was noticing it now. He only knew that he was.

Kael still hadn’t spoken since those first two words. His silence was an answer too.

“She’s going to keep broadcasting until we answer,” Ben said. “That protocol is built for persistence. She’ll cycle the handshake every ninety seconds until she gets a response or the signal degrades beyond recovery.”

Desh looked at him. “You’re considering it.”

“I’m considering the fact that she’s been invisible for fifteen chapters and she just stepped into the open. That isn’t an accident. She wants something, and whatever it is, I’d rather learn it in a room where I can see her hands than over a comm channel where I can’t.”

“The room is the trap,” Sera said. “You cannot see her hands if her hands are the room.”

“Then we don’t meet in her room.” Ben brought up the nav display on the main screen. The Veranthos course overlay was still there beneath the argument, the bearing they’d been following since the first survey world. “Neutral ground. Midpoint between our position and Veranthos. She can’t have had time to prepare it.”

“She has had a thousand years to prepare everything,” Sera said. “You are assuming her preparation began when we arrived. It did not.”

Ben met her eyes. “I’m not assuming anything. I’m choosing to face her now instead of later, when she’s had more time to shape the encounter. That’s not trust. That’s tactics.”

The debate was still open. Desh had another objection forming behind his eyes; Sera’s jaw was working on a sentence that hadn’t escaped yet. Torin was watching Ben with that same patient warmth, and Kael was still silent, and the comm panel was going to chime again in forty seconds with another handshake cycle.

Ben didn’t wait.

His hand moved to the comm controls. He keyed the acceptance sequence—coordinates, timestamp, acknowledgment of the protocol—and transmitted before the argument could reopen. The confirmation packet left the Steady Returns with a soft chime that already sounded like a decision made.

The cockpit went quiet.

“Coordinates sent,” Ben said. “She’ll have them in twelve minutes. We’ll have her response in twenty-four.”

Desh looked at the transmit log, then at Ben. His expression didn’t change; it was the same operational stillness he wore through a hundred decisions he hadn’t agreed with. “You just overrode all of us.”

“I know.”

“Without answering Sera’s objection. Or mine.”

“I know.” Ben met his eyes and held them. “I couldn’t answer them. That’s not the same as not hearing them.”

Sera’s voice came out quieter now, the formality still there but the edge gone. “You have committed us to a meeting with a woman who has killed more Jedi than anyone in this cockpit has ever met, on ground she will have time to assess before we arrive, with no guarantee she will honor the truce.”

“Yes.”

“And you believe this is the correct course.”

Ben let the question sit for a beat. The unease was still there at the edge of his senses, familiar and patient as weather. Veraine was somewhere ahead of them inside it, moving along the Corridor with a plan he couldn’t see and a timeline he couldn’t afford to let her control.

“I believe I can’t fight an abstraction,” he said. “And she’s been an abstraction since the moment we found her empty chamber. I need to see her hands. I need to hear her voice in a room instead of over a speaker. I need to know what she wants badly enough to stop running and start talking.”

He looked at each of them in turn—Desh, Sera, Torin, Kael. “That’s not an answer to your objection. It’s why I made the call anyway.”

Torin nodded once. The warmth was still there, and Ben still didn’t know what to do with it. “Then we prepare.”

Desh was already bringing up the sensor logs, his disagreement filed away in the same place he put everything that wasn’t immediately useful. “She’s going to be early. Somebody with her patience doesn’t arrive on time. She’ll be in position before we get there, and she’ll use the wait to study the ground.”

“Then we don’t give her much ground to study.” Ben pulled the nav display back up. The meeting point was a flat stretch of terrain on a world too insignificant to have a name on their charts, equidistant between the Steady Returns’ current position and the Veranthos bearing. “Minimal approach. We land, we talk, we leave. She doesn’t get a tour.”

“She will not need one,” Sera said. She had turned back to her own console, but her shoulders still held the rigid line that meant she was keeping something in. “She has been studying this Corridor for a thousand years. She knows the ground better than we do.”

“Then we’ll learn it from her.” Ben locked the coordinates into the nav computer. “That’s what this meeting is. Whatever else she wants from it, we’re going to get something too.”

The nav display shifted as the computer accepted the new course. The overlay marked the meeting point, and beyond it, a single contact already in position.

Veraine’s ship.

One vessel. Minimal crew. No support craft, no escort, no visible weapons signature. Either she was supremely confident or supremely prepared.

Probably both.

Ben studied the contact for a long moment. The unease pressed at the edge of his senses, patient and familiar, and somewhere ahead of them in it was a woman who had spent a thousand years planning her next move.

He’d just agreed to walk into a room with her.

“Twelve minutes to confirmation,” Desh said. “Then we’ll know if she’s serious about the truce or just wanted to see if we’d answer.”

“She’s serious,” Kael said.

Everyone turned. Kael hadn’t moved from the jump seat, his silence broken only by those two words. He met Ben’s eyes with the same watchful economy he’d worn since the moment they’d woken him.

“She used my name,” he said. “She does not waste names.”

The comm panel chimed. Incoming confirmation.

Veraine had answered in eight minutes instead of twelve.

## Scene 16.02

The clearing was flat and bare, a stretch of anonymous ground chosen for meetings nobody wanted on their own turf. Veraine’s ship sat at the far edge with its gangway down, one vessel and little else, while the Ash-Tithe Compact held position in ash-gray vacuum coats and mixed armor harnesses—unarmed in posture, not in fact. One of them, a woman with a manifest slate and false transponder tags, tracked the Steady Returns as it approached without even fingering a weapon. Another stood by a maintenance crate that looked too square to be accidental. A third cradled an archive cylinder as if it were a sacred relic. The fourth watched Kael with the focused attention of someone who had taught fear to stand at attention.

Ben took all of it in during the walk from the landing zone to the clearing’s center, his senses open, his hand hovering near his lightsaber. Something sat at the edge of his awareness—patient, familiar, the same off-key weight he had been following since the first survey world. Veraine was inside it somewhere, and in a moment she would be in front of him. His body wanted motion, the clean answer of action, the same pull it always got when the quarry finally stopped running.

Then she stepped out from the shadow of her ship’s gangway.

Calm. Composed. Military bearing under academic refinement, like a blade carried by someone who also wrote treatises. Her hands were empty. Her posture was straight without being rigid. She extended her right hand in greeting with the easy formality of a colleague, and the gesture was so ordinary, so stripped of theater, that Ben felt his threat assessment wobble.

He had wanted a monster. Monsters were easier.

“Ben Skywalker,” she said. No contraction. Clean, complete grammar wrapped around the name. “I have wanted to meet the Jedi who could read the seal’s failure before it became visible.”

Ben did not take her hand. He stopped two paces short and let the distance answer for him. “You have been leaving us a trail. That is not the same as wanting to meet.”

“The trail was an invitation. You accepted.” She withdrew her hand without the slightest sign of irritation, which somehow made her harder to read. “I am Darth Veraine. The title is accurate, not a provocation. I trust you will not waste time pretending otherwise.”

Behind Ben, Sera had gone still in the way that meant she was cataloguing threats. Torin had drawn inward, his warmth tightening around him. Desh stood at Ben’s left shoulder with the same operational quiet he carried into every tactical problem, his eyes moving across the Compact in a pattern Ben recognized from a dozen operations. Kael had stopped three steps back and had not moved since.

Veraine’s gaze moved across them and settled on Ben. “You have questions. I have questions. I propose we exchange them rather than fire.”

“You are the one who broke the seal,” Ben said. “That puts the burden of explanation on you.”

“Does it?” The question carried no heat, no challenge—only that slight lift of genuine inquiry. “You have seen the anchor system. You have seen what your predecessors built. Do you believe the seal was a structure that deserved to hold?”

Ben felt the shape of the trap before he could put words to it. She was not asking him to defend the seal. She was asking whether he had already decided it was indefensible, and she knew the answer he had been carrying since the moment he understood what Gavran had done to twelve living beings.

He did not give it to her. “The seal was keeping a wound closed. Whatever else it was, it was doing that.”

“It was keeping a wound closed by turning Force-sensitives into structural rebar,” Veraine said. “Twelve anchors. One keystone. A thousand years of half-aware imprisonment while their life-force was drained to maintain containment. You have met three of the survivors. You have seen what the system cost them. Do you believe the cost was acceptable?”

The question settled into the clearing’s quiet. Ben could feel Sera’s stillness sharpen behind him—she had been one of those anchors, and Veraine knew it, and Veraine had not once looked at her. The omission was deliberate. The question was aimed at Ben because Ben was the one meant to feel the weight of answering.

“I believe the people who built it thought it was,” Ben said.

“Yes.” Veraine gave a single, precise nod. “They thought many things. They thought the Weave was natural and the wound was an aberration. They thought the binary was eternal and the war was inevitable. They thought imprisoning their enemies as living infrastructure was mercy because they did not execute them. A thousand years of war, Skywalker, and at the end of it they built a prison and called it peace. Did it work?”

Ben opened his mouth, then shut it again as the ground shifted under the question. She was not asking whether the seal had held. She was asking whether the thing they had built had produced anything worth the cost.

“It held for a thousand years,” he said.

“It held because twelve people were made into a wall. When they died, the wall weakened. When I escaped, the wall began to fail. The seal was not a solution. It was a postponement. You are now living in the moment the postponement ended.” She let that sit for a beat. The silence after her sentences had a pressure of its own—not the silence of someone waiting to be challenged, but the silence of someone who had already run every objection to ground and was leaving him room to arrive there himself. “The wound is real. The seal is a prison built from fear. Release is not destruction. It is the end of an arrangement that should never have been made.”

“Release kills people,” Ben said. “You know that. You have run the numbers.”

“I have. I counted the transition dead before I came here. Did you?”

The question landed like a blade laid flat. Ben felt Sera shift behind him and knew without looking that her jaw had tightened. Torin had gone still. Desh’s attention had stopped roaming the Compact and fixed on Veraine with the flat, measuring stare of someone who had just reclassified a threat.

“You are asking me to accept casualties you do not have the right to decide,” Ben said.

“I am asking you to acknowledge that the current arrangement also produces casualties. The seal is failing regardless of what either of us chooses. The question is not whether people will die. The question is whether they will die preserving a prison or whether they will die because someone finally had the courage to end one.” She held his gaze. Her Force presence was analytical and precise, not a surge of dark-side malice but something stranger and worse—certainty without heat. “You are a Jedi. You have been taught that the dark side is corruption and the light is purity. Has that teaching prepared you for what you found in Gavran’s fortress? Has it given you a framework for understanding what your predecessors did?”

Ben did not answer. He could not—not without stepping deeper into the logic she was building, and every step into it made the next question harder to refuse.

Veraine waited. When he stayed silent, she turned.

The movement was small and deliberate, a shift of attention that reoriented the entire clearing. She turned toward Kael the way a scholar might turn to a text she had been waiting to examine, and her voice changed—still precise, still complete, but with something specific and personal under the academic cadence.

“Kael Drenn,” she said. “You were conscripted by the Brotherhood because your Force-sensitivity made you useful. You were captured by Gavran’s forces and repurposed because your Force-sensitivity made you useful. You have spent a thousand years as infrastructure because someone else’s institutional framework required your compliance. Why would you defend the frameworks that used you as building material?”

Kael stood motionless. His face was a mask, and it cost him; Ben could see the strain in the set of his jaw, the stillness around his eyes, the careful control of his breathing. He did not speak.

The quiet stretched.

Ben felt the question hit his own chest. It was not rhetorical. It contained a real answer Kael might give, and Veraine’s refusal to oversell it—no gesture, no lean, no theatrical pause—made it worse, not better. She had asked because she genuinely wanted to know what Kael would say, and that made the question more dangerous than any speech.

“You and I are the same,” Veraine said, softer now, the precision still there but the volume lowered to something almost intimate. “We were both made into tools by institutions that claimed the right to use us. The difference is not that I am wrong and you are right. The difference is that I have stopped waiting for permission to stop being a tool.”

Kael’s silence held. Ben could feel the effort in it—the way Kael was holding himself still against the question’s weight, the way his answer was assembling somewhere behind the mask and was not ready yet. Ben wanted to speak. He wanted to interrupt, to deflect, to do anything that would break the pressure before it found its target.

He could not. The question was not his to answer.

Veraine studied Kael’s face for a long moment. Then she asked, quieter still, “What did you conclude during the centuries in the wall? When you had nothing to do but conclude.”

She did not wait for an answer. She turned and walked back toward her ship, her people falling into formation around her without a word, and the question remained behind in the clearing like something she had placed there rather than spoken.

Kael still had not moved.

Ben stood ready for the handoff, his worldview tested by a coherent argument he could not dismiss, delivered in a register that denied him the target a monologue would have given. The cost sat in his chest like a weight he could not shift. Kael’s silence was not refusal. It was the specific silence of someone whose answer was still assembling, and Veraine had known exactly where to put the question so it would sit where Kael stored answers.

The gangway retracted. The ship’s engines cycled up, a low hum that vibrated through the packed earth. Ben watched it lift and did not reach for his lightsaber.

He had gotten what he came for. He had seen her hands. He had heard her voice in a room instead of over a speaker. He knew what she wanted badly enough to stop running and start talking.

He just wasn’t sure he knew how to fight it.

## Scene 16.03

Sera stepped forward.

It was a small, deliberate movement, one pace that took her past Ben’s shoulder and into the open ground between the group and Veraine. She stopped at arm’s length from the other woman. Close enough for Ben to see the tightness at the corner of her jaw, the way her hands hung at her sides in the stillness of someone who had learned composure as survival and worn it so long it had gone into her bones.

Veraine turned to face her. The movement was unhurried, almost academic—the same measured attention she had given Kael, the same attention she had given Ben. Her hands stayed empty. Her expression did not change.

Sera spoke.

“You have described the seal as a prison.” Her voice was formal, the archaic syntax giving each word the weight of a blade set down on stone. “You have described release as mercy. You have counted the dead from the transition and called the arithmetic acceptable.”

She paused. The tremor was in her voice now, controlled but present, like a note held too long at the edge of breaking.

“I was inside the seal for a thousand years. I know what it cost. I know what Gavran did. I know the weight of every year and every hour and every moment I spent as structural rebar in a machine I did not consent to power.”

Her gaze never left Veraine’s face.

“And I am telling you that when you open the wound, what comes out will not be mercy. It will be nothing. I have felt the nothing. I have lived beside it. I have spent a millennium listening to it press against the seal like a hand against glass, and there is nothing on the other side of that glass that deserves to be let through.”

She stopped.

Ben felt the silence after that as if someone had laid a weight across his chest. Sera had not raised her voice. She had not gestured. She had delivered it once, short and lived, and now stood motionless with her hands still at her sides, a thousand years hanging in the air between her and the woman who had asked for it.

The Force around them carried no combat texture. No surge of dark-side pressure, no gathering of defensive energy. Only the stillness of a room where something had been said that could not be unsaid.

Veraine’s composure sharpened.

The change was small—a recalibration of attention, a narrowing of the intelligence behind her eyes. Her expression did not warm. Her posture did not shift. But something in her face tightened into focus, and when she spoke her voice was quieter than before.

“You have described what the wound feels like from inside the seal,” she said. “You have described the nothing. You have described the pressure. You have described a thousand years of proximity to a thing you believe should never be released.”

She held Sera’s gaze.

“You have not described what the place felt like before the wound was made.”

The question landed without flourish—no lean, no gesture, no theatrical pause. Veraine gave it the way she gave everything else: precise, complete, already certain of the answer she was not asking for.

“You were an anchor, Knight Varik. You were placed at the seal’s perimeter. You felt the wound’s pressure for a thousand years. But you also felt the Weave as it existed before the seal was built. You felt the structure Gavran’s technique damaged. You felt what the Jedi Lords chose to preserve.”

She tilted her head a fraction.

“Was it natural? Or was it already a prison you had been taught to call home?”

Sera did not answer.

Ben watched her face and saw the cost of testimony land—not in movement, not in any crack in composure, but in the absolute stillness that followed the question. Sera’s jaw tightened. Her hands did not move. Her breathing did not change. But something behind her eyes went very quiet, and Ben understood with cold clarity that Veraine had not refuted her.

She had asked a question Sera could not answer without unmaking the ground under her feet.

The silence stretched. Veraine held it for three heartbeats, four—long enough for the question to settle into the clearing like something placed rather than spoken—and then she turned.

The movement was unhurried. She walked back toward her ship with measured steps, her boots pressing into the packed earth with the same even rhythm she had brought into the meeting. She did not look back.

The Ash-Tithe Compact fell in around her without an order. The woman with the manifest slate—Vess, Ben’s mind supplied, cataloguing without permission—checked the sky before the ramp, a reflex that smelled of combat landings and contested extractions. The one by the maintenance crate touched it once, a quick verification, then followed. The man with the archive cylinder bowed his head to it instead of to Veraine, cradling it against his chest as he climbed the gangway. The fourth—Mira, the one who had watched Kael—moved only when Veraine’s silence allowed it, stepping into the gap behind her with the easy economy of someone who had done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

Ben caught himself cataloguing. The ship’s approach vector—low and fast, a combat descent that had flared at the last moment, not the cautious spiral of a diplomatic arrival. The two tells in the crew’s discipline: Vess’s sky-check and the way the maintenance crate had been placed to give cover if the meeting broke into fire. The three archive fragments Veraine had referenced as if everyone in the clearing had read them—Karness Muur’s taxonomy of Force-wounds, the Veranthos observational corpus, and something Ben did not recognize, a citation delivered in the same level tone as the others but without the anchoring reference that would have placed it in any archive he knew.

He did not comment. He did not stop doing it.

The gangway retracted. The ship’s engines spun up, a low hum that vibrated through the packed earth and rose to a quiet, uncontentious frequency—civilian-grade, or made to sound civilian-grade. The ship lifted, turned, and receded against the cleared sky.

No Force-shift came. No easing, no release of the thing sitting at the edge of Ben’s awareness since the first survey world. The meeting was over, and it was not over.

Ben stood in the clearing with the group. His body stayed tense. His mind kept at its catalog—archive-fragment register, crew tells, approach vector, the specific stillness in Veraine’s face when she had asked Sera the question that could not be answered—but the list did not clarify anything. It only made the load heavier.

Sera had not moved. Kael stood where he had been since Veraine turned away from him, his face unreadable. Torin’s warmth had drawn inward until it was almost gone. Desh was watching the sky where the ship had been, his expression wearing the same operational flatness he used after every tactical problem that did not resolve cleanly.

Ben still had no language for what he was carrying out of the meeting. Only the certainty that Sera’s testimony and Veraine’s counterquestion were both true, and the meeting had not settled them, and something in the room had been not only the room.

## Scene 16.04

The ramp sealed with a hard, final thunk.

Ben climbed to the cockpit deck behind the others, pale clearing dust marking their path across the plating. The Steady Returns smelled of recycled air, old caf, and the faint machine grease that always lived somewhere under Desh’s maintenance work. Familiar enough that the wrongness stood out harder.

Nobody sat where they usually sat.

Sera took the space by the nav station with her back against the bulkhead, arms crossed, posture held so tightly it looked built rather than chosen. Torin had dropped into the jump seat beside the engineering console instead of anywhere that belonged to him, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, trying to keep warmth in the room by sheer decision. Kael stayed near the galley hatch in the corner, hands visible on his thighs, expression shut down but not absent. Desh was already at the pilot’s station, not settling in, just working through passive departure checks with the kind of silence that meant his thoughts were several moves ahead and none of them pleased him.

Ben stopped at the threshold a moment too long. Some part of him kept waiting for the shipboard rhythm to reassert itself—for restraints to click, for Desh to mutter at a balky display, for Torin to ask whether anyone wanted caf as if they had only come back from another hard stop and not from a conversation that had left something open in all of them. The room would not give him that.

“Everyone in one piece?” Desh asked without looking up.

No one answered.

Desh exhaled once through his nose. “I’ll log that as yes.”

Ben crossed to the copilot’s station and sat. The seat still held residual warmth. Desh had been up and moving since before they boarded, then. Ben let that file itself away with everything else.

On the passive display, Veraine’s ship rode the edge of the board as a narrowing blip, vector clean, speed controlled, no flare of panic, no sign of haste. It looked like what it wanted to look like: meeting concluded, parties withdrawing.

Ben’s mind kept refusing the larger shape and catching instead on fragments.

Veraine’s voice, measured enough to make every question sound like record rather than challenge. Sera answering anyway, not because Veraine deserved it but because the dead did. Kael’s silence after Veraine turned to him, no refusal announced, no surrender either. And around all of it, the Ash-Tithe Compact not standing like witnesses or attendants but occupying ground by function.

Vess by the ramp, checking sky and perimeter with reflex too old to be performance. Pira’s crate set down too square, too useful, in a spot that would matter if the truce collapsed—or if the truce was never the only business being done there. Serran with the archive cylinder tucked close and guarded like cargo already assigned. Mira still as a lesson learned under fear, moving only when Veraine’s pause released her. It kept needling at him because it did not fit the shape of a leader bringing people merely to stand behind her while she talked.

“The meeting did not change her,” Sera said.

Her voice came out level, but the control cost her. Ben could hear that much.

Torin looked across at her. “No.”

He let the word sit before adding, “But knowing her shape still matters.”

Sera kept her eyes on the forward viewport. “Her shape was already clear.”

“Clear in principle,” Torin said softly. “Not always in practice.”

That almost got a laugh out of nobody. The room stayed where it was.

“She asked questions she had already answered for herself,” Sera said. “She counted losses as tolerable before I spoke. She heard what happened and used it to refine the next cut.” Her jaw set. “Knowing that does not make her smaller.”

“No,” Torin said again, gentler still. “It makes her more exact.”

Sera did not reply. It was not an argument, not really. They were too tired for one, and too close to the thing itself.

Ben turned his head slightly toward Kael. Since boarding, Kael had said nothing. Ben had seen him carry silence before: as discipline, as retreat, as refusal to feed other people what they wanted from him. This was none of those cleanly. There was strain in it now, not visible until you looked twice.

“Kael,” Ben said.

Kael’s eyes shifted to him. Slow. Deliberate.

“I don’t have a useful report,” he said.

The dryness in his voice would have passed for indifference to anyone who did not know him. Ben knew better.

“She asked you something,” Ben said.

“Yes.”

Ben waited. Kael did not give him more at first, and then, after a breath, “I am still deciding what belongs to me and what belongs to the question.”

Torin glanced toward him, concern plain and carefully unobtrusive. Sera’s posture changed by less than an inch. Ben nodded and left it there. Pressing would only make Kael retreat into the old blankness, and this was not blank. It only looked that way from a distance.

Desh straightened a little over the board. “I’m running back-sweeps across the meeting window.”

Ben looked over. “Back-sweeps?”

“Passive only.” Desh tapped one display, then another, building overlays from approach logs and local traffic drift instead of touching the active systems. “I want our own tracks, their tracks, the clearing perimeter, and every piece of movement we recorded while we were busy having an honest philosophical exchange.”

Torin’s mouth tightened at that, not in disagreement.

Sera pushed off the bulkhead half a step. “You think she brought us there to distract us.”

“I think,” Desh said, “the Compact moved like a crew with split jobs.”

That pulled everyone’s attention to him.

He kept his eyes on the display as he spoke. “Not bodyguard work. Not ceremonial placement. Vess watched the perimeter before and after. Pira didn’t set that crate down at random. Serran had the archive cylinder in hand like he was already on assignment, not like he was carrying a prop for the conversation. Mira’s placement changed with Veraine’s pauses, not with the argument.” His fingers moved, drawing time marks across the meeting window. “And Veraine has had too much in motion for too long to be doing all of it herself in sequence.”

Ben heard the point under the procedure. Arrays. Archive visits. The truce itself. Veranthos ahead of them by weeks if their worst read was right. That only worked if she had been laying rails before they ever saw her, using what the old Brotherhood records gave her and sending different hands to different pieces while everyone else was still recovering survivors and trying to understand what they had already lost.

Desh did not say all of that aloud. He didn’t need to. The shape of it was there.

“Could be nothing,” he said after a beat. “Could be me disliking how tidy they were.”

“No,” Ben said, before he meant to.

Desh glanced at him once.

Ben looked back to the fading passive track. “Not nothing.”

Because that was the part he could not shake. Veraine had spoken sincerely in the clearing. Ben was sure of it. She had meant every word. That made it worse, not better. People who lied for cover could be read eventually. People who told the truth while something else moved under the truth were harder.

The display washed pale light across Desh’s hands as he pulled old sensor slices and departure vectors into alignment. No alarms. No visible anomaly. Just a pilot refusing to let a bad pattern pass untested.

Sera’s arms loosened, though she did not uncross them. “If there was another layer,” she said, “we should have seen it.”

“Maybe,” Torin said.

Kael spoke without looking up. “Not if the meeting was meant to hold our eyes.”

The quiet after that landed differently.

Ben turned back to the viewport. Veraine’s ship had dwindled to almost nothing, a point easy to lose against the starfield if you looked away even once. He kept watching until the last passive trace vanished.

Desh started the back-sweeps. Ben watched Veraine’s ship vanish from the last passive track and knew the meeting was not finished with them.
