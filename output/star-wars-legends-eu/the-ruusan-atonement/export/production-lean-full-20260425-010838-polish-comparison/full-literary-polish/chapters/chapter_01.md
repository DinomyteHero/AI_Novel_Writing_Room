# Chapter 1

## Scene 01.01

Rendell’s training saber snapped across Ben’s shoulder with a crack that cut through the salle’s late-night hush.

Ben had seen the strike coming. His body had read the angle, the timing, the shift of weight onto Rendell’s back foot—and then his guard had dropped a half second late, the blade slipping through a gap that should never have been there. The hit landed clean, a sharp sting that would bloom into a bruise by morning.

He let out a breath and stepped back, lowering his blade.

Rendell shut off his saber and pulled off his helmet, sweat darkening the hair at his temples. “You all right?”

“Fine.” Ben rolled his shoulder once and got the answer for himself. “Good hit.”

“You were open.” Rendell sounded more puzzled than critical, as if he’d found something wrong in a drill schematic. “Third time this week.”

Ben knew that too well. The first time, on a Form V transition he’d been drilling since he was fourteen, he’d written it off as distraction. The second, in a three-on-one exercise, he’d blamed the hour, the week, and the usual weight of Temple life. Now this. A Soresu opening sequence—the sequence, the one his body knew by reflex—and the Force had come in behind the beat, late enough to trip him.

He didn’t say any of it.

“Late night,” he said, thumbing his blade off. The hum died. “I’m done.”

Rendell hesitated. Ben could feel the question gathering—the reflex to press, to check on a peer who had gone off rhythm—but Rendell was tired too, and Ben was already moving toward the bench. The moment passed.

“Get some sleep,” Rendell said at last.

“Yeah.” Ben didn’t look back. “You too.”

Footsteps crossed the salle floor. The door opened, then closed. Silence settled again, broken only by the faint chime of a turbolift somewhere in the corridor and the distant, steady click of a cleaning droid at work.

Ben stood alone in the empty salle.

The Temple at this hour was never truly quiet. Night-watch traffic murmured through the walls—Knights and Masters moving between meditation chambers and the refectory, staff keeping the Order running while most of Coruscant slept. The salle’s usual Force presence, that old accumulation of practice and focus and long hours, should have settled around him like a steady hand.

It didn’t.

That off-note in the field was still there. It had been there all week, a half-beat lag Ben kept trying to blame on fatigue or distraction or the lingering static everyone said the Force was still shedding after Abeloth. The analysts had a term for it. Residual echo. Background noise that hadn’t gone away.

Ben had stopped believing that three days ago. He hadn’t told anyone.

He lifted his training saber. Didn’t ignite it. Just held the hilt and moved into the Soresu opening sequence—slowly, deliberately, the way he’d first learned it in his father’s practice sessions. Step, pivot, guard high. The blade stayed dark. He wasn’t testing muscle memory. He was testing whatever sat underneath it.

Step, pivot, guard low.

The first three movements came clean. His body knew them the way it knew breathing. On the fourth—a tight rotation into a cross-block that should have flowed like water—his timing broke.

The Force came in late. Not absent. Not blocked. Just behind, like a musician entering half a beat after the rest of the ensemble, all the right notes at the wrong instant. His body, expecting the field to be there at the turn, overcorrected. His shoulder pulled. His balance slipped.

Ben stopped.

He stood in the center of the salle, breathing hard, the dark hilt still in his hand. It wasn’t fatigue. It wasn’t the week or the hour or the weight of a Jedi Knight’s schedule. It was in the Force itself—a missing load-bearing beam, a gap where there should have been support. The field was lagging, and Ben’s body, built to move inside a rhythm he had never had to think about, was paying for it.

He didn’t think *structural*. He didn’t think *wound*. He felt it as a tightening in his chest, a sharpening of focus, a hard shift from *I’m failing* to *something’s wrong*. The difference came not as a theory but as a physical jolt—his shoulders squaring against a weight he couldn’t name, his jaw setting in a way that would have told his mother, if she’d been there, that he’d already decided not to share.

He deactivated the blade he hadn’t ignited and set it on the bench.

The cleaning droid hummed past the salle door. A pair of voices drifted from the turbolift vestibule—Knights coming off a late meditation shift, talking too low to make out. The Temple was still alive around him, still carrying its ordinary night traffic, and Ben was standing in the middle of it with a gap in the Force that nobody else could feel.

He didn’t know whether that made him the only reliable sensor in the room or the only broken one.

The door opened.

Ben turned. His father stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and the kind of stillness Ben had learned to read before he’d learned to read anything else—the look of a Jedi Master about to hand over a mission and waiting to see how it landed.

“Ben.” Luke’s voice was quiet, the way it always was this late. “I need you for a survey run.”

Ben’s hand tightened on the bench edge. Relief and dread hit together—relief that he might not be alone with this, dread that being alone with it had just stopped being an option.

“How bad?” he asked.

Luke’s expression stayed level. “I don’t know yet. That’s the problem.”

## Scene 01.02

Luke’s office smelled of old flimsi and the faint ozone tang of a holocomm that had been left on too long. The door was open when Ben arrived. His father was already behind the desk, the datapad casting a blue glow over his hands, and the look on his face was the one Ben had learned to read before he’d learned much of anything else—the stillness of a Jedi Master who had already chosen his question and was waiting to see how it would land.

“Ben.” Luke didn’t stand. His voice was quiet, the way it always was this late. “Close the door.”

Ben did. The latch clicked shut, and the wash of Coruscant traffic through the window became the loudest thing in the room—speeders, airhaulers, the distant chime of a landing beacon, the city grinding on while the Grand Master of the Jedi Order sat behind a desk that had seen better decades.

“You were in the salle late,” Luke said.

It wasn’t a question. Ben didn’t answer it like one. “Couldn’t sleep.”

“So I gathered.” Luke held his gaze a moment longer, then looked down at the datapad. He slid it across the desk. “I need you for a survey run.”

Ben reached for the pad before his mind had quite caught up with the motion. He knew what one of his father’s mission briefings felt like—the pause, the careful neutrality, the way the whole thing came wrapped in words meant to sound ordinary. He’d been on the receiving end since he was old enough to hold a lightsaber. Familiarity settled into his shoulders and made them tight.

“Survey,” he said flatly. “Where?”

“Outer Rim. Darrpa Sector and points rimward.” Luke leaned back. At this distance, his Force presence was warm and steady, the same presence that had anchored Ben through half a dozen crises. “Seventeen flagged reports over the last six weeks. Anomalous Force readings. Nothing that matches any known phenomenon.”

Ben’s thumb found the scroll control. The pad lit with a string of entries—survey stations, observation posts, automated beacons all reporting the same thing. Yellow flags, not red. Anomaly, not emergency. But the pattern was wrong. The reports ran along a single bearing, a clean line through the Outer Rim that pointed toward a blank spot in the charts.

He zoomed in on three readings near the middle of the chain. The descriptions were clinical—*unclassifiable Force disturbance*, *persistent low-level variance*, *recommend follow-up*—but he didn’t need the words. He knew what the survey teams had been trying to name. The same sour taste in the Force that had been sitting under his ribs for weeks. The same half-beat lag. The same something-off that had refused to leave him alone.

His father had been reading it too.

“You flagged these yourself,” Ben said. The edge in his voice came out sharper than he’d intended.

“I did.” Luke kept his tone level. “I’ve been sitting on them.”

Ben looked up from the pad. The room felt smaller than it had a moment ago. Traffic still moved outside the window, still indifferent, but the air between desk and door had changed. His father was watching him with the patience of a man who had already done the math and was waiting for his son to catch up.

“You felt it,” Ben said.

“Yes.”

The word landed and stayed. Ben set the datapad down on the desk. His hand lingered a beat too long, blue light bleeding through his fingers, the Temple’s power grid humming under the silence.

“How long?” he asked.

“Weeks.” Luke didn’t look away. “The same as you, I suspect.”

Ben heard the difference before he could name it. His father was describing an anomaly the way a Grand Master would—a flagged report, a data point, a problem to be assigned. Ben had been carrying it as a physical thing, a sour note in the field that his body had been trying to correct since the first sparring session it cost him. Luke saw the signal. Ben felt the wound.

The distinction sat between them like a third person in the room.

“It’s not residual echo,” Ben said. “Not from Abeloth.”

“I know.”

“The analysts are wrong.”

“I know that too.” Luke’s mouth shifted into something that wasn’t quite a smile. “That’s why I’m sending you.”

Ben’s jaw tightened. He looked down at the datapad again—the seventeen flags, the bearing, the blank spot at the end of the chain—and felt the pull of it, directional now. Not a diffuse ache. A current.

His father had given him a mission that ran straight along the bearing. That wasn’t coincidence. Luke didn’t do coincidence.

“You could send anyone,” Ben said. “You could send a full team.”

“I could.”

“But you’re not.”

Luke didn’t answer. He didn’t need to. The silence did the work—the same silence that had been working between them since Ben was old enough to understand that his father trusted him with things he didn’t trust anyone else with, and that the trust came wrapped in a distance neither of them had ever managed to close.

Ben pushed back from the desk. He needed to move. The window was three steps away, and he took them, his shoulders settling into a readiness that looked a lot like retreat. The city spread below him, a river of light and motion that had been running for thousands of years without pausing for the Jedi Temple or the people inside it.

“The survey route,” he said. “It lines up.”

“Yes.”

“You planned that.”

Luke’s voice came from behind him, quiet and even. “I planned for the possibility that I wasn’t imagining it. The survey gives you a reason to be out there. The Order needs the data either way. If you find something, you find it. If you don’t, you’ve done your job.”

Ben didn’t turn around. His reflection in the transparisteel was a ghost over the city lights—a young man in Jedi robes, his father’s son, standing alone in an office that had seen a thousand briefings just like this one.

He didn’t ask for a partner. The thought rose and he let it remain unspoken. He knew what operational silence meant. He’d learned it young, in a different uniform, with a different partner at his shoulder. Calling the Order meant calling people who would ask questions he wasn’t ready to answer. Desh wouldn’t ask. Desh would just be there, a steady hand on the controls and a warm absence in the Force, and Ben could carry this without explaining it to anyone who would try to put a name on it.

“I’ll need a pilot,” he said. “Someone who won’t file a lot of questions.”

Luke was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice carried the weight of a man who understood exactly what his son was not saying.

“I assumed you’d have someone in mind.”

Ben turned from the window. His father was still behind the desk, still watching him with that steady patience, still holding the space between them open without pushing into it. The datapad sat dark between them.

“I’ll file the departure manifest in the morning,” Ben said.

Luke nodded once. “Keep me informed.”

“I will.”

Neither of them acknowledged what the silence around the word *partner* meant. Neither of them named the choice Ben had just made—to carry the burden with a man who couldn’t feel it, rather than with the Order that could. The distance between father and son tightened, held, and did not close.

Ben picked up the datapad. It was solid in his hand, the mission parameters already loading, the bearing already set.

“Ben,” Luke said.

Ben stopped at the door.

“Be careful.”

It wasn’t a command. It was the thing his father said when the mission was bigger than the briefing and they both knew it. Ben didn’t turn around.

“Yeah,” he said. “I will.”

The door opened. The traffic noise swelled to meet him. Ben walked out into the corridor with the datapad in his hand and the pull of that bearing already tugging at him like a direction he’d started following before he ever left the room.

## Scene 01.03

Ben’s ship was a modified SoroSuub Starfire, bought used three years ago and maintained by Ben ever since. The cockpit still carried a faint trace of the previous owner’s tabac habit, a ghost no amount of scrubbed air had managed to erase. Ben had stopped noticing it sometime during the second year. Now it just smelled like his ship.

Preflight was complete. The hangar bay tech had signed off ten minutes ago, and the Temple’s departure protocol indicators glowed green across the console. All Ben had to do was file his partner selection and lift.

His thumb slid past the Jedi roster without slowing.

The datapad rested in his lap, angled toward the cockpit lights. Three names waited in the recent-contacts queue—Knights he’d worked with before, competent people who would clear their schedules if he asked. He didn’t open their profiles. He didn’t open the roster at all. His thumb found the personal contacts list and scrolled down to the Ds while his eyes stayed on the preflight readouts, as if the choice had already been made somewhere below conscious thought and his hand was only delivering the news.

Desh Rolan. The entry was two years old and still listed a Kuat exchange code.

Ben opened the comm channel before his mind could catch up. The request pulsed on the console, riding Coruscant’s orbital relay network out toward the Core. He let it spin while he checked the nav-computer’s first-jump calculation—a short hop to the edge of the gravity well before the main hyperspace insertion—and found nothing wrong with it.

The channel answered in a crackle of long-range static.

“Ben Skywalker.” Desh’s voice came through tinny and warm at once, the way long-range comm always did when the relay was old and the signal had bounced through three satellites. “It’s past midnight on Coruscant. You’re either in trouble or about to be.”

“Neither,” Ben said. “I need a pilot.”

A pause. Not hesitation—Desh didn’t hesitate. It was the length of a man taking a sip of caf and deciding whether the conversation was worth setting the mug down for.

“Survey run,” Ben added. “Outer Rim. Should be routine.”

“Should be.” Desh sounded dry enough to strip paint. “The last time you said that, I spent three days in a medbay.”

“That was different.”

“That was a firefight in a fuel depot.”

“And you didn’t spend three days in medbay. You spent two.”

Desh snorted. The static softened the sound, but the warmth under it came through cleanly. “Where and when?”

“Terminus Station. Two days.”

Another pause. This one carried weight—years of it, the years neither of them discussed. The Galactic Alliance Guard had been dissolved for over a decade, but the habits it had carved into both of them were still there, worn smooth by time and never quite gone. Ben had been sixteen when he’d worn the gray uniform. Desh had been older—not old enough to be a mentor, but old enough to know what they were being asked to do and keep his mouth shut until they were off-duty and the barracks door was closed.

Ben didn’t think about the GAG often. He didn’t let himself. But Desh’s voice on a long-range comm at midnight dragged it back anyway—the narrow corridors, the rules that only made sense if you didn’t stare at them too hard, the understanding that the Order had built something it didn’t know how to clean up after.

Desh knew what it meant when a Jedi Knight didn’t call his own Order for backup. He didn’t ask why Ben hadn’t opened the roster.

“I’ll clear my schedule,” Desh said.

Ben’s shoulders eased a fraction. He hadn’t realized they’d been up. “I’ll send you the coordinates.”

“Send me a caf recommendation too. Terminus has terrible food.”

“Everything on Terminus is terrible. That’s why I picked it.”

“Considerate.” The channel clicked as Desh shifted something on his end—a datapad, probably, the sound of a man already pulling up his calendar. “Two days. Don’t get shot before I get there.”

“I’ll try.”

“Don’t try. Just don’t get shot.”

The channel closed. Ben sat in the cockpit with the preflight readouts still green and the partner roster still unopened and the wrongness in the Force sitting under his ribs like a stone he’d swallowed and couldn’t cough up.

He filed the departure manifest without listing a partner name. The form accepted it—the Temple’s administrative system didn’t require a second body for survey runs, only recommended one. Recommendations weren’t orders.

The hangar bay doors irised open above him. Coruscant’s night sky was a smear of city glow and orbital traffic, the stars hidden behind the light haze of a planet that hadn’t known true darkness in twenty thousand years. Ben’s ship rose on repulsors, hangar echoes fading as the landing struts retracted and the hull cleared the Temple’s outer perimeter.

He angled toward orbit. The Temple shrank in the rear display—a ziggurat of ancient stone and modern durasteel that had been the center of his life since he was old enough to walk its corridors. From altitude, it looked small. Everything did.

The wrongness didn’t diminish.

That was the first thing Ben noticed. The sensation in the Force should have eased as he left the Temple behind—disturbances tied to a location stayed with the location. But this one didn’t. It held steady through the atmospheric climb, through orbital insertion, through the nav-computer’s countdown to the first jump. The sour taste in the field didn’t fade. It sharpened.

Ben’s hands moved through the jump sequence on muscle memory. The nav-computer chimed readiness. The hyperdrive hummed up through the deck plates, a vibration he’d felt a thousand times before and barely registered anymore.

The stars stretched.

Hyperspace swallowed the ship. The blue-white tunnel of the alternate dimension closed around the hull, and the normal-space sensor feeds went dead, replaced by the steady pulse of the hyperdrive indicators. Everything was nominal. Everything was routine.

The wrongness stayed.

Ben’s hands tightened on the controls. The pressure didn’t just hold—it took on weight. Direction. It tugged at him the way a compass needle pulls toward magnetic north, a pull he felt in his chest and behind his eyes and somewhere deeper than either.

The Force did not work this way. Disturbances faded with distance. They didn’t follow ships through hyperspace. They didn’t pull.

This one did.

Ben’s breath caught. He stared at the hyperspace tunnel beyond the viewport, the endless blue-white stream of a dimension with no geography and no direction except the one the nav-computer assigned it. But the disturbance had a direction now. It had a bearing. It was drawing him toward something specific—something waiting at the end of the survey route his father had given him, the route that ran straight along that bearing, the route Luke had chosen for the possibility that he wasn’t imagining it.

The pressure stopped being a problem.

It became a destination.
