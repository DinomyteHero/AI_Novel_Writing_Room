# Chapter 6

## Scene 06.01

The common area smelled of caf gone stale an hour ago and the sharp ozone tang of a ship pushing its life-support harder than it liked. Desh was already at the table when Ben ducked through the hatch, datapad in hand, looking like he’d been up before the shift alarm.

“We have a name,” Desh said.

Ben stopped halfway through the hatch. Across the table, Sera kept her eyes on the archive materials spread before her. Her hands continued their neat sorting—the same precise rhythm she’d had since the fortress—but her shoulders had gone rigid.

Desh set the datapad on the table and angled it toward the room. The reconstructed monitoring file had cleaned itself into readable lines, the corrupted packets stitched back together. The first clear line in the header read: *Chamber Seven — Primary Occupant — Darth Veraine.*

“Darth,” Ben said. The word landed heavier than he expected.

“Brotherhood of Darkness,” Desh said. “Alchemist. Strategist. Gavran flagged her as high-risk.” He tapped the display. “She didn’t just break out. She ate through the chamber from the inside. Took weeks. The system logged the damage in real time, and nobody was there to watch it.”

Ben’s mind was already moving—vectors, timing, threat angles, every assumption from the last two days shifting under his feet. The name changed the shape of the hunt. A Brotherhood alchemist with weeks of lead time and a thousand years of patience wasn’t just ahead of them. She was building something.

He pulled out the chair across from Desh and sat. His body had already gone into readiness without asking him—weight forward, hands flat on the table, the posture his mother had taught him before he was old enough to know why.

Sera still hadn’t spoken.

The silence stretched. Torin, standing near the galley with his arms crossed in that old-soldier way of his—close enough to help, never in the way—watched Sera with an expression Ben couldn’t read. Kael had folded himself into the corner by the nav display, hands loose at his sides, face blank enough to be a wall.

“Sera,” Ben said.

She looked up. Her composure held—the same blade-edged discipline she’d worn from the moment she woke—but she didn’t fill the gap between them. She left it there, and Ben understood she was waiting to see what he asked before deciding what she’d give.

“You know her,” Ben said. Not a question.

“I know of her.” Each word came out measured, set exactly where she wanted it. “She was captured at the Ryloth front. Gavran assessed her personally. He classified her as—” She stopped. Her jaw tightened, the smallest motion, gone almost before Ben caught it. “He classified her as a strategic asset.”

“Meaning what?”

Sera met his eyes. “Meaning he believed she was too valuable to execute and too dangerous to contain by ordinary means. So he made her an anchor instead.”

The word settled into the recycled air like a chunk of durasteel. Ben thought of the chamber on Level 3—the claw marks in the alloy, the peeled-back metal, the patient destruction of a prison built to last forever. Veraine had been awake in there. Partly aware, Desh had said. Feeling her Force-presence drained away as structural support for a thousand years.

And then she had decided to leave.

Kael moved. Only a shift of weight, his shoulders settling against the bulkhead, but Ben caught it the way a sensor caught an outlier. Kael’s face flickered. Something crossed it when Sera said the name, recognition there and gone, smoothed over into the studied blankness he wore like armor.

Ben went still. He didn’t need to analyze it. The fact was there, plain as a sensor reading: Kael Drenn knew Darth Veraine, and in the space of a heartbeat he’d chosen not to let anyone see that he knew.

Ben filed it away. He didn’t press. He didn’t forget.

Torin cleared his throat. The sound was warm and easy, like most things Torin did until they weren’t. “Well,” he said, “at least now we know who we’re chasing. That’s better than chasing a shape.”

“It’s better,” Desh said, “if you assume knowing the name tells you what she’ll do next.”

“Does it?” Ben asked Sera.

She didn’t answer. Her hands had stopped sorting. She was looking at the datapad, at the name in the header, and her silence answered for her—a warning that whatever Veraine could do, Sera wasn’t going to spell it out in front of people she’d known for less than three days.

Torin shifted his weight, filling the silence without breaking it. “The Brotherhood trained their strategists to think in campaigns, not battles. If Veraine’s moving along the Corridor, she isn’t running. She’s executing a plan she’s had a thousand years to refine.” He paused. “We should assume she knows we’re coming.”

“Why?” Desh asked.

“Because she’s a strategist. Strategists don’t assume an escape goes unnoticed.” Torin’s smile was thin, but real. “They assume pursuit and prepare for it. It’s what I’d do.”

The room absorbed that. Ben felt everyone’s attention shift—not fracture, exactly, but realign around the weight of the name, the way a ship adjusted its load to a new vector. They were no longer chasing an escaped anchor. They were chasing Darth Veraine, Brotherhood alchemist, with weeks of lead time and a plan she’d been sharpening since before the Republic existed.

Sera stood. The movement was economical, all clean lines and no waste. She crossed to the cockpit access ladder without a word. Ben watched her go, tracking the tension in her shoulders, the way her hand reached for the rung and stopped.

She had reached for a control that wasn’t there. Her fingers closed on blank hull plating where a console used to be—a gesture so automatic she seemed not to notice it. Her body remembered a ship that hadn’t flown in a thousand years.

Ben looked away before she could catch him staring. The motion was too private, too raw, and all at once he understood what they were asking these people to do. They’d woken in a galaxy that had moved on without them, aboard a ship built by a civilization they didn’t know, hunting an enemy who’d had weeks to prepare while they were still learning where the galley was.

Sera’s hand dropped from the hull. She turned back to the room, and her composure settled into place again—the blade-edge discipline, the soldier’s mask. But something in her posture had changed. She was looking at Ben with an expression he hadn’t seen before.

“The Corridor route,” she said. “You’re following the anchor nodes.”

“Yes.”

“Veraine will expect that. She knows the network better than we do—she’s had weeks to study it while we’ve been catching up.” Sera folded her arms. “What’s your alternative when she forces us off the expected path?”

It was a test. Ben recognized it the way he’d recognized his mother’s tests—the question that wasn’t really a question, the challenge tucked inside the words. Sera was asking him to prove he’d thought beyond the obvious answer.

“The anchor nodes are the only reliable navigation points through uncharted space,” Ben said. “If we leave the chain, we’re flying blind. So we don’t leave it. We use it differently.”

“How?”

“She’s expecting pursuit along the nodes in sequence. One, two, three. What she won’t expect is somebody who knows a modern hyperdrive can skip a node if the vector is clean enough.” Ben dragged the nav display toward him and rotated it so Sera could see. “The *Steady Returns* has a Class One hyperdrive and a nav computer that’s been mapping these anomalies since we entered the Corridor. If Veraine’s laying traps at the expected stops, we go around them. Hit the nodes she isn’t defending and work backward.”

Sera studied the display. Her expression didn’t change, but something in her stance eased—a half degree, maybe less.

“That could work,” she said.

It wasn’t praise. It wasn’t even approval. But it was the first time she’d looked at him as though he might be worth following.

Torin found him in the corridor afterward. Ben had stepped out to check the sensor relay—or that was the excuse he gave himself. Really, he’d needed thirty seconds of quiet to sort through the way the room had rearranged itself around Veraine’s name, the way Kael had gone blank, the way Sera had tested him and found him not entirely useless.

“Your father’s name carries weight even in our time,” Torin said.

Ben turned. Torin leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, wearing the same easy posture he’d had since the fortress. But his eyes were sharper than the rest of him—measuring, the way a commander measured another commander’s work.

“Luke Skywalker,” Torin said. “Gavran spoke of him. Not by name—we didn’t have the name—but he knew the bloodline would continue. He believed someone would come.” He paused. “He was right.”

The compliment felt genuine. Torin’s warmth always did, which made it hard to read. But underneath it, faint as a sensor ghost, Ben caught something else—an appraisal that hadn’t been spoken, a judgment left hanging in the air.

“Thank you,” Ben said.

Torin clasped his shoulder. The grip was measured—a commander’s grip, the kind that carried approval and expectation in equal measure. Then he let go and headed back toward the common area, leaving Ben alone in the corridor with the hum of the hyperdrive and the weight of a name he was only beginning to understand.

## Scene 06.02

The cargo corridor was cold this early in the ship’s cycle, the life-support still climbing out of its night setting. Ben had come aft to check a sensor relay that had flickered twice during the last watch—minor enough that Desh didn’t need waking, annoying enough to deserve thirty seconds of attention.

He stopped three paces from the corner.

Kael Drenn stood in the open space between the cargo lockers and the engineering crawlspace hatch, moving through a sequence Ben didn’t recognize. It wasn’t a combat form; the motions were too small, too contained, wrists and shoulders and spine doing the work instead of full-body extension. Kael’s eyes were open but unfocused, fixed on a point in the bulkhead that wasn’t there. His breathing ran in a pattern Ben couldn’t place: two short inhales, one long exhale, then a pause of exactly three heartbeats before it began again.

Then it ended. Kael’s hands dropped to his sides, his weight settled, and his face returned to the same studied blankness he wore in the common area. The whole thing had taken maybe forty seconds.

Ben stepped back before Kael could turn. The sensor relay could wait. Some things got broken if you acknowledged them wrong, and Ben had learned young—from his mother, from the GAG, from every hard lesson that had taught him to read a room—that private rituals people built to survive bad systems weren’t meant for witnesses.

He went back toward the common area with the image still hot behind his eyes: the precision of it, the economy, the way Kael had moved like someone who’d learned that doing one thing exactly right every morning was the only way to keep the rest of the day from killing you.

---

The common area smelled of field rations that tasted like nothing any of the survivors remembered. Desh had laid out the breakfast packets with his usual practical efficiency—no ceremony, just fuel arranged where people could reach it. Sera sat at the table with her back to the bulkhead, posture unchanged from the fortress: spine straight, hands visible, sightlines clear to both exits. Torin had taken the corner by the galley, nursing a cup of caf with the air of a man who’d been awake for hours and saw no reason to advertise it.

Kael arrived last, slipping into the corner by the nav display without a sound. His face gave nothing away. Ben didn’t look at him any longer than he looked at anyone else.

“The Reformations,” Sera said. She hadn’t touched her rations. “You said they were penance. I want to know what the galaxy was told they were for.”

Not a question. A demand, delivered in the clipped formal register she wore like armor. Ben pulled up the galactic history archive on his datapad—the public version, the one every student on Coruscant learned from—and slid it across the table to Sera.

“The official story,” he said. “The Republic centralized after Ruusan. The Jedi Order was restructured. The military was disbanded. The galaxy was told it was about preventing another Army of Light.”

Sera’s eyes moved down the screen. She read without reacting, the same blade-edged discipline she’d had from the moment she woke, but her hands had gone still on the table. Not attention. Holding very tight to something that wanted to move.

“The Republic didn’t centralize,” she said. “It conquered. The Outer Rim was annexed. The Jedi Lords were abolished and their territories absorbed. The military wasn’t disbanded—it was replaced with a Judicial Department that answered to the Core.” She looked up. “The galaxy was told a lie.”

“Yes.”

The word settled into the small space like a shut door. Sera didn’t flinch. She set the datapad down with the same precision she brought to everything, and then she went quiet in a way that made the quiet feel like a third person at the table.

Torin broke it first. “A thousand years of peace built on a lie,” he said, his tone almost conversational. “That’s not the worst foundation I’ve seen. The Sith built empires on worse.”

Ben caught himself watching Torin’s ease the way he’d caught himself watching Kael’s discipline—the reflex to look for what lay under it. Torin had taken the Reformations answer with the same warmth he brought to everything, and that warmth was starting to feel like a door Ben couldn’t see through.

Desh snorted and poured himself more caf. “The Sith also lost,” he said. “So maybe we don’t use them as the benchmark.”

Torin laughed—a real laugh, warm and easy—and Ben felt the room loosen a fraction. Desh had that effect. He’d had it in the GAG too, the ability to cut tension without cutting anyone down, and Ben was grateful for it in a way he didn’t know how to say.

Kael hadn’t moved from his corner. He still hadn’t spoken. He was watching the room the way Ben had watched him in the cargo corridor—tracking, filing, giving nothing back.

“What about the Sith worlds?” Kael asked.

The question came out flat, almost idle, the way Kael said everything. But Ben had seen the morning discipline. He knew what economy looked like on this man, and the question was too casual by half.

“Which ones?” Ben asked.

“The ones the Brotherhood took conscripts from.” Kael’s eyes met his. “The small ones. The ones nobody wrote histories about.”

Ben pulled up the database. The query took longer than it should have—the nav computer had to reach through three archival layers to find records that old, and the connection through the thin place was patchy. The screen flickered. Then it resolved.

Nothing.

He ran the search again. The parameters were right—the sector, the era, the Brotherhood’s known recruitment patterns. The database had records for the major systems, the fortress worlds, the battle sites that had made it into the histories. But the planet Kael was asking about wasn’t there. It had been too small, too rural, too insignificant to leave a mark on a thousand years of galactic record.

“There’s nothing,” Ben said. “The planet was too small for surviving records.”

Kael’s expression didn’t change. Not a flicker, not a tightening, not the tiny shift Ben had learned to read in the cargo corridor. But the line of his shoulders did something Ben couldn’t name—a settling, a small adjustment, the movement of a body absorbing weight it had expected and still wasn’t ready for.

The silence after that was different from Sera’s. Sera’s silence was a wall. Kael’s was a room with the door closed, and Ben could feel it taking up more space than Kael’s body did.

He didn’t speak. There was nothing to say. The database had answered, and the answer was erasure—a homeworld so completely gone that even its name had been lost, and Kael was the only one left in the galaxy who remembered it.

Desh set his caf down. He didn’t look at Kael—Desh was too good at reading rooms to stare at someone who’d just been gutted—but he pushed the ration packets half an inch closer to Kael’s side of the table. A small motion. Barely there. The kind of thing you did for someone you couldn’t help with words.

Sera’s stillness had changed. She was looking at Kael now, and her expression had shifted into something Ben hadn’t seen before—not pity, not quite, but recognition. She knew what it cost to lose a world. She’d lost hers too, even if it still had a name in the records.

Torin cleared his throat. “The Brotherhood took conscripts from a dozen worlds,” he said, and his voice was gentler than Ben had heard it. “Most of them were small. Agricultural. Places the Sith didn’t think mattered except as recruitment pools.” He paused. “I was stationed near one of them for six months. The people there didn’t care about the war. They just wanted to be left alone.”

It wasn’t an answer. It wasn’t comfort. But it was something—a shared history, a witness, a reminder that Kael’s world had existed even if the galaxy had forgotten it.

Kael didn’t respond. He reached for one of the ration packets, opened it with the same economical precision he’d brought to the morning discipline, and began to eat. The motion was so ordinary, so deliberately normal, that Ben felt his chest tighten.

The conversation didn’t resume. The common area had become smaller, the bulkheads closer, the recycled air thicker. But the silence was different now. It wasn’t five people avoiding each other. It was five people who had just registered what a thousand years had cost, who didn’t have words for it yet, and who were still sitting at the same table anyway.

Torin caught Ben’s eye across the common area. He nodded—a single measured motion, the nod of a commander acknowledging a subordinate who’d handled something difficult well. There was approval in it, or something close enough to pass for approval. There was also assessment, the look of a commander deciding where someone fit in a structure that hadn’t been built yet.

Ben nodded back. He wasn’t sure what he was agreeing to. He wasn’t sure whether he should be reassured that Torin was already thinking in those terms, or concerned that the group’s fragile empathy was already being threaded through a command structure none of them had asked for.

The hyperdrive hummed. The rations sat half-eaten. Kael’s silence still filled more of the room than his body did, but the others were sitting inside it now instead of around it, and that was something.

It wasn’t enough. Ben knew that. But it was what they had.

## Scene 06.03

The cockpit of the *Steady Returns* had never felt cramped. It did now, with all of them packed around the nav display and the blue glow of the Corridor route washing over faces Ben had just finished seeing at their worst.

Desh had the display up in a clean, hard-edged hologram above the console. Fourteen nodes hung in the air, each marked with the same practical precision he brought to everything. Red for dead. Amber for degraded. Green for active. One node near the far end of the chain pulsed white, a steady flash that meant recent activity and nothing more useful than that.

“Three green,” Desh said, tapping the air beside the display. “The rest are dead or barely reading. The white one was visited in the last seventy-two hours. Someone’s moving.”

Ben’s attention narrowed to the map. The problem had a shape now. Fourteen points, one route, and a clear sign that they weren’t the first ones through. Beneath the clean lines of the projection, unease tugged at him. This wasn’t just a damaged seal. Veraine had been here.

Sera leaned forward in the copilot’s seat, eyes tracking the node positions. She’d been quiet since the common area, still carrying the Reformations answer in the room like an object none of them had found a place for yet. The map drew her back into herself. Ben saw it in the straightening of her back, the shift from hurt to evaluation, the discipline clicking into place like a latched blade.

“Fourteen nodes,” she said. “Spiral pattern, centered on Veranthos. The seal’s architecture is radial—each node pushes pressure outward from the center.” She traced the route with one finger, blue light spilling across her knuckles. “If we check each node in order, we can see the pattern of degradation before we commit to the center. We don’t run blind into a system that’s been failing for a thousand years.”

Torin, still in the doorway, shook his head. He had the same loose stance he’d worn in the common area, but the easy warmth in his voice had gone thin. “Systematic assessment takes time we don’t have. Veraine’s already hit one node in the last three days. She’s ahead of us, and every hour we spend examining dead relays is an hour she uses to get farther ahead.” He nodded at the flashing white node. “We go straight at Veranthos. The seal’s heart is where this ends. Everything else is delay.”

“Delay is information,” Sera said, not looking at him. “Charging straight at the center without understanding the pattern is how you walk into a trap.”

“It’s also how you catch someone before she finishes what she started.”

Their voices overlapped, Sera clipped and exact, Torin faster now, urgency gathering behind each word. Ben felt the cockpit tighten around them. Not the Force—not yet—just the human strain of two people who were both right, and who could not both get their way.

Kael spoke from the corner by the nav display.

“We don’t know why she’s moving.”

The words landed flat and clean, the way Kael’s often did. Not an argument. Not a vote. Just the missing piece nobody else had said aloud. Sera stopped mid-gesture. Torin’s jaw flexed.

Kael didn’t add anything else. He didn’t need to. Veraine had been visiting nodes, but none of them knew what she was doing there. Torin’s route assumed she was driving for the center. Sera’s route assumed there was a pattern to read first. Both were bets placed on a woman whose purpose they still couldn’t see.

Ben’s shoulders tightened. The display still glowed between them, the fourteen nodes waiting, and the three voices in the cockpit had stopped being ideas and started being a decision he had to make. Sera wanted caution. Torin wanted speed. Kael had pointed at the hole in both plans and left the rest to him, which was as close as Kael came to saying it.

Ben wanted the clean answer his father’s Jedi Order always seemed to find in the stories. But this wasn’t a story, and there wasn’t a single right line through it. Synthesis meant choosing one path and living with the fact that the other two had reasons to exist.

Desh glanced at him from the pilot’s seat. The look said he’d seen officers make harder calls with less to work on. No push. Just readiness.

Ben reached for the nav computer.

“Sera’s route,” he said.

Torin started to speak.

“Because we don’t know why she’s moving.”

Torin shut his mouth.

Ben keyed in the coordinates. The computer took them with a soft chime, and the route settled into the display—Sera’s methodical arc through the Corridor, node by node, building a picture before the final run at Veranthos. The white node gave one last pulse and folded into the sequence.

“We check the nodes,” Ben said. “We gather data. We read the pattern.” He looked at Torin. “If Veraine’s doing something we haven’t guessed, rushing past her work to get to the center won’t catch her. It just means we arrive ignorant.”

Torin held his gaze for a beat longer than comfort allowed. Then he nodded, measured and reluctant and still somehow steady—the same kind of nod he’d given Ben in the common area, approval and assessment packed into one movement.

“Your call,” Torin said. Some of the warmth had come back into his voice, but not all of it. “I hope you’re right.”

Sera didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. The route was her plan, but Ben had named Kael’s reason out loud, and the two of them had become something neither had built alone. She was looking at him with an expression he couldn’t pin down. Not gratitude. Not quite respect. More like surprise, sharpened by calculation.

Kael had already turned back to the display, face unreadable. But his shoulders had eased a fraction.

The pressure in the cockpit eased with them. Not gone—Torin’s urgency was still there, Sera’s caution still there, and the agreement between them was held together by the reasoning Ben had spoken aloud. Still, they were moving. That mattered.

The course shifted. The *Steady Returns* angled toward the first node, the hyperdrive humming under Ben’s feet. He set his attention on the space ahead and reached for the Force, looking for whatever waited there.

The Force answered from a step farther off than it should.

It wasn’t pain. It wasn’t a voice. It was a gap, a place where continuity should have been and wasn’t. Like putting your weight on a stair that sat a fraction too far forward. The sense of it ahead was thin, like fabric worn through.

Ben held the feeling for a second, then let it rest where it was. No interpretation. No diagnosis. Just the fact of it. He would have time later, if there was a later, to decide what it meant.

For now, he had a route to live with.

And if it was wrong, the failure would be his.
