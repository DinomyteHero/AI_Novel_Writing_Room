# Chapter 3

## Scene 03.01

The third world looked wrong before they broke atmosphere.

Ben felt it through the hull while Desh was still working the approach vector—a weight low in his chest, denser than the uneasy pull he’d been tracking for weeks. This wasn’t ambient. It was close, as if the planet itself had leaned in and pressed a thumb against his sternum to see how he’d react.

“Readings?” he asked.

Desh didn’t take his eyes off the console. “Atmosphere’s breathable. Gravity’s a little light—point nine three standard. Temperature’s cold, but nothing hostile.” He paused. “And there’s power.”

Ben looked up. “Power.”

“Yeah.” Desh’s tone stayed flat, the way it did when something refused to make sense. “Not much. Not a settlement. But something down there is drawing current.”

Ben studied the sensor display. The blip was small and steady, tucked in a valley between two ridges of dark rock. Their bearing ran straight through it.

“That’s our survey point,” he said.

“I know.”

“There shouldn’t be power at our survey point.”

“I know that, too.”

They set down a kilometer out, on a flat stretch of hardpan that crackled under the *Steady Returns*’ landing gear like old bone. Outside, the air was thin and dry, carrying a mineral bite—silica dust, maybe, or something ground finer than sand over a very long time. The sky overhead was the color of a bruise in the process of healing.

From the ramp, the structure was already visible. It sat half-buried in the ridge face, dark stone cut into angles that didn’t belong to the surrounding geology. No collapsed walls. No weather-eaten edges. The lines were clean.

“That’s not ruins,” Desh said.

“No.”

“That’s a bunker.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was already moving, one hand near his blaster but not on it, his Force-sense stretching ahead toward the dark stone and the hum buried beneath it. The weight in his chest tightened with each step. Not hostile. Not overtly. More like standing too close to something vast that hadn’t noticed him yet.

Desh came up beside him, scanner in hand, jacket zipped against the cold. “Power cells. Deep ones. The readings say this place has been running a long time.”

“How long?”

“Longer than it ought to.”

The entrance was a recessed slab set into the ridge face, seamless except for a hairline seam tracing its perimeter. No handle. No visible mechanism. Just dark stone, that fine line, and the vibration of power rising through Ben’s boots.

He stopped three meters short of the door. The thing in his chest had become heavier, specific and patient, like a hand resting flat against his ribs.

“Ben,” Desh said quietly. “Your chrono.”

Ben glanced down. The readout on his wrist was flickering—not dead, just drifting, the seconds moving forward and then stuttering back as if time itself couldn’t decide which way to run.

“Mine’s two seconds fast,” Desh said. “Has been since we hit atmosphere.”

Ben looked at the door. The seam was glowing now, a faint blue line around the perimeter, and the hum had risen into something he could feel in his teeth.

“It knows we’re here,” he said.

The blue light detached from the seam and reached for him.

It wasn’t a beam. It was a pulse, a cool wash that crawled over his body from shoulders to boots and back again, lingering at his chest, his temples, the base of his skull. Ben held still. The scan felt like a hand reading a text it hadn’t seen in centuries—checking each line, pausing where something didn’t quite match what it had expected, then moving on with something that felt almost like reluctance.

Four seconds passed. It felt longer.

Then the door split down the center and slid open, silent on tracks that hadn’t moved in longer than the Republic had existed.

Desh let out a slow breath. “What was that?”

“Force-recognition.” Ben’s voice came out rougher than he intended. “It was checking my signature.”

“Did you pass?”

“It let me in.” He stepped through the opening. “I don’t think it liked doing it.”

The interior stayed dark for exactly as long as it took Ben’s boot to touch the stone floor. Then the lights came on—not all at once, but in sequence, a wave of pale blue illumination rolling down the corridor like a held breath finally released. Displays flickered to life in the wall consoles. The power cells’ hum deepened, steadied, became a thrum he could feel through the floor plates.

The air smelled of dust and old metal and something else—ozone, faintly, the way a lightsaber smelled after a long sparring session.

Desh had his sidearm out before the light finished spreading. He swept the corridor from left to right in one smooth motion, checking angles the way Ben had seen him do a hundred times. “This is military.”

“I know.”

“Command-and-control. The layout, the console placement, the sight lines—” He gestured with the blaster. “Somebody built this to run a campaign from.”

Ben moved to the nearest console. The display cycled through characters he didn’t recognize—not Aurebesh, not anything the Order had taught him, something older and sharper, all hard angles where modern writing curved. The message had been running so long the pattern seemed worn into the screen like a scar.

“Can you read it?” Desh asked.

“No.”

“But you know what it is.”

Ben touched the console. The characters flickered under his fingertips, and the thrum in the floor shifted. It stopped being steady and started cycling, repeating, a message trying to force itself through a frequency nobody used anymore.

“It’s been broadcasting,” he said. “All this time. On channels nobody monitors.”

The characters stuttered. Changed. Settled again.

Ben watched them resolve, the angular strokes softening into letters he knew, archaic but readable, the Basic of an era long before the Order he served.

Two words.

SEAL FAILING.

The message cycled. SEAL FAILING. Cycled again. SEAL FAILING.

Ben’s breath caught. The weight on his chest turned into pressure that pinned him where he stood, one hand still on the console, his eyes fixed on the display and the two words repeating over and over. He read them. He did not yet know what they protected against. The meaning hadn’t landed. Only the alarm had—the hard knowledge that something built before the Republic had been waiting all this time for someone to hear it.

“Ben,” Desh said. His voice had gone very quiet. “What does that mean?”

Ben didn’t answer. The message cycled again. SEAL FAILING.

The wrongness wasn’t ambient anymore. It was the sound of something breaking, and it had been breaking for centuries while no one listened.

## Scene 03.02

The data core wasn’t a room so much as a vault—circular, maybe eight meters across, ringed with consoles that had gone dark centuries before the facility’s power grid woke up and remembered its job. Emergency strips glowed pale blue along the floor seams, enough to work by, nowhere near enough to feel safe.

Desh had already found the access panel. It sat knee-high in the wall, its surface pocked with corrosion from air that hadn’t moved in a thousand years. He was down on one knee, working the edge with a flat tool Ben didn’t recognize, his face blank in the way it got when something resisted him and he’d already decided it was going to lose.

“Whoever built this didn’t want anyone pulling data out of it later,” Desh said. The tool scraped metal, found a gap, then bit. “Panel’s welded itself shut. The metal’s fused.”

“Can you get through it?”

“Give me a minute.”

Ben stood in the center of the vault and let the cold settle around him. The air was so dry his breath came out in white wisps that vanished almost at once in the blue light. The power cells hummed deeper here than in the corridor—not louder, just heavier, a vibration that climbed through his boots and lodged in his bones. Beneath it, the Force carried something else. A record-keeping residue. Not a threat. Not a warning. Just the sense that these walls had been paying attention for a very long time and hadn’t lost a thing.

The panel came free with a crack that rang off the stone. Desh set it aside and peered into the opening.

“Storage media,” he said. “Old. Not crystal—something earlier. Solid-state, maybe, or whatever they used before solid-state.” He reached into his jacket and brought out a data coupler, the universal adapter that had gotten him through half a dozen planetary customs offices and twice as many locked doors. “I’m going to have to guess at the interface. If I miss, we lose the data.”

“How badly can you miss?”

“Badly enough that nothing happens. Or badly enough that the storage bricks itself and we get nothing.” He was already fitting the coupler into the gap, his hands moving with practiced economy, the motions of someone who’d done this in worse places and under worse pressure. “Ready?”

“Do it.”

The connection sparked once—a brief flare of blue-white light that sharpened Desh’s face—and then again, a second arc that jumped from the coupler to the console housing and left a scorch mark on the stone. The hum in the floor stuttered. Came back. Held.

Desh exhaled. “That was the bad kind of spark.”

“Is it holding now?”

“Looks like it.” He rose, brushed dust from his knees, and studied the nearest console. “Connection’s stable. Data’s moving. Give it a second to—”

The displays came on.

Not one at a time. All at once, a ring of pale blue light washed the vault in old-star color. The central projector—a circular plate set into the floor that Ben hadn’t clocked as a projector until it lit—threw a holographic star map into the air above it. The map was incomplete, gaps going dark where the data had degraded, but the surviving sections were sharp enough to read.

Ben stepped closer. It didn’t match any chart he knew. The stars were where they should be—the coreward drift recognizable, the sweep of the Outer Rim there—but someone had drawn lines across them that didn’t belong to any modern navigation system. Routes. Connections. A network.

“That’s not a survey map,” Desh said.

“No.”

“That’s military.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was reading the geometry of it—the way the lines converged, the way the installations sat. Not randomly. Not even defensively. They formed a perimeter, nodes spaced at intervals that made no sense unless you were trying to hold something in. At the center of the ring, where every line met, was a single point. A fortress.

The pressure in his chest tightened. It had been there since they entered the facility, a weight in the back of everything, the sense of something huge holding still. Now it had edges. The lines on the map weren’t just routes. They were a fence. And the fence pointed at something.

“Command-and-control architecture,” Desh said. He’d moved to the console nearest the door and was reading its display with the flat, rapid focus of someone trained to pull tactical meaning out of unfamiliar systems. “The node placement, the routing, the way the data flows back to the center—somebody built this to run an operation. A big one. Multiple installations reporting to a central authority, all feeding the same decision point.” He glanced up. “This isn’t a listening post. It’s part of a network.”

“How many installations?”

“I count twelve nodes on the overlay. Nine are dark—no power readings, no active data feed. Three are still live.” He paused. “Including this one.”

Ben traced the perimeter with his eyes. The installations formed a ring around the central fortress, spaced evenly, each one a point on a circumference stretching across a sector’s worth of empty space. The scale was wrong for anything the modern Order would build. This wasn’t a listening network. It wasn’t a research array. It was a cage.

He didn’t say that. The word wanted to surface—containment—but saying it would mean admitting he was already building a theory, and the theory would lead to questions he wasn’t ready to ask. He let the map stay a map. Geometry. Data. Facts that didn’t need interpretation yet.

“Can you pull the navigation data?” he asked.

“Already copying.” Desh’s fingers moved across the console, bringing up a secondary display. “Route coordinates, installation schematics, whatever’s left of the comm logs. Most of it’s degraded—the storage medium’s been living on residual power longer than the Republic’s been around, and it’s losing bits every time it cycles. But the core nav data’s intact. Someone wanted the routes to survive.”

The central projector flickered. The map shifted, zooming in on the fortress, and a string of coordinates resolved in the air beneath it—a destination marker in a script Ben didn’t recognize, with a translation overlay the system had generated automatically. The coordinates pointed to a region of space that wasn’t on any modern chart.

“Uncharted,” Desh said. “No hyperspace lane. No Republic survey. Nothing.” He went quiet for a beat. “Somebody erased it.”

“Not erased.” Ben watched the coordinates cycle. “Hidden. There’s a difference.”

“Is there?”

“Erasing means you don’t want anyone to find it. Hiding means you don’t want anyone to look.”

Desh considered that, then reached into his jacket again and pulled out a datapad, syncing it to the console with the same casual efficiency he used for everything he wasn’t supposed to be doing in front of polite company. “I’m pulling everything. Coordinates, route data, whatever’s left of the comm logs. If there’s a signature on any of this, I want it.”

The comm logs were first to resolve. Most were too degraded to read—fragments of text in the same angular script, cycling too fast for the translation overlay to catch. But one entry held. The system had preserved it, or the operator who wrote it had flagged it for preservation, and the translation came through clean.

Authorization: Gavran. Status: active. Seal integrity nominal. Next scheduled inspection: —

The date field was blank. The log had been set to repeat, and it had been repeating for a thousand years without anyone updating it.

“Gavran,” Ben said. The name felt strange in his mouth—old, weighted, belonging to a history he’d never been taught.

“Commanding officer, maybe. Or the architect.” Desh was reading over his shoulder. “Whoever he was, he signed off on this network. And he’s been dead a very long time.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was looking at the map again—the ring of installations, the central fortress, the routes connecting them in a pattern that was starting to feel less like infrastructure and more like a sentence he couldn’t quite read. The pressure in his chest had sharpened into something else. Not pain. Not warning. Just the weight of a thing that had been waiting for someone to notice it.

“Coordinates are on the nav computer,” Desh said. “Route’s plotted. The *Steady Returns* can make the jump whenever you give the word.”

Ben copied the data to his own datapad. The transfer took three seconds. The destination marker blinked on the display—a point in deep uncharted space, surrounded by nothing, linked to the network by a single thin line the map had preserved for a thousand years.

The fortress was waiting.

And Ben was the one who’d found it.

## Scene 03.03

Ben had typed the report three times.

The first draft was clinical—survey notes, sensor logs, a dry account of the perimeter facility that read like every report he’d ever filed and meant absolutely nothing. He deleted it before the third paragraph. The second was honest—the seal, the warning, the thing he’d felt without finding a name for it—and it sat in the buffer for eleven minutes while he stared at the comm panel and felt the Force tug toward coordinates that weren’t on any chart. He deleted that one too.

The third version was still on the screen. A compromise. Enough detail to satisfy protocol, enough omission to leave him room to move. The cursor blinked at the end of a sentence about anomalous sensor readings. He hadn’t mentioned the Force. He hadn’t mentioned the seal.

He highlighted the draft. His finger hovered over the delete command.

“You’re not sending that.”

Desh’s voice came from the cockpit hatch, low and flat. Ben didn’t turn. The copilot’s chair creaked as Desh settled into it, and then came the silence that meant he was reading over Ben’s shoulder, the same way he’d once read threat assessments and mission briefs when they’d both worn gray and pretended they were old enough for the work.

“No,” Ben said. “Not yet.”

The word landed between them and changed the shape of the cockpit. The pressure sitting in Ben’s chest—unremarked, easy enough to ignore while he was busy deleting drafts—tightened into something with edges.

Desh didn’t ask why. He didn’t ask what Ben was waiting for. He looked at the draft, then at Ben, his expression going still in that unreadable way it got when he recognized a pattern before anyone named it.

Then he reached across the console and brought up the nav computer.

The fortress coordinates were still in the buffer from the data-core download—a string of numbers pointing to a blank spot on every chart. Desh pulled them up without asking permission, his hands moving with the same practiced economy he’d used on the access panel in the vault. He paused with his fingers over the entry key.

“These?”

Ben looked at the coordinates. The Force pulled again, not as a haze at the back of his mind but as a hard, directional tug, like a current finding a gap in a bulkhead. The seal was failing on a clock no one else could hear. The report would take days to reach the Council, more days to work through the analysis queue, more after that before anyone decided what to do with it. The seal didn’t have days.

“Yeah,” he said. “Those.”

Desh entered them. The nav computer shifted, probability matrices cycling as it tried to build a route through space that didn’t exist in its databases. Course corrections appeared and vanished while the processor worked, throwing out solutions that violated safety parameters, then trying again.

“Computer doesn’t like it,” Desh said.

“Can it do it?”

“It’s a nav computer. It’ll do what you tell it to do. Whether we come out the other side in one piece is a different question.” He watched the display cycle. “Route’s going to be rough. No hyperspace lane, no beacon network, no relay buoys. We’re flying blind into a hole in the map.”

Ben didn’t answer. The pull had turned heavy now, less like a tug than a weight pressing from ahead of them. Not the missing-handrail feeling he’d grown used to, but something spatial and immediate. The bulkheads seemed to lean in. Distance itself was refusing to behave.

Desh leaned back in the copilot’s chair and studied him. The silence stretched.

“You know what you’re doing,” Desh said. It wasn’t a question.

“I know the seal won’t wait for a committee.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Ben met his eyes. Desh’s face stayed unreadable, but Ben knew him too well to mistake the stillness. He wasn’t objecting. He was making sure Ben understood the choice—and staying in the chair while Ben made it.

“You could tell me to send the report,” Ben said.

“Would you send it?”

The question hung there. Ben looked at the draft on his screen—the third version, the compromise, the honest-enough report he’d been about to delete anyway. He closed it without saving.

“That’s what I thought,” Desh said.

He turned back to the nav computer. The route had resolved into a thin line through unmapped space, the probability matrix showing a seventy-two percent confidence rating and declining. The hyperdrive was ready.

Ben pulled up the jump controls. His hand didn’t hesitate. The urgency of the seal’s degradation crowded the doubt aside before it could fully form. The coordinates waited. Desh stayed in his chair, hands resting on the console, the choice made and shared and locked in.

“Jumping,” Ben said.

The stars stretched.

Hyperspace swallowed them in the familiar rush—blue-white tunnel, brief disorientation, the ship settling into a new rhythm. Ben waited for the strain in the Force to ease. It always did in hyperspace. Distance usually thinned everything.

It didn’t thin.

It pressed harder. Not the directional pull of the coordinates, but a weight against the hull, against the bulkheads, against Ben’s own awareness. The seal’s failure wasn’t something they were flying away from; it was the medium they were flying through, and hyperspace wasn’t carrying them clear of it.

It was carrying them deeper.

Ben’s fingers tightened on the armrest. The blue-white tunnel streamed past the viewport, and the pressure climbed—spatial, directional, the distance ahead of them still refusing to make sense. It was as if the ship had hit weather in a place weather shouldn’t exist.

Desh was watching the sensor readouts. His face had gone still in a different way now—focus, not surprise, as he read data he didn’t like.

“That’s not normal,” he said.

“No.”

“The wrongness. It’s not fading.”

“I know.”

Desh didn’t ask what it meant. He didn’t need to. The nav computer was still painting its route through uncharted space, and the strain was pressing against the hull like something waiting for them to commit. Ben was alone with what he’d chosen—and Desh had chosen to stay alone with him.

The cost of that sat at the edge of Ben’s awareness, unexamined, as hyperspace carried them toward the source.
