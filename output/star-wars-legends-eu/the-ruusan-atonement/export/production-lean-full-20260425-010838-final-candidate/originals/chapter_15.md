# Chapter 15

## Scene 15.01

The comm station aboard the *Steady Returns* was barely bigger than a storage locker, and Ben had been packed into it for forty minutes.

He cycled frequencies with the easy motion of someone who had spent too much of his adolescence on ships like this one, running survey routes with his father while the galaxy rebuilt itself around them. The encryption protocols were standard Jedi Order: half the channels open on outpost relays, the rest sealed tight for Temple eyes only. Ben had clearance for both. He had been using it.

The first fragment caught him in mid-scroll.

*—anomalous Force disturbance at the Dantooine nexus. Repeat, anomalous disturbance. Requesting guidance from—*

He stopped the feed. The message was stamped six hours ago, routed through the Ord Mantell relay. Half-decrypted, the voice at the other end belonged to a young Knight Ben didn’t recognize. Static gnawed away the rest, but the word *anomalous* landed hard, like a rock dropped into still water.

He kept cycling.

The second report came from Telos. The third from a listening post near the Hapes Cluster, a Jedi observer who had been watching gravimetric shifts in the Transitory Mists and found something else instead. Something that made her voice tighten in a way the encryption couldn’t hide.

Ben’s hands moved on the controls before his mind caught up. The trouble had been a Corridor problem. A Veranthos problem. Something he could fit into the space between his ship and the seal.

It wasn’t anymore.

He pulled the decrypted fragments into one file and sent them to the main display in the common area. Then he stood, the ache across his shoulders only now making itself known. He’d been bent over the console long enough for the muscles to knot, and he hadn’t noticed.

Desh was already at the nav station when Ben came out, dragging a galactic map onto the main holodisplay. Sera stood near the bulkhead, arms crossed, her expression flat in the way that meant she was storing information for later. Torin sat at the table with a cup of something that had gone cold an hour ago.

Ben skipped preamble. “We’ve got reports from three outposts. Dantooine, Telos, and a Hapes listening post. All of them are seeing anomalous Force activity at nexuses.”

Desh glanced up from the display. “The same anomalies we’ve been tracking?”

“Same language. Different coordinates.” Ben brought up the file on his datapad and read the timestamps aloud. “Dantooine six hours ago. Telos four. Hapes is still live—the observer there says the Mists are acting wrong. Not gravimetric. Something else.”

“Something else,” Desh repeated. “Very helpful.”

“She’s a Jedi observer, not a physicist. She’s describing what she feels.”

Desh made a noise that wasn’t agreement and turned back to the holodisplay. His hands moved with the same economy Ben had learned to read years ago—the quick, precise motions of a man used to making sense of information that didn’t want to be understood.

The map shifted. Three points flared to life: Dantooine in the Outer Rim, Telos in the border regions, the Hapes Cluster hanging near the Coreward edge. Desh added the Corridor—the stretch of space they had been following since the first survey world, the bearing that had pulled Ben across half the Outer Rim.

Then he added the Valley of the Jedi.

The shape was immediate. The disturbances weren’t random. They spread outward from the Corridor in a rough spiral—not a straight line, not a clean circle, but a pattern all the same. Like a pressure front moving through the galaxy’s weather, the leading edge of something that had begun at Veranthos and hadn’t stopped.

Sera stepped closer to the display. “The Valley of the Jedi.”

Ben nodded. He’d seen it the same instant she had.

“The Reformations began there,” Sera said. “The first Council met on Ruusan. The decrees that buried Gavran’s work were signed in that valley.”

“And now it’s answering the seal’s failure.” Torin’s voice came from the table, steady and warm in a way Ben had learned to distrust. “The wound is running backward through the history that tried to bury it.”

Ben didn’t answer. The weight in his chest had changed—not alarm anymore, not quite. Something heavier. The trouble had been something he could feel in his bones, a constant pressure like the air before a storm that never broke. Now it had edges. Now it had a map.

Desh added two more points—listening posts farther out, stations that hadn’t reported anything yet but sat right in the pattern’s path. “If this keeps spreading at the current rate, those stations are going to start screaming within twelve hours.”

“Can we warn them?” Sera asked.

“Warn them of what?” Desh turned from the display. “We don’t have a sentence that fits in a standard comm burst. ‘The Force is coming apart at a vergence nobody remembers’ isn’t going to get through relay filters.”

“Then we give them something that will.”

Ben was only half listening. The datapad in his hand had chimed—a soft, brief tone, the one he’d assigned to his personal frequency. A frequency only a handful of people in the galaxy had.

He looked down.

The message was short. Coded, but readable—the sort of encryption Luke used when he wanted privacy without the ceremony of formal security protocols. Three lines, in the voice of a Jedi Master who had felt something wrong in the Force and was asking his nephew for an accounting.

*Sensing disturbances at multiple nexuses. Report status.*

*—Luke*

The cursor blinked in the reply field.

Ben stared at it.

The hum of the ship filled the silence. Desh was still talking—something about relay protocols and whether the Hapes observer could be reached directly. Sera had a question about the Valley’s current status. Torin said nothing, but Ben could feel his attention, steady and direct.

The cursor blinked.

Ben’s hands rested on the datapad. He didn’t move them.

The reply field stayed empty. Not because he didn’t know what to say—he knew exactly what he should say, and he knew exactly what it would mean. A full report would pull the Order into the Corridor. It would bring Jedi resources, Jedi oversight, Jedi questions he couldn’t answer without explaining why he’d been filing partial reports for weeks. It would hand the mission to someone else, and someone else didn’t know what Ben knew. Someone else hadn’t been inside Gavran’s fortress. Someone else hadn’t felt the seal failing from the inside.

A partial report was worse. A partial report was the thing he’d been doing since the first survey world, and it had already cost him more than he wanted to count.

The truth was impossible. The lie was worse.

The cursor blinked.

Ben closed the message. He didn’t delete it. He didn’t file it away. He let it sit in his inbox, unread and unanswered, the way he’d let too many things sit over the last three weeks.

The pressure in his chest didn’t lift. It sank deeper, like a migraine settling behind the eyes and refusing to break—not pain yet, but the promise of it. The front was still moving through. The storm hadn’t arrived.

But it would.

Desh’s voice cut through the quiet. “Ben. You still with us?”

Ben looked up. The holodisplay still glowed, the disturbance points burning amber against the starfield. Sera was watching him with that sharp, unreadable focus she used when she’d noticed something and had decided not to name it. Torin’s expression gave nothing away.

“Still here,” Ben said.

His voice came out steady. The datapad was dark in his hand.

He didn’t look at it again.

## Scene 15.02

Torin waited until Ben had finished half his plate before he brought it up. He always waited long enough for the food to dull the edge, long enough for the silence to stop feeling like a pause and start feeling like dinner. Then he set his fork down with a small, deliberate click against the ceramic.

“The Valley’s destabilizing.”

Ben kept his eyes on his plate. “I saw the reports.”

“Then you saw the pattern.” Torin’s voice stayed warm and easy, the same tone he’d used three nights ago telling a story about a Coruscanti tapcaf that had made Sera laugh despite herself. “The disturbances aren’t random. They’re spreading outward from the Corridor, and the leading edge hit Ruusan six hours ago. The Valley of the Jedi is answering the seal’s failure.”

Sera sat at the end of the table, her own plate barely touched. She had been quiet since the comm station, and Ben knew that silence. It had weight. It meant she was filing things away for later. This time she was listening.

“The seal was always temporary,” Torin said. “Gavran knew it. The Council knew it. They built a containment system out of living anchors and called it mercy, but they knew it would fail eventually. The only question was when.”

Ben set down his fork. The food had gone lukewarm—some protein-and-grain mix Desh had pulled from the ship’s stores, edible in the way emergency rations were edible. “The seal held for a thousand years. That’s not temporary.”

“It’s temporary if it ends.” Torin pushed his plate aside and drew the tactical display between them. The holomap flared to life: disturbance points Desh had plotted, the Corridor stretching across the Outer Rim, the Valley glowing amber at the edge of the pattern. “Look at the rate of deterioration. The breach at Darrpa was sixty-three days ago. The anchor deaths are accelerating. The cascade is already moving, and nothing we’ve done has slowed it.”

“We’ve been running recovery, not intervention.” Ben leaned forward, the urge to meet him point for point already pulling him in. He wanted to find the flaw, to put pressure on the weak seam in Torin’s logic. “We haven’t tried to reinforce the seal yet.”

“Because reinforcing it won’t work.” Torin’s finger traced the Corridor’s line. “The seal was built to fail. Twelve anchors, one keystone, all of them mortal. Gavran built a dam out of bodies and hoped it would hold forever. It didn’t. It won’t.” He looked up from the display, and his eyes met Ben’s with the same warmth they’d held since the fortress. “The only permanent answer is someone who understands the technique becoming the new seal.”

The words settled between them and stayed there.

Ben heard himself say, “No.”

“You haven’t thought about it.”

“I don’t need to think about it. We’re not trading one person for a stopgap.”

“It’s not a stopgap if it holds.” Torin still didn’t raise his voice. He was explaining, the way he’d explained the tactical situation on the ridge, the way he’d walked Kael through modern comm protocols with patient good humor. “The technique can be applied in reverse. Instead of tearing the lattice apart, you weave yourself into it. You become the structure. No anchors, no chambers, no slow decay. One person, one seal, permanent.”

“One person dead,” Ben said.

“One person choosing.” Torin smiled, and it was real—not the polished warmth Ben had started to watch for, but the thing that made people trust him. “I’m not proposing a sacrifice, Ben. I’m offering to pay a price I’ve already accepted. I used the technique once. I know what it costs. I’m the only person in this room who can make that choice with full knowledge of what it means.”

The display glowed between them, the disturbance points pulsing amber. Ben could feel the shape of Torin’s argument—clean, narrow, impossible to ignore. No more racing Veraine to failing nodes. No more watching the seal sag by inches. One decision, one action, one answer that would not give way tomorrow.

It was the same shape as every choice Ben had made since the first survey world.

“You’re asking me to let you die.”

“I’m asking you to let me finish what I started.” Torin’s hand rested on the edge of the display, steady as durasteel. “The technique damaged the Force. I damaged the Force. A thousand years of stasis didn’t change that—it just postponed the accounting. This is the accounting.”

Sera still hadn’t moved. Her silence had changed, too—not the listening stillness of someone storing information, but the heavy stillness of someone who had heard this argument before. Ben glanced at her, and she gave him nothing back. She was watching him, not Torin.

“You’ve been withholding information,” Torin said.

Ben’s attention snapped back.

“Since the first survey world. You’ve been filing partial reports, controlling what the Order knows, managing the narrative to keep the mission in your hands.” Torin’s tone held no accusation. The warmth remained, and that made it hit harder. “You’re curating reality to manage the outcome you want. You know who else did that?”

The sentence stopped there.

The galley’s recycled air hummed through the vents. Ben’s hands found his knees under the table. He gripped them hard, the pressure a way to stay anchored while he had the strange, sick feeling of being seen—not watched, not measured, but seen clean through. His breath caught. The impulse to answer died in his throat, because answering meant either lying or admitting Torin was right.

Torin didn’t finish the sentence. He didn’t have to.

The display still glowed. The disturbance points still pulsed. The argument still hung there, all clean lines and brutal simplicity, the permanent answer that would cost one life and end the crisis. Ben could feel the pull of it, the temptation to treat Torin’s sacrifice as the solution. It was the same pull he felt every time he closed a report without sending it, every time he let his father’s message sit unanswered.

Sera’s silence settled around them. She wasn’t neutral; her stillness was witness. She knew where this conversation led. She’d known before it started.

Ben kept his hands locked on his knees. The sentence Torin hadn’t finished stayed in the air between them, and Ben did not complete it. He didn’t name the pattern. He didn’t say the parallel out loud. He just sat there while the recognition sank into him, heavy and cold and impossible to shake.

The question was whether recognizing the pattern was enough to break it.

He didn’t know yet.

## Scene 15.03

The companionway was shadowed enough that Ben saw the two figures before he made out their faces. Kael had his back to him, shoulders set in the way that meant he was listening to something he had not committed to yet. Torin faced outward, one hand braced on a coolant conduit at chest height, his head angled slightly toward Kael.

They were standing close. Not the closeness of two people who had paused in the same passage by accident. The closeness of a conversation that had been going on for a while.

Ben’s boot rasped against the deck plate.

Kael turned first. His expression didn’t shift—that same watchful economy he wore everywhere—but he stepped back from Torin in one clean motion and walked away down the companionway without looking at Ben. Not avoidance. Something closer to a door sealing.

Torin watched him go. His gaze tracked Kael’s retreat with an expression Ben couldn’t pin down, a blend of assessment and recognition, and then he turned back and met Ben’s eyes.

Neither of them said anything.

Ben’s first impulse was to do something about it—ask a question, lift an eyebrow, acknowledge that he’d walked in on something and noticed it. He swallowed it down. The restraint felt like a small, deliberate wrong turn, a muscle he was learning to use.

He kept walking.

The sleeping nook was barely larger than the fold-down bunk it held, the same stripped-down slot the *Steady Returns* offered every passenger who wasn’t its pilot. Ben sealed the panel behind him and stood in the quiet for a moment, listening to the ship’s ventilators cycle through the night pattern.

His lightsaber hung at his belt. He unclipped it and sat on the edge of the bunk.

The components came apart in the order they always did. Emitter housing. Focusing lens. Power cell. Cycling field energizer. He laid each piece on the fold-down shelf with the care of a ritual he had done a thousand times, his hands moving through the familiar sequence while his mind went to the harder work.

The report. The one he should have sent three days ago.

He set down the last component—the diatium power cell, still warm from the saber’s standby draw—and pulled his datapad from the locker. The screen came alive with the message queue he had been avoiding. Luke’s coded inquiry sat at the top, timestamped fourteen hours earlier, still unopened.

Ben opened a blank report template.

The cursor blinked.

He typed.

*Survey team located Jedi Lords-era containment facility in the Darrpa Sector. Facility functioned as central node in a network of twelve anchor sites distributed across the Outer Rim. Nine anchors deceased. Three survived in stasis. One escaped approximately sixty-three days before our arrival. Survivors identified as New Sith Wars-era Jedi Knights Sera Varik, Torin Hal, and conscript Kael Drenn. Escapee identified as Darth Veraine, Brotherhood of Darkness alchemist and strategist.*

*The containment network was designed to seal a Force wound caused by a forbidden technique deployed during the New Sith Wars. The technique damaged the local structure of the Force at Veranthos. The seal is failing. Veraine is moving along the Corridor ahead of us, and based on the rate of deterioration, she will reach Veranthos with enough momentum to attempt a full breach.*

*I am pursuing with the survivors. They have operational knowledge of the seal’s architecture that the Order does not possess. I am withholding exact coordinates pending further assessment of the tactical situation.*

He read it through twice.

The paragraphs were honest. They named the fortress, the survivors, the wound, Veraine, the seal’s failure, the stakes. They were also complete enough that sending them would force the Order’s hand. A Jedi strike team would be dispatched within hours. The survivors would be debriefed by analysts who had never felt the strain in the air, who would treat the seal like an engineering problem and the anchors like museum pieces. Ben would be relieved of operational command and assigned a liaison role while someone with more seniority made decisions about people he had woken from a thousand years of stasis.

The part of him that trusted the Order—that believed in hierarchy and protocol and the chain of command his father had spent decades rebuilding—wanted to hit send. That part wasn’t small. It was the part that had filed every report on time as a junior Knight, that had never lied in a mission briefing, that still believed the institution could be trusted to do the right thing if it had all the facts.

The other part had watched the Order move too slowly too many times. Had seen what happened when a crisis hit committees and subcommittees and the careful consensus-building that passed for decisive action. Had learned, at seventeen, that the people who were supposed to protect you could become the people you needed protection from.

Both choices felt like betrayal.

Ben’s thumb hovered over the send command.

He deleted the report with a single keystroke.

The screen went blank. The cursor blinked once and stopped.

He set the datapad aside and reached for his lightsaber components. His hands weren’t quite steady. He fit the focusing lens back into the emitter housing, then the power cell, then the cycling field energizer, each piece sliding home with the same precision he’d used to take it apart. The motions were practiced, mechanical, a performance of steadiness his body could offer while the rest of him caught up to what he’d just done.

The wrongness settled into place. Not the Force wrongness—that was still there, a constant pressure at the edge of everything, like weather moving in from the horizon. This was smaller and more personal. The weight of a choice that could not be unmade.

He finished reassembling the lightsaber and set it on the shelf beside the datapad. The file index was still open. The ghost of the deleted report sat in memory cache, its entry grayed out but intact, recoverable by anyone who knew where to look.

Ben stared at it.

He did not clear the ghost.

The part of him that knew he would need proof later—that he had tried, that he had written the truth before choosing to bury it—wasn’t yet the loudest part. But it was there. It had been there since the first survey world, since the first partial report, since the first moment he had looked at his father’s message and decided not to answer. It was a passenger now. A permanent one.

He closed the file index and lay back on the bunk. The ventilators cycled. The pressure at the edge of his senses held steady, patient and familiar, weather rather than event.

He did not sleep.
