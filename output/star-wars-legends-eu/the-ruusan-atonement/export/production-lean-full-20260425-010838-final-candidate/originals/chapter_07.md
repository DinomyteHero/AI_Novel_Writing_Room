# Chapter 7

## Scene 07.01

The *Steady Returns* settled onto the battlefield world with a low thrum that bled away into silence. Through the cockpit viewport, Ben saw ground shaped by war and then left to fade—trenches softened into shallow dips, artillery berms worn down into grassy swells. A line of corroded machinery jutted from the soil a hundred meters out, half-swallowed by wild grass, the barrel of something that had once hurled fire now aimed at nothing.

Ben unstrapped and rose. Behind him, the others were already moving—Sera’s clipped footsteps on the deck plates, Torin’s heavier tread, Kael’s near-silent presence at the rear. Desh caught Ben’s eye from the pilot’s seat and gave a fractional nod. Ship secure. Systems nominal. All the things Desh said without wasting breath.

The ramp lowered. Ben’s boots hit soil that still carried the shape of thousand-year-old trenches.

He reached for the Force.

It answered from a step farther off than it should have.

The sensation struck at once, all body and no mercy. Not pain. Not darkness. A gap where there should have been continuity—like putting weight on ground that arrived a fraction late to meet him. His balance corrected before his mind caught up, his body reading a tilt his eyes couldn’t see. The green hillside ahead looked soft, almost peaceful. The Force beneath it felt wrong.

Ben let the sensation settle without pushing deeper. Every instinct urged him to extend, to reach until the offset resolved into something he could name. He didn’t. The half-beat delay he’d felt in the cockpit had been a warning. This was what it had been warning him about.

He moved clear of the ramp and let the others follow.

The bunker sat two hundred meters east, a low durasteel structure half-buried in the hillside. Ancient military architecture—utility without ornament, built to be ignored until someone needed to survive inside it. The entrance was a recessed blast door, still sealed, the control panel beside it dark.

Sera stopped at his shoulder. Her shoulders were tight, jaw set hard enough to show in profile. She scanned the hillside the way she’d scanned Gavran’s fortress, measuring threat vectors, marking exits that weren’t there. The thin place was getting to her; Ben could see it in the stiffness of her movement. She felt it as danger. That made sense. Sera had spent her life reading the Force as a tactical tool, and this place made that tool lie.

“The door’s still sealed,” she said. “Whoever came here didn’t use the front.”

“Or didn’t need to,” Ben said.

He dropped to one knee beside the control panel. The casing was corroded, but the internal leads were intact—old Jedi Lords tech, built to outlast the people who installed it. He laid his palm against the access plate and let the Force move through him, feeling for the mechanism inside.

The lock didn’t recognize him.

That was wrong. The bio-locks at Gavran’s fortress had opened for his Skywalker signature, grudgingly but open. This one sat dead under his touch, as if it had forgotten what it was supposed to answer to. The thin place had seeped into the machinery, Ben thought. Or the machinery had died with its anchor centuries ago, and no one had noticed.

He drew his lightsaber. One clean cut through the manual release, and the blast door groaned open on seized tracks.

Inside, the air was cold and dry, preserved as if the bunker had been waiting for someone who never came. Ben’s boots left prints in dust that had settled for centuries and had never been touched.

The corridor ran straight for thirty meters before opening into a central chamber. Stasis pods lined the walls—twelve of them, identical to the ring at Gavran’s fortress, arranged in a semicircle around a central console. All of them dark. All of them empty.

Ben moved to the nearest pod and brushed dust from the access panel. The readings were dead, the occupant’s vitals flatlined so long ago the timestamp had gone to nonsense. He checked the next pod. Same. The next. Same. Twelve anchors, all gone silent centuries before the seal started its final collapse.

“The anchor here died first,” Sera said. She stood at the central console, fingers moving over the interface with brisk precision, as if the machine might still be persuaded to talk. “The node’s been running on residual charge for at least three hundred years. It wasn’t enough to hold the seal, but it kept the environmental systems alive.” She paused, then looked up. “The installation records show recent access.”

Ben looked up. “How recent?”

“Weeks. Maybe days.” She tapped the console again, and the display flickered with a pale wash of corrupted text.

Ben crossed to her side. The wrongness of the place faded to a background drag as he focused on the console, the dust, the timeline. His hand hovered over the panel. Weeks. Not centuries. Not ruins. Recent. “Open the chamber records.”

Sera’s mouth thinned. “Already trying.”

The chamber door stood open a handspan, its seam dark with dust. Ben slipped inside, and the air changed at once—same cold, same dryness, but thicker with old filtration and sealed time. The stasis chamber was empty. The cradle at its center stood open, lifeless, the anchor that should have been there gone so long ago the absence felt built in.

He swept a hand across the rim and down the access panel, pushing dust aside. Fresh tool marks caught the dim light—bright scrapes on top of gray film that had lain untouched for centuries. His fingers stilled.

Sera made a low sound behind him. “That wasn’t the only access.”

Ben glanced over his shoulder. Torin had moved farther into the bunker than the others, almost loose-limbed, as if the narrow corridors and dead air bothered him less than the open hillside. Kael stood near the chamber threshold, face blanked out, his usual sharpness pulled flat. Sera had gone rigid enough to hurt just looking at her. Ben took it in without naming any of it. Sera’s tension made sense. Kael’s shuttered expression read like withdrawal. Torin’s ease didn’t. Not here. Not in a place that made every step feel half a beat off.

Ben looked back down at the panel. Fresh lines scored the metal. Recent access. Recent hands. The trail had gone from archive to present tense.

Someone had been here weeks ago.

## Scene 07.02

The records room lay behind a door that hadn’t been opened in centuries. Sera stopped before it and set both palms flat on the metal.

Ben realized what she was doing a half-second before his training fully caught up. The door was Force-locked—ancient work, layered sealing the modern Jedi studied as history instead of craft. He started to tell her they should find another route.

Sera’s fingers flexed once.

The lock gave way.

Not cracked. Not bypassed. It simply came apart under her touch the way a household latch yielded to someone who knew exactly where the pressure belonged. One moment the door hummed with contained energy. The next it was dead metal, and Sera was already pushing it open with the heel of her palm.

Ben felt the ease of it before he understood it. The domestic familiarity. The thousand-year muscle memory that hadn’t lost a step. A faint vertigo opened in his chest—the distance between what he’d been trained to expect and what he was seeing.

Sera stepped through without looking back. “The archive will be in the central vault. They always put it at the core.”

Ben followed her into the dark.

The records room was a round chamber sunk into the bedrock beneath the bunker. Holographic projectors ringed the walls, their emitters dull with age but still intact. A central console sat in the middle of the floor, its interface panel dark. The air smelled of cold stone and old filtration, the same preserved deadness that had filled the stasis chamber outside.

Desh lingered at the doorway, blaster loose in his hand. “I’ll watch the corridor. You two do the history.”

Ben nodded without looking back. He was already moving toward the console.

The thin place made concentration harder. His attention kept tugging slightly off-center, as if something in the room wanted to be seen from the edge of his vision. He forced himself back to the console. Its interface was archaic, data architecture from an era before modern systems had settled into standard formats, but the power couplings were still live. He found the activation sequence on the third try.

The projectors woke with a low hum. Blue-white light filled the chamber, resolving into text and diagrams that hung in the air like frozen smoke.

Ben scrolled through the first layer of records. Military logistics. Supply routes. Troop movements across a sector whose name meant nothing to him. The language was archaic Basic, the sentence structure formal enough that his eyes wanted to skip over it.

“This is all operational,” he said. “Where’s the technical documentation?”

Sera came to stand beside him. Her gaze moved over the holographic text with the speed of someone who’d been trained to read this format before she’d been trained to fight. “Second layer. The Jedi Lords compartmentalized their archives—surface records for logistics, deeper vaults for Force-work. The lock I opened wasn’t the only security.”

She reached past him and set her fingers on the console’s edge. Her eyes half-closed. Ben felt the Force move through her—not broad and warm, like his father’s presence, but narrow and exact. A blade, not a hand.

The display flickered. The logistics records vanished. New text bloomed into place.

Ben read the first line and felt the floor shift under him.

*Classification: Restricted. Clearance: Anchor-Command. Subject: Force-Lock Discipline and Containment Architecture.*

Sera started translating before he could ask. Her voice stayed steady, clinical without going cold, the way she might have briefed Gavran a thousand years ago. “The Jedi Lords developed Force-lock techniques as a military discipline. Not meditation. Not philosophy. Engineering.” She paused on the last word, and Ben heard the weight she put on it—the same word Torin had used in the thin place, the same framing that had made Sera’s jaw tighten. “They trained specialists to build barriers, seals, and containment fields using structured applications of the Force. It was a formal school. Ranked. Credentialed.”

Ben scrolled deeper. The records opened into diagrams—Force-flow patterns mapped onto architectural schematics, the same interlocking geometry he’d seen in the fortress. “This is what Gavran used to build the seal.”

“Yes.” Sera’s voice had gone quieter. “And this is what the Brotherhood studied to learn how to break it.”

She touched a line of text near the bottom of the display. The holograph shivered beneath her fingertip.

“The technique has a name,” she said. “They called it unweaving.”

The word hit Ben’s body a beat after it left her mouth. Not his mind—his chest, his hands, the place behind his ribs where the Force moved when he reached for it. Unweaving. The shape of the procedure opened in him like something he’d always known and never been taught. A pull. A separation. Thread from thread, layer from layer, the structure of the Force coming apart under deliberate pressure.

His breath caught. Not fear. Recognition.

“Read it,” he said.

Sera’s eyes tracked across the text. “Unweaving is a counter-lock procedure derived from the Force-lock discipline itself. The practitioner identifies the structural nodes of a constructed barrier and applies localized pressure to each node in sequence. The barrier does not break. It comes apart.” She stopped. Her hand had gone still on the console. “The technique was classified after Veranthos.”

Ben’s focus sharpened. The thin place’s pull seemed to tighten, drawing his attention toward the name like a hand on his shoulder. “Pull up the Veranthos entry.”

Sera’s fingers moved across the interface. The display shifted again.

Coordinates bloomed in the air—a location in the Darrpa Sector, deep in the chart-void that had been wiped from modern navigation records. Beneath the coordinates, a purpose statement: *Primary containment anchor. Seal keystone. Wound origin site.* Beneath that, in text that glowed amber instead of blue-white, a warning notation: *Do not approach without Anchor-Command authorization. Local field instability Class 5. Active degradation ongoing.*

The wound was real. It had a location. And Veraine was practicing the specific discipline that had made it.

Ben’s body tightened before his mind finished catching up. Urgency settled in his chest—not panic, but the cold clarity of someone who understood the scope of the problem. Veraine wasn’t flailing. She wasn’t improvising. She was executing a named procedure from a formal training tree that had been classified for a thousand years because of what it had done to Veranthos.

He looked at Sera. She was still reading the display, her profile sharp in the blue-white light, and he saw her clearly now—not as a historical relic or a liability, not as a burden he had tolerated because the mission needed her knowledge. She was the only person in this bunker who could have done what she’d just done. The only person on the *Steady Returns*. The only person in the modern Order.

“We need to go,” he said. “Now.”

## Scene 07.03

Ben hunched over his datapad and deleted another paragraph.

The thin place’s aftereffects were easing out of him now—the half-beat lag in his reflexes smoothing over, the wrongness draining from muscle and nerve like a headache backing off after too many hours under bad light. He’d been at the report for twenty minutes. It should have taken five.

*Survey continues. Encountered navigational anomaly consistent with previous readings. No change in bearing. Proceeding to next checkpoint.*

True. All of it. None of it the thing he needed to tell his father.

His thumb hovered over the next line—the one about the technique having a name now, about unweaving and Veranthos and a classified discipline buried for a thousand years because of what it had done to the wound they were flying toward. He’d written it three different ways. Deleted it three different ways. The fourth version sat on the screen like a charge.

He deleted that too.

The datapad’s chrono ticked over. Luke’s comm frequency sat open in the corner of the display, patient and waiting, the way his father’s silences always were. Ben had learned to read those silences before he’d learned much else. This one he was writing himself.

“You’re editing.”

Torin’s voice came from the doorway. Not loud. Not sharp. Just there, the way Torin was there—warm, easy, and carrying something underneath that Ben had been refusing to name since the thin place.

Ben didn’t turn. “Reports need editing.”

“Mm.” Torin leaned in the frame, arms crossed, comfortable in his own skin in a way that reminded Ben of old Jedi Knights in Temple recordings—the ones who’d been fighting so long that stillness had become armor. “That’s not what I asked.”

The question settled on top of Ben’s keyboard. He felt it there, a weight he couldn’t type around.

Torin didn’t push. He just stayed in the doorway, watching Ben not answer, and the silence stretched until it stopped being an evasion and started being something else—something neither man moved to fill.

Ben’s fingers stayed on the datapad. The cursor blinked at the end of his last line, waiting for him to type something he wasn’t going to type.

“What will you tell them?” Torin asked.

Not *are you lying*. Not *why are you lying*. Just the question that would cost Ben the most to answer, delivered in the same warm register Torin used for stories and for making the crew feel like a team.

Ben looked at him then. Torin’s expression was open, unguarded, the face of a man who’d asked because he genuinely wanted to know—not because he was building a case, not because he was waiting to catch Ben out. The warmth was real. So was whatever lay under it.

“I’ll tell them what they need to know,” Ben said.

It wasn’t an answer. Torin took it as one anyway. He pushed off the frame, easy and unhurried, and the weight of him backed away by inches—still there, still patient, still carrying the question Ben hadn’t answered.

“Fair enough,” Torin said, and left the doorway empty behind him.

Ben stared at the datapad. The cursor blinked. The comm frequency waited.

He sent the report.

Across the common area, Kael’s voice came low and precise—a monk’s sentence with no spare words.

“Desh. The Kolven-era disciplinary logs. Can you pull them?”

Ben caught it from the corner of his eye before his mind caught up. Kael stood near Desh’s station, hands loose at his sides, not asking permission. Not explaining why. One sentence, delivered in the same dry register Kael used for everything, then silence while Desh took it in.

Desh didn’t ask either. He looked up from his console, held Kael’s gaze for a beat—Ben saw the quick appraisal, the familiar operational calculation of a man who’d spent years reading people for danger—and nodded.

“Yeah,” Desh said. “Give me a minute.”

His datapad turned in his hands as he queued the pull. The motion was casual, practiced, the same way he’d hand someone a hydrospanner or check a coolant valve. No commentary. No follow-up. Just easy acceptance.

Kael waited, still and patient, the way he always waited—like someone who’d learned that the best way to stay unnoticed was to keep still until the room forgot he was there. Ben had noticed anyway. He’d noticed the target of the request, Kolven—one of those Corridor-era settlement sites with disciplinary records, not general history, not context. Something specific. Something Kael had decided to chase on his own.

Ben didn’t move toward them. He kept it as texture—Kael’s low voice, Desh’s nod, the datapad turning in his hands—and let it sit at the edge of his attention without claiming it. A man who was used now choosing to learn. A request that asked nothing of Ben and required no reply.

The ship hummed around them. Recycled air carried the faint tang of the thin place’s residue, already thinning, already becoming just another thing they’d flown through.

Desh brought up the nav display. The next node’s readings bloomed across the screen—degradation stacking into an uglier baseline, the Force-ahead signal intermittent and broken. Where the previous stop had felt offset, a note played a little flat, this one came in pieces. Gaps between the pieces. Silence where structure ought to have been.

Ben felt it before he finished reading the display. The wrongness tightening. The bearing pulling harder.

“Energy signature’s up,” Desh said. His voice was flat, operational, the tone he used when the news was bad and there was no point pretending otherwise. “Same profile as the last stop, but stronger. Much stronger.”

Ben looked at the readings. The concentration pattern was unmistakable—not a pass-through, not a survey, not someone moving fast and leaving traces. Someone had stopped here. Worked here. Spent time dismantling the seal’s infrastructure with the same patience and precision that had clawed through a stasis chamber from the inside.

Renewed determination settled into Ben’s chest. Not hope. Not confidence. Just readiness, cold and clear, the thing that came after the choice had already been made.

The next node was on approach. The Force ahead came in pieces.

And someone had been there waiting.
