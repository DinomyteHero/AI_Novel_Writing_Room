# Chapter 20

## Scene 20.01

Veranthos filled the viewport.

Ben pressed his palm to the cold transparisteel and felt the jolt in his chest before he could put a name to it. The planet was beautiful. Blue-green oceans spread across most of the hemisphere, broken by cloud banks that caught the system’s star at a low angle. Temperate forests ran in deep green bands. Coastlines curved into sheltered bays. From orbit, it looked like a world someone would choose on purpose—the sort of place the old Republic would have flagged for colonization surveys and then argued over for three generations.

His hand stayed on the viewport. The cold seeped into his skin. Beneath his ribs, something tugged.

He didn’t turn. “Desh.”

“Already on it.” Desh’s voice came from the cockpit console behind him, steady and low. The sensor suite hummed through its startup cycle. “Give me thirty seconds.”

Ben kept his hand where it was. The planet turned beneath them, slow and silent, and its beauty sat wrong in his perception the way a door that didn’t quite latch sat wrong—close enough to miss if he hadn’t already been uneasy. The cockpit smelled of recycled air, old caf, and the faint hot-metal tang the Steady Returns always picked up when the drives had worked too hard. Someone had left a ration wrapper wedged beside the nav console. Normal things. Ship things. Details that should have anchored him.

The pull under his ribs deepened.

“All right,” Desh said. “Weather first. It isn’t following the rotation.”

Ben took his hand off the viewport and turned.

Desh had the sensor display up on the main console, half a dozen data streams running in parallel. Blue-white light from the screen lit his face from below, and his expression was the one he wore when the numbers refused to behave—calm, focused, filing each mismatch without letting any one of them shake him.

“Show me.”

Desh tapped the display. A weather overlay spread across the planetary surface in false-color bands. “Prevailing winds should be riding the rotation vector. Coriolis, standard atmospheric modeling. These are about forty degrees off. And that’s not a local storm front—the whole band’s shifted. Vegetation index is throwing errors too.”

“What kind of errors?”

“The kind where the computer thinks its own sensors are bad.” Desh brought up a second overlay. “Photosynthetic response is peaking in the wrong hemisphere. The forests are acting like they’re in direct sunlight, but the star angle says they should be in twilight. The nav computer flagged it as a calibration fault. I ran the diagnostic twice. Sensors are clean.”

Ben moved in behind Desh’s shoulder. On the display, the false-color bands shifted and re-formed as the sweep continued its arc. The data was clean. The planet was not.

“Ocean currents,” Desh said. He still wasn’t looking up. “Reversed. Not just in one basin—the whole circulation pattern. Warm water toward the poles, cold water toward the equator. Thermal gradient’s inverted.”

The cockpit went quiet except for the sensor suite’s hum and the soft chime of the nav computer logging each anomaly. Ben’s attention narrowed to a point. The awe that had pressed his palm to the viewport was still there, still pulling under his ribs, but it had company now—the cold clarity of a tactical assessment running alongside a body that knew something was wrong before the data finished proving it.

“Show me the boundary,” Ben said.

Desh’s hands paused over the console. “Which boundary?”

“The one between the anomalies and the normal readings.”

A beat. Desh pulled up a composite overlay—weather, vegetation, ocean thermals, stacked in translucent layers. The pattern snapped into view at once. A ring. Not geometric; it followed the contours of a mountain range on one continent and the curve of a deep-ocean trench on another. But it was unmistakable. Inside the ring, the data went bad. Outside it, the readings edged toward normal. Not cleanly normal—none of it was clean—but closer. The sort of readings a survey team might flag and then move past.

The boundary was failing. Ben could see it in the overlay: patches of bad data bleeding outward, tendrils of reversed current and misplaced photosynthesis reaching past the ring’s edge. The seal wasn’t holding.

He didn’t say any of that. The interpretation stayed in his body—a change in pressure he’d felt the moment they dropped out of hyperspace, a membrane he’d crossed without knowing it, the sudden drag of something immense on the far side. His Force-sense was still there, technically. He could still reach for it. But what came back was flattened, muffled, like sound on the far side of a sealed hatch.

The wound was pulling. Immense. Silent. Heavy in a way that had nothing to do with mass.

“Sera,” Ben said. “Torin. Kael. Cockpit.”

He didn’t raise his voice. He didn’t need to. The ship was small enough that it carried.

Sera arrived first, her footsteps quick and quiet on the deck plating. She stopped just inside the cockpit hatch and looked at the viewport, then at the sensor display, then at Ben. Her face didn’t change. The same composure she’d worn in the fortress, in the thin places, in every moment since she’d woken a thousand years out of time—blade-sharp, disciplined, held.

“How bad?” she said. It wasn’t really a question.

Torin came through the hatch behind her. He stopped. He looked at the planet.

Ben watched him.

Torin’s expression shifted—not much, not enough for anyone not watching closely. The warmth that usually lived at the corners of his eyes went still. His shoulders settled. His breathing eased by a fraction.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. The planet was tugging at him too, and whatever it was pulling, it wasn’t dread.

Kael entered last and stayed in the hatchway, arms folded, face unreadable. He looked at the sensor display for a long moment. Then he looked at Ben.

“That’s the boundary,” he said.

Ben nodded.

“The seal.”

“Yes.”

Kael’s gaze moved to the overlay—the ring, the bleeding edges, the bad data spreading outward in slow, patient fingers. His expression didn’t change. The silence that followed belonged to someone who’d seen containment fail before and knew exactly what came next.

Desh was still working the console. His hands moved through a sensor-sweep routine Ben recognized—the one he used when he was looking for something specific and didn’t want to say what until he found it.

“Desh.”

“Valley coordinates,” Desh said. His voice had gone flat in the way it did when he was processing something he didn’t like. “Where the seal’s central infrastructure should be. I’m getting more than one signature.”

The cockpit went still.

Desh expanded the scan. “One ship, powered down under canopy at the north end of the valley. Ash-Tithe profile match on the hull geometry. And scattered metallic returns along both ridge approaches—buried or netted over. Not natural. Older power traces too.” He glanced at Ben then, briefly. “She didn’t just beat us in. She staged this.”

Ben felt the words land and lock. Of course she had. While they had been pulling survivors out of wreckage, while they had been crawling through archives and dead outposts and trying to understand what they were even chasing, Veraine had been reading the same old Brotherhood records for routes and manifests, seeding teams ahead of herself, caching equipment where she knew she would need it, letting them spend time on answers she had already turned into motion.

“On the surface,” Desh said. “Not in approach. Not in orbit. Parked. Cold. Main thermal residue says it’s been there for weeks. The ridge returns aren’t fresh either. Surface work started before we ever entered the system.”

He zoomed the display. The valley resolved in topographic relief—a long, narrow cut between two mountain ridges, dense with forest cover, a river winding through the center. At the northern end, a heat shadow too regular to be geological. Along the valley floor and up one slope, faint lines and nodes the computer kept trying to smooth away as terrain noise until Desh forced the gain higher. Emplacements. Buried hardware. A worked path under the trees.

Veraine had not arrived with them. She had been here while they were still crossing the Corridor, while they were still piecing together what the old records meant, while Ben still thought there might be approach time left if they moved hard enough. She had used every hour they spent catching up.

The tactical recognition landed clean and sharp, cutting through the awe and the pull under his ribs. There was no approach time left to spend. No room for planning, no margin for hesitation. The timeline had collapsed into the present.

Sera was still looking at the display. Her hands were at her sides. Her face was the same face she’d worn since she stepped into the cockpit—composed, disciplined, held—but something behind it had locked into place.

Torin hadn’t moved. His eyes were on the valley coordinates. His expression was the one Ben had been watching for since the thin places, since the outpost, since the first moment Torin had relaxed into the wrongness instead of bracing against it. Not certainty. Not determination.

Longing.

Ben looked away. He filed it. He did not name it.

Kael was the last to speak. His voice was quiet, dry, the way it always was when he was delivering the thing nobody wanted to hear.

“How long until we can land?”

Desh didn’t look up from the console. “Twenty minutes if I push the descent. Fifteen if I don’t care about the thermal shielding.”

“Push it,” Ben said.

Desh’s next sweep tightened on the valley. The ship signature stayed where it was. So did the buried returns. Nothing about the readout suggested a camp being set. Everything about it said the operation was already in progress.

The cockpit hummed. The planet turned beneath them, beautiful and wrong, and the wound pulled at Ben from the far side of a failing membrane. Veraine was already on the surface at the valley coordinates. No approach time left to spend.

## Scene 20.02

The descent path cut through a low saddle between two ridges, and the moment Ben stepped past a squat outcropping of grey-green stone, the Force was gone.

Not thinned. Not shifted. Not muffled behind a sealed hatch the way it had been since orbit. Gone.

His boot was still in the air. The next step should have landed; his body knew the rhythm, the weight transfer, the little corrections that kept him upright on uneven ground. But the presence that had threaded every thought and motion since before he could remember was simply absent. The space where it had been did not fill in. It stayed open, a blank cut in the world—like reaching into a dark pocket and finding no seam, no fabric, nothing to catch the hand.

He stumbled.

His knee slammed stone. Cold shock bit through his leg, followed by the hard, sharp pain of kneecap on rock. His palms skidded over the ground. The stone was cold. The air was still and cool and carried a green, slightly off smell, vegetation that had learned its lesson in the wrong direction. Behind him, someone said his name. Sera, he thought. The voice sounded far away, the way voices did when they came from the other side of a threshold he had crossed alone.

He stayed on hands and knees for three breaths. The stone was real. The air was real. The absence was absolute.

He pushed himself upright. His knee throbbed. His hands were scraped raw. Small, ordinary hurts from a body that had no choice but to keep going.

No current. No presence. No answer.

“Ben.” Sera’s voice, closer now. She had stopped two paces behind him. She had not crossed the boundary.

He straightened. His breathing stayed even by force of habit. His hands stayed steady. The reflex to reach for the Force rose, met nothing, and rose again—a skipped beat that refused to settle.

“Give me a moment,” he said.

He didn’t turn. He looked at his hands—scraped, cold, ordinary—then at the path ahead, winding down through a thinning stand of trees toward the first ring of what had once been a city. The wrongness wasn’t in his hands. It wasn’t in the stone. It was in the space between things, and it wasn’t going to stop.

He took the next step. And the next. The group followed, crossing the boundary one by one behind him, and he didn’t look back to see who hesitated.

---

The evacuation zone started without a sign or a marker. One moment they were walking through forest—grey-green trees, undergrowth that curled in spirals instead of straight, the occasional flower opening on the wrong side of a shadow—and the next they were passing a line of ground vehicles parked beside a road that hadn’t been maintained for a thousand years.

The vehicles were intact. Their canopies were shut. Their nose cones pointed away from the city, toward the ring boundary, toward the place where the seal had once held. A dozen of them, then two dozen, then more as the road widened and the forest gave way to cleared ground that had once been a landing field. Overgrowth had swallowed most of it—vines thick as a man’s arm, trees splitting permacrete and keeping on—but the pattern was plain. Every vehicle pointed out. Every door on every structure still standing hung open.

Desh stopped beside a personnel carrier with a crumpled rear panel. He ran his hand over the metal. “They didn’t take anything.”

Ben looked at the open doors, the lined-up vehicles, the landing field that had been a staging area. “They didn’t have time.”

“Or they didn’t care about things.” Desh’s voice had gone flat in the way it did when he was working through something he didn’t like. “They just wanted out.”

Sera had moved ahead, her hand resting on the hilt of her lightsaber. She was looking at the city. What showed through the trees—low buildings, pale stone, the suggestion of streets—was intact. No blast scoring. No collapsed roofs. No sign of the kind of destruction that emptied a city.

“Kael,” Ben said. “Anything?”

Kael had stopped at the edge of the landing field. His arms were folded. His face was unreadable in the way it always was when he was reading something he didn’t want to name. “No birds.”

Ben listened. The silence wasn’t complete—there was wind, the rustle of strange-growing leaves, the sound of his own breathing—but there were no birds. No insects. No animal calls of any kind. The city ahead stood whole and silent in a way that had nothing to do with peace.

They walked.

---

The city was not a ruin. That was the horror.

The buildings stood. The streets were clean except for the overgrowth—vines climbing walls and then turning back halfway up, tendrils curling downward as if gravity had changed its mind. Trees had pushed through walls without knocking them down, horizontal splits in stone where trunks had grown through and kept going, bark and mortar woven together. Flowers bloomed on the shadowed faces of buildings, their petals opening toward the dark.

A child’s toy lay on a step. A stuffed animal, faded to grey, one button eye missing. It had been set there, not dropped. Someone had meant to come back for it.

Ben’s hand brushed a wall as he passed. The stone was cold. Real. Ordinary. The contradiction between intact city and the thing warping through it pressed at his chest in a way the Force would normally have smoothed out—a dissonance he could have reached into and named. Without it, the pressure stayed.

Desh had stopped in the middle of the street. His shoulders had gone rigid in a way Ben recognized—the stance of a man carrying weight with no name, no source, no reason to be there. A sadness about nothing. A grief that wouldn’t find its target because there wasn’t one to find.

“Desh.”

“I’m fine.” The words came out flat, automatic. “It’s just—” He broke off. His hand pressed against his sternum, a gesture that looked unconscious. “It’s nothing. I’m fine.”

It wasn’t nothing. Ben could see that in the way Desh held himself, in the shallow cut of his breathing, in the way his eyes moved over the empty street as if he expected something to answer. Non-Force grief. The body’s response to a wound it could not name.

Ben didn’t try to name it for him. He waited. After a moment, Desh let his hand fall and kept walking.

---

The ridgeline opened without warning.

One step they were in the city, passing a school with its doors standing open and its windows intact, and the next the buildings dropped away and the ground fell into a valley stretched between two mountain ridges like a scar between ribs. The river Ben had seen from orbit wound through the center, silver-grey in the late-afternoon light. The forest on the far ridge stood green and still.

And ahead, in the valley between the ridgelines: the Scar.

It was roughly three meters tall. A vertical line in the air, like heat shimmer without heat. It didn’t glow. It didn’t pulse. It didn’t move. It simply hung there—a distortion the eye could catch but the mind couldn’t settle. Straight on, it was almost invisible. From an angle, it was unmistakable. The air bent around it in a way air shouldn’t. Light on the far side arrived a fraction too late, or too early, or both.

Sight came before understanding. Ben’s eyes registered it before his mind could give it a name, and in that thin gap between looking and knowing, awe and horror hit at once. The thing every warning had been pulling him toward. The wound at the center of the seal. The tear in the Force Gavran had tried to contain and Veraine was trying to open and a dozen Jedi Lords had died to keep shut.

His breath settled. His hands hung at his sides. He did not reach for the Force because the Force was not there to reach for, and with a clarity that had nothing to do with thought, he understood it would not be there for what came next.

Behind him, nobody spoke. The silence on the ridgeline belonged to five people staring at the same impossible thing and finding no words that could make it smaller.

Ben stopped walking.

## Scene 20.03

They set camp on the ridgeline where the trees thinned and the valley opened below. From there the Scar hung in the air like a vertical wound, three meters tall, a line that should not have been there. It gave off no glow. It never moved. It simply hung above the river, bending the light around it so the eye caught it and the mind slid off.

Ben drove a tent stake into the cold ground and kept his eyes away from the valley. His hands knew the work—stake, mallet, line, repeat. The place pressed at the space where his Force-sense ought to have been, a steady weight like a hand against his ribs, and every muscle in him wanted to move, to do something, to make the camp ready for a threat that was already here. His mind knew better. Movement would not fix this. He slammed the next stake in harder than he needed to.

Desh handled the other tent. Sera laid out bedrolls with the neat, economical motions of someone who had pitched camp on hostile ground a hundred times over. Kael disappeared into the treeline without a word—scouting or solitude, Ben did not ask.

Torin stood at the ridge edge and stared at the Scar.

He had not shifted since they stopped walking. His pack sat at his feet where he had dropped it. His arms hung loose at his sides. The late light caught his profile—the hard line of his jaw, the breadth of his shoulders, the stillness of a man who had found what he was looking for and needed nothing else.

Ben finished the tent and straightened, rubbing at a knee that still ached from the fall at the boundary, a small, ordinary pain that helped keep him anchored to something sane. He looked at Torin’s back and knew he ought to say something—ask a practical question, call for watch assignments, anything that would put words into the widening silence.

He said nothing. Whatever sat on Torin’s face was not fear.

It was longing. Quiet, settled, the certainty of someone who had already chosen and was only waiting for the hour.

Ben’s hands went still on the tent line. The recognition hit with a sickening tilt, as if the ground had shifted under him: not abstract unease anymore, but fact. Not *what might happen tomorrow*, but *what Torin had already decided.* The group’s fragile cohesion, held together by momentum and necessity since Gavran’s fortress, was starting to crack. Torin was not waiting. Torin was ready.

Sera passed him with an armful of ration packs. She did not look at Torin. Somewhere in the city, Ben realized, she had stopped looking at him the way you stopped staring at a door you knew would open. Desh kept his back to the ridge, all his attention on the poles and lines and the things he could actually fix. Kael remained in the trees.

Nobody watched Torin except Ben. And Ben could not unsee what he had seen.

Dark fell fast on Veranthos. The sky slid from grey to black without a real sunset, the way light drains from a room when the door shuts. The Scar outlined itself against the stars—a line of absence, a cut in the sky where the light arrived wrong or not at all.

Ben sat in the tent with the flap open. The datapad rested on his knee, its low glow the only light. Outside, Torin’s silhouette had not left the ridge edge. He had been standing there for hours. He would still be there at dawn, Ben knew, and then he would walk down into the valley, and the thing Ben had been dreading since the first thin place would happen.

Tomorrow. The word sat in his chest like a stone.

His gaze dropped to the datapad. The file was still open from the outpost—Veraine’s second ledger entry, the one he had not read yet. He had been saving it, or avoiding it, or both. Now the waiting had nowhere else to go.

He opened it.

The entry ran maybe three hundred words. No salutation. No date. No addressee. The diction struck first—no contractions, no ornament, no softness. Short working sentences. Academic syntax cut down to its load-bearing pieces. She wrote like a surgeon recording findings: precise, certain, and not interested in whether anyone agreed.

She had walked the midpoint-era site three days before Ben’s group reached it. Her notes on the technique-lineage fragment were brief—a cross-reference to Ankhural’s third discourse, a notation on the thread-density variance between the Corridor’s inner and outer rings, and a single line observing that Gavran’s containment architecture had been built to withstand pressure from outside, not erosion from within. Then, at the end of the entry, a structural observation:

*The keystone’s load-distribution pattern assumes a twelve-anchor array in full contact with the local field. The ninth anchor’s chamber shows stress fractures consistent with asymmetric loading—the field pulling against a void where the anchor should be. Gavran did not name this feature. It is the point at which his arithmetic fails.*

Ben read it twice.

The ninth anchor. Torin’s chamber. The structural feature Gavran had never named was the thing that would fail first—not because the seal was weak, but because it had been built on the assumption that all twelve anchors would hold. And one of them was standing at the ridge edge, already gone.

He closed the file.

The tent stayed quiet. Outside, the Scar hung in the dark like a seam that had come undone. Torin’s silhouette had not moved. Desh was in the other tent with his instruments, Sera not watching Torin watch, Kael awake and still somewhere in the dark. The clock had run down to hours.

Ben set the datapad aside. Veraine’s voice stayed with him—precise, certain, detached in a way that was not cruelty but arithmetic. A mind at work. A mind ahead. She had seen the same structural feature he was now staring at from a tent on a ridge, and she had written it down without comment, without triumph, without anything but the cold clarity of someone who had turned suffering into a problem and solved it.

He could not close the knowledge. He could only sit with it, and with Torin’s stillness, and with the line of absence in the sky that would still be there when morning came.
