# Chapter 17

## Scene 17.01

The wrongness lurched.

Not a jolt. A settling. The floor of the Force seemed to drop another meter, and Ben felt it in his body before his mind caught up: a vertigo twist at the base of his skull, the same stale lurch as a gravity deck stuttering underfoot, except the Steady Returns held steady through hyperspace. The ship’s recycled air carried the usual tang of machine grease and old caf. Everything physical was fine.

Everything else was not.

“Got it,” Desh said from the pilot’s station.

His voice had gone flat, which meant he’d found something he hated and was already three steps into figuring out how much it would cost them. The bulkhead display flickered as he threw the back-sweep data across it—sensor traces from the meeting window, the ones he’d been running since they left the clearing.

Ben straightened in the copilot’s chair. “What am I looking at?”

“One of the Corridor’s intermediate anchor sites took a hit while we were sitting in that clearing listening to Veraine’s philosophy lecture.” Desh highlighted a cluster of signatures on the display. “Pira Kul’s work. Pressure-cut sequence in the node housing—clean, surgical. Somebody who knew exactly how the infrastructure was built and where it could be split.”

He shifted the highlight. “Vess Oran masked the traffic. False beacon hop in the local relay—anyone watching would’ve seen normal throughput. Serran Voth scraped the site archive with an old-language authentication key. And this—”

A faint residue ghosted the calibration lock. Ben didn’t need Desh to name it.

“Mira Dann,” he said.

“Low-level,” Desh agreed. “Not a Sith. Just Force-sensitive enough to finesse a lock calibrated for Jedi hands.” He paused. “She’s not the threat. She’s the tool.”

Ben stared at the display. The signatures were specific. Precise. Work that took planning, coordination, and a window when the people who might stop you were busy elsewhere.

The meeting had been cover.

Not a debate. Not a last attempt at persuasion. Veraine had sat in that clearing and counted the dead in front of Sera and asked questions she already knew the answers to—and while she held their attention, her people had been tearing down the seal’s infrastructure one system at a time.

Sincere and operational. Both true. The sincerity had made the operational part invisible.

“How bad?” Ben asked.

Desh didn’t answer at once. His hands moved over the sensor board, pulling data from the Corridor’s remaining active nodes, running degradation projections against the seal’s failure curve. The math scrolled across the bulkhead in a language Ben could read but didn’t want to.

Desh posted the number.

Hours. Not days.

The countdown numeral sat steady on the cockpit display, cold blue against the starfield. Ben felt his pulse against it—a separate thing, just out of sync with the ship’s hum. The pressure at the back of his head had settled into something constant, like altitude sickness that had decided to move in and stop paying rent.

“Veraine’s already moving,” Ben said. It wasn’t a question.

“She’s been moving since she left that clearing. She’s got a head start and a compromised seal to work with.” Desh turned from the board, his face carrying the same hard, level assessment he’d worn during the worst GAG ops—a man who’d done the arithmetic and wasn’t going to waste time wishing it had come out different. “We’re sprinting now.”

Ben pushed up from the copilot’s chair. His body wanted motion. His mind wanted control. Neither was going to get what it wanted, but standing still felt too much like surrender.

He paced into the common area. Sera stood at the galley counter with her back to the bulkhead, arms crossed, the posture she defaulted to when she was bracing for something she couldn’t fight. Kael sat near the engineering crawlspace hatch, hands open on his thighs, watching the room with the quiet attention of someone cataloging exits. Torin had taken the jump seat by the nav station, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, warmth still on his face but worn down into something Ben had to look at twice.

“All right,” Ben said. “We’re on the clock. Desh has the countdown posted. Sera—I need you on the seal’s structural specs. Anything in Gavran’s archive that tells us how the anchor sites interact, what happens when one gets compromised mid-cascade.”

Sera nodded once. Short. Controlled. “The archive has maintenance logs. I will find what applies.”

“Kael. You know Veraine’s operational habits better than anyone here. I need—”

“I know what she trained me to see,” Kael said. His voice stayed dry, the monk’s brevity intact. “That is not the same as knowing what she would do now.”

“It’s more than I have. Work with Desh on the approach vectors. If she’s got a preferred tactical pattern, I want it before we drop out of hyperspace.”

Kael held his gaze for a beat—something unreadable moving behind it—then nodded.

Torin hadn’t moved. He was watching Ben with an expression that might have been approval, or something else entirely, something Ben couldn’t name but felt in his chest like a shift in pressure.

“Torin—”

“You’re doing it again,” Torin said. Warm. Almost amused. “Assigning tasks before anyone’s had a chance to breathe.”

“There isn’t time to breathe.”

“There’s always time to breathe. Breathing is what keeps you from making the wrong call because you’re too tired to see the right one.” Torin leaned back in the jump seat, and the warmth on his face was genuine, the same easy paternal affection he’d worn since the first day Ben had met him. “But go on. What do you need from me?”

Ben heard his own voice come out louder than the task warranted. “Approach contingencies. If we’re walking into a compromised site with Veraine already there, I want options that don’t involve walking into a trap.”

The word landed harder than he’d meant it to. He heard the amplification in his own tone—the wound pressing on his patience, narrowing the distance between thought and speech—and caught it. Didn’t explain it. Just let it sit.

Sera’s jaw tightened. “She will expect us to come in force. That is what she would do.”

“Which is why we don’t,” Ben said.

“Which is why we consider what she would not expect,” Sera shot back, and the edge in her voice was sharper than the disagreement needed. She heard it too. Her arms tightened across her chest, and she said nothing else.

The wrongness was in the room with them. Ben could feel it pressing at everyone’s composure—a constant atmospheric weight that made small frictions sound like accusations and silences feel like withdrawals. He couldn’t stop it. He could only watch it work and try not to feed it.

He paced. The common area wasn’t big enough for real pacing, but his body didn’t care. Four steps to the galley counter. Four steps back. The durasteel deck was cold under his boots, the ship’s hum steady beneath the countdown numeral burning on the cockpit display behind him.

Desh’s voice carried from the pilot’s station. “I’m running parallel approach calculations. Kael’s feeding me Veraine’s known tactical preferences. We’ll have options before we drop out.”

“Good.”

“Ben.” Desh’s tone shifted—the flatness he used when he was about to say something Ben didn’t want to hear. “The long-range sensors just picked up another ship on a parallel course toward Veranthos.”

Ben stopped pacing. “Veraine.”

“Has to be. Same vector, same speed, same destination.” A pause. “She’s not hiding anymore.”

The convergence tightened. Two ships running the same line toward the same dying seal, one of them carrying a woman who’d spent a thousand years planning exactly what she’d do when she got there. The countdown numeral on the cockpit display didn’t change. It didn’t need to.

Ben turned back toward the common area—

And Torin was standing in the doorway.

Ben hadn’t heard him move. That wasn’t unusual; Torin moved quietly for a man his size. But something about the way he stood there, a silhouette against the cockpit glow, weight settled on both feet like a man who’d already chosen his ground, made Ben’s stomach drop before Torin opened his mouth.

“We need to talk,” Torin said.

His voice was calm. Warm. The tone of someone who had already decided and was now only negotiating the announcement.

“About what happens when we get there.”

The line itself was quiet. What it announced was not.

Ben had heard that tone before. The cadence lived in a part of his memory he didn’t visit by choice—the warmth of someone who’d made up his mind and was going to tell you about it whether you were ready or not. The certainty under the ease. The finality dressed as conversation.

Jacen had sounded like that. Before the worst conversations.

Ben’s stomach stayed dropped. He couldn’t name what decision Torin had made. He only knew it was made, and that the argument Torin had just announced would happen later, and that when it came it would split something that couldn’t be put back together.

He knew the cadence. He didn’t yet know the shape.

“When we get there,” Ben repeated.

Torin nodded. The warmth on his face was still genuine. That was the worst part. “When we get there.”

Behind him, the countdown numeral held steady. Ahead of him, the passive track showed two ships converging on a point Ben couldn’t see yet but could feel—a pressure at the back of his head, the Force settling into a lower floor, a weight that wasn’t going to lift until something broke.

Torin’s silhouette in the doorway carried the exact weight of an old remembered cadence.

Ben braced himself. The shape of what was coming remained opaque.

## Scene 17.02

The argument started about hyperspace routes.

“The Darrpa approach is compromised,” Sera said, tapping the nav display hard enough to leave a smear. “Veraine’s people hit the intermediate anchor there. We have to assume she’s watching the approach corridor.”

“Which is why we use it,” Torin said. He was still in the doorway, arms crossed, his voice warm enough to sound almost reassuring. “She won’t expect us to walk through a door she thinks she’s locked.”

“She won’t expect it because it is tactically idiotic.”

The word landed wrong. Ben felt it in his chest a heartbeat before his ears caught up—a flash of heat that didn’t fit the comment. Sera had been sharp, yes, but not insulting. The reaction in him was too big for the exchange, a sudden flare of irritation reaching for anger and not caring about the difference.

“It’s not idiotic if she’s already committed her resources elsewhere,” Torin said, still calm, still easy. “She can’t cover every approach.”

“She does not need to cover every approach. She needs to cover the one we are most likely to take.” Sera turned from the nav console, and Ben saw the tight line of her jaw, the way her arms had crossed higher on her chest. “You are proposing we gamble on her incompetence. She has not been incompetent once.”

“I’m proposing we use her expectations against her. That’s not gambling. That’s—”

“That’s what you would do,” Sera said.

The silence after that felt physical. It pressed against Ben’s ears the way cabin pressure shifted before a seal failed. Torin’s expression didn’t change; the warmth stayed in place, but something behind it went still.

“Meaning what?” Torin asked.

“Meaning you think like a Battle Master. You always have. Direct engagement, force concentration, the assumption that your opponent will break before you do.” Sera’s voice climbed, and Ben could hear it climbing. “That worked against the Brotherhood. It will not work against Veraine. She is not a formation you can shatter.”

“I’m aware of what she is.”

“Are you?”

Ben opened his mouth to cut in and heard his own voice come out louder than he’d intended. “Enough.”

Both of them looked at him. Sera’s face had closed down, all brace and edge. Torin’s was unreadable—warmth still there, but thinned, like light through a door pulled almost shut.

“We don’t have time to litigate who thinks like what,” Ben said. “Desh is running approach vectors. We’ll have options before we drop out. Fighting about it now doesn’t help.”

“It helps if the option we choose gets us killed,” Sera said.

“Then we don’t choose that one.”

“That is not—”

Sera’s palm slapped the nav console.

The crack of it against metal silenced the room for a breath. The display flickered once and steadied. Her hand stayed where it had landed, fingers spread, tendons standing out pale against her skin.

Nobody moved.

Ben felt the air tighten around his ribs, the wound’s weight settling over the common area like a front moving in and deciding to stay. His pulse was too fast. His breathing had gone shallow. The anger that had spiked at Sera’s first sharp word was still there, banked but alive, and with a cold lurch he knew the heat was not entirely his.

He caught himself mid-breath.

“Sera,” he started, and stopped. The sentence waiting behind his teeth was the wrong one. Too sharp. Too much. He could feel it crowding up, louder than the argument deserved. He didn’t say it.

Sera lifted her hand from the console. The smear from her finger was still there, a faint print on the display. She didn’t look at it.

“I apologize,” she said. Her voice had gone controlled again, discipline back in place, but Ben could see the effort it took. “That was—”

“Don’t,” Ben said. “It’s not just you.”

He didn’t explain. He didn’t need to. The room had that sour edge to it now, the kind that made small frictions sound like accusations and pauses feel like slamming doors. They all felt it. They’d been feeling it for hours, since the countdown had posted and the second ship had appeared on the passive track and Torin had made his doorway announcement in that calm, decided cadence Ben couldn’t stop hearing.

Torin hadn’t moved from the doorway. His arms were still crossed. The warmth on his face had settled into something Ben couldn’t read—not cold, but distant, the way a fire looked through a viewport.

“The approach vector is a real question,” Torin said. “We should answer it.”

“We will,” Ben said. “When Desh has the data.”

“And if the data doesn’t give us a clear answer?”

“Then we make the best call we can with what we have. That’s what we’ve been doing.”

Torin nodded slowly. The motion was easy, unhurried. It didn’t match the strain in the room. It didn’t match anything.

Kael spoke.

“What’s wrong with him?”

The question came out quiet. Flat. No adornment at all. He was sitting near the engineering crawlspace hatch, hands open on his thighs, watching the room with the same still attention he always wore. He hadn’t moved during the argument. He hadn’t spoken. He’d waited, the way he always waited, until the room forgot he was there.

He wasn’t waiting now.

The question dropped into the charged silence and made the space around it seem to contract. Ben felt it in his chest—simple, devastating because it was simple, aimed at whoever would answer. Kael wasn’t looking at Torin. He wasn’t looking at Ben. His gaze rested on the middle distance, the monk’s register carrying no accusation and no mercy.

Ben knew what Kael was asking. He’d been not asking it himself for days, since the thin place, since Torin’s nostalgic comfort in the destabilized Force, since the easy warmth that had started to read as something else. He’d been avoiding the words because once they were spoken, he’d have to deal with them.

Kael had just spoken them.

Ben looked at Sera.

She was still standing at the nav console. Her hands had gone still on the edge of the display, the fingers that had struck the housing a moment ago now resting motionless against the metal. Ben knew the shape of the sentence she would have said two days ago—the defense, the explanation, the reason Torin was not what Kael was implying. He’d heard her make that defense before, in the thin place, in arguments, in the quiet moments when someone questioned Torin’s judgment and she’d stepped in to close the gap.

The sentence didn’t come.

Sera looked at the table.

Her eyes found the scratched durasteel surface of the galley counter and stayed there. Her hands didn’t move. Her jaw didn’t tighten. She didn’t look at Torin. She didn’t look at Kael. She looked at the table, and the silence where the defense should have been said more than any words she might have spoken.

Ben watched her and felt something shift in his chest that had nothing to do with the wound. It was colder than that. Cleaner.

Torin was still in the doorway. He hadn’t reacted to Kael’s question. He hadn’t reacted to Sera’s silence. His expression held the same distant warmth it had worn since the argument started, and Ben couldn’t tell if he hadn’t heard or had simply chosen not to answer.

The silence stretched.

Kael didn’t repeat the question. He’d asked it once, and the answer had come—not in words, but in the shape of the sentence Sera hadn’t said. He gave a small nod and looked back at his hands.

Ben didn’t know how long they stood there. Long enough for the weight in the room to settle from a moving front into something steadier. Long enough for his pulse to come down from the spike the argument had driven into it. Long enough for the countdown numeral on the cockpit display to tick over again.

Desh came through from the cockpit.

He was carrying four ration packs, balanced against his chest with the practiced ease of someone who’d done this before and would do it again. He set one on the galley counter beside Sera without breaking stride. He handed one to Kael, who took it with a small nod. He crossed to Ben and pressed a pack into his hands.

Ben took it. He didn’t thank him. Desh didn’t expect it.

The last pack stayed in Desh’s hand. He looked at Torin, still in the doorway, still distant. Torin shook his head once. Desh set the pack on the jump seat and sat down.

Nobody spoke.

The silence after the argument told them more than the argument had. Ben sat with his back against the bulkhead, the ration pack unopened in his hands, and felt exhaustion settle in. The pressure behind his ribs was still there, the sour edge still brushing at the corners of his awareness, but it wasn’t climbing anymore. The wound remained. The countdown remained. Veraine’s ship was still on the passive track, converging on the same point they were.

But somewhere in the middle of it all, Kael had asked the question, and Sera had not answered.

The room had changed.

## Scene 17.03

Desh said it without turning from the sensor display.

“Contact. Parallel course, bearing two-seven-one mark four. Same vector we’re riding.”

Ben was halfway across the cockpit before he realized he’d moved. His hand hit the back of the copilot’s chair, and the jolt ran up his arm, a small correction that came a heartbeat too late. He’d been wound tight since the argument in the common area, since the countdown had lit in the corner of the display, since the second ship had flickered onto the passive track and Torin had filled the doorway with that calm, decided cadence Ben still couldn’t shake. The alertness had been sitting in his chest like a coiled spring. Now it had something to bite into.

“Veraine’s?” he asked.

“Signature matches the drive profile from the bunker logs.” Desh kept his voice level, the same operational calm he’d used through every crisis since Terminus Station. “She’s not sprinting. Holding steady parallel about six light-hours to spinward. Same speed, same heading.”

“She knows we’re here.”

“She’s not trying to hide.” Desh tapped the display, and the contact brightened into a single hard point on the long-range plot. “If she wanted to lose us, she’d have jumped by now. She’s keeping station.”

Ben leaned over the nav console and pulled up the trajectory overlay. The Steady Returns’ course drew itself as a blue arc, clean and computer-precise, curving toward the blank space on the charts where Veranthos waited. He overlaid the sensor contact—Veraine’s ship, drive signature matching the bunker readings—and watched the computer think.

Two lines. One meeting point.

The numbers settled in the corner of the display. Arrival windows separated by less than four hours.

Ben’s grip tightened on the chair back. The spring in his chest didn’t loosen. It cinched down harder, the alertness narrowing into something colder. He’d been running scenarios since Gavran’s fortress—intercept courses, blockade positions, the geometry of arriving first and holding the approaches. Every one of them had assumed Veraine would try to beat them there, rush the seal, use her head start.

She wasn’t rushing.

She’d matched their speed and settled into formation like a ship joining a convoy. The endgame wasn’t a race. It was a scheduled arrival.

“She timed this,” Ben said.

Desh glanced at him. “Looks that way.”

“She’s been ahead of us since the bunker. She could have pushed harder, gotten there days ago, started work on the seal before we cleared the Corridor.” Ben heard how flat his own voice sounded, the words coming out as fast as he thought them, not shaped for anyone else. “She waited. She wanted us to see her.”

“Or she wanted to arrive at the same time.” Desh’s tone went careful, the way it did when he was handing over something he knew Ben wouldn’t like. “Some people like an audience.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was watching the display, the two trajectories locked now, the convergence point fixed in a system erased from every chart the modern Order possessed. The countdown numeral in the corner ticked over. Hours, not days. The vector had settled. Nothing left to adjust, no approach to revise, no window to exploit. They would arrive when they arrived, and Veraine would arrive when she chose to, and the gap between those two numbers was the only variable left.

The weight of it settled into Ben’s shoulders. Not dread—dread was for things that might not happen. This was the recognition of a door closing, the sound of a lock catching somewhere on the far side of a room you’d just stepped into.

Torin was standing in the cockpit hatch.

Ben hadn’t heard him come up. He was simply there, arms folded, shoulder against the frame, looking at the nav display with an expression Ben couldn’t name at first. It wasn’t the distant warmth he’d worn during the argument, that fire-through-a-viewport look that had made Sera slap the console and Kael ask his quiet question. This was something closer. More immediate.

Torin was watching the convergence point the way a man watched a city he’d left long ago and never expected to see again.

“She’s not running,” Torin said. It wasn’t a question.

“She’s not running,” Ben said.

Torin nodded. The motion was slow, unhurried, the same easy rhythm he’d carried since the fortress. But his eyes stayed on the display, on the two lines sliding together, and Ben saw something move behind them that didn’t match the calm.

Anticipation.

Not dread. Not the grim resignation that had settled into Ben’s own chest. Torin was looking at the endgame and seeing something he wanted to reach. Something he’d been waiting for.

Ben’s attention shifted from the display to Torin’s face and back again. The connection was there, half-formed, visible but not yet clear. Torin’s readiness and Veraine’s timing—those two things rhymed. They shared a quality Ben couldn’t name, a certainty that the convergence was not a crisis to manage but a date to keep.

He didn’t ask. The question crowded behind his teeth anyway, but he’d learned enough about Torin in the past weeks to know that asking straight out got him warmth and a story and not much else. The answers Torin gave were always true. They just weren’t the whole answer.

Ben pulled his datapad from the console slot and stepped back from the nav display. His legs took him to the jump seat near the engineering hatch without any conscious decision, the motion automatic, the way you moved in a cockpit you’d spent weeks living in. The datapad’s glow was low, the screen dimmed for night-cycle ops. He tapped it brighter, and the recovered ledger fragment resolved in pale text against the dark.

He’d read it twice already. Once when Desh had pulled it from the bunker terminal, skimming for anything tactical, and once more during the long quiet after the argument, when the room had changed and Sera had stopped defending and Kael’s question had hung there unanswered. Both times he’d read for information. This time he read because he needed to understand what they were converging toward.

The entry was short. A working note, undated, filed under a citation reference Ben recognized from the bunker archives—Karness Muur, early Sith alchemical taxonomy, the sort of source a scholar would cite without comment because the choice of source said enough. Veraine’s hand was precise. No contractions. Academic syntax that would have sounded formal spoken aloud, but on the page it was simply efficient, the register of a mind that didn’t waste words on ornament.

She had been examining the seal’s pressure dynamics. Not theorizing—measuring. The entry recorded a fluctuation pattern in the containment lattice, correlated it against the failure rate of the outer anchors, and noted that the cascade acceleration matched a model she’d built from first principles. There was no triumph in the note. No hint of victory. Just the work, laid out in clean sentences that assumed the reader would follow the math or wouldn’t, and either outcome was acceptable because the note was not written for a reader.

It was written for her. A mind recording its own motion. The residue of someone who thought at night and wrote down what she found because the writing was part of the thinking.

Ben read the last line twice.

*The interval between anchor failures shortens per the projected curve. The seal’s structural integrity is now a function of time rather than resistance. Arrival at Veranthos should be timed to the final anchor’s collapse, not to the seal’s. The seal is already gone.*

No drama. No declaration. Just arithmetic, rendered in the same measured rhythm the group had heard in the clearing, when Veraine had asked her questions and waited for answers she already knew. The voice in the ledger was the voice in the clearing. Her mind and her ship were the same shape. She thought the way her movement tracked—precise, patient, timed to a curve she’d calculated before anyone else knew the calculation was possible.

Ben set the datapad on his knee.

The cockpit hummed around him, the steady background note of the Steady Returns’ systems running through their night-cycle routines. Desh was still at the sensor display, his posture relaxed but his attention sharp, the way he looked when he was tracking something he didn’t trust. Torin hadn’t moved from the hatch. His reflection lay over the viewport, superimposed on the starfield, his expression still carrying that quiet anticipation Ben couldn’t stop seeing.

Two ships. One convergence point. A world where the Force had been torn a thousand years ago and never healed.

The countdown ticked over.

Ben didn’t reach for the datapad again. He sat with his back against the bulkhead and let the stillness settle in, the cold clarity that came from understanding an enemy’s working mind while watching her ship hold its course in the dark. No dread. No hope. Just the shape of what was coming, rendered in two trajectories and a line of handwriting that didn’t need to explain itself.

The seal was already gone. Veraine had done the math. And somewhere in the quiet of the cockpit, with Torin watching the nav display like a man coming home and Desh tracking the contact that wasn’t trying to hide, Ben understood that the only question left was what happened when they all arrived at the same time.
