# Chapter 10

## Scene 10.01

The outpost door slid open on tracks that hadn’t been serviced in a thousand years, and Ben stepped through into something worse than a temple.

The corridor stretched ahead in hard prefabricated lines—durasteel panels, dead light strips inset overhead, modular construction thrown up in days and meant to outlast the people who built it. Brotherhood of Darkness engineering. No statuary. No altar. No holovid grandeur. Just a hallway wide enough for cargo, with junction boxes at regular intervals and the stale chemical tang of old insulation.

A workplace.

Ben stopped two paces in and let his eyes adjust to the emergency lighting Desh had brought up from the ship. The beam swept across a control panel, a bank of dead monitors, the corner of a workstation chair still tucked under its desk. Someone had left for the day and never come back.

“Sith architecture,” Sera said behind him. Her voice had the flatness she used when something bothered her more than she wanted to show.

“No,” Ben said. “Sith prefab. There’s a difference.”

Desh moved past him, scanner raised, reading the space with the same unhurried professionalism he’d used at every stop along the Corridor. “Power’s dead. Atmospherics are stable, but I wouldn’t trust the scrubbers long-term. Whatever she ran here, she ran off her own supply.”

Veraine.

The energy signatures Desh had picked up from orbit were still bleeding residual heat into the walls. Ben felt it through the Force before his skin caught up. Equipment had been running here. Days ago, not weeks. She’d spent time in this place, and she’d left it arranged.

The corridor opened into a larger room, and the team spread out by instinct. Sera took the left wall, checking power couplings and data ports with the efficiency of someone who’d cleared hostile structures before. Torin moved right, his heavier footsteps the only sound beyond the hum of Desh’s scanner. Kael drifted toward the center, quiet, his attention settling on a workstation that still had a data tablet docked in its cradle.

Ben watched him a beat too long. Kael’s movements had changed since the thin place. Not slower—more deliberate. The way a man moved when he was thinking about something he hadn’t said.

“Anything?” Ben asked.

“Equipment’s been pulled,” Desh said from across the room. “Not scavenged—curated. She took specific components and left the rest. Power regulators, field stabilizers, a whole rack of signal boosters.” He paused, running the scanner over an empty shelf. “Whatever she’s building, she’s building it in pieces. This was a supply stop.”

“A supply stop with a message,” Sera said.

Ben felt it before he understood it. The dark-side residue bleeding through the Weave’s thinning places wasn’t just in the air—it clung to the room in layers. Heat in a chamber where a fight had gone on too long. Anger with nowhere left to live. Cruelty with no living mind behind it anymore, only the stain it left. The Force tasted wrong there, sharp at the back of his throat.

Sera’s jaw tightened. She’d felt it too—Ben saw the strain in her shoulders, the way her hand stayed near her lightsaber without closing on it. Discipline holding against pressure.

“Each room we clear,” she said quietly, “it gets worse.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was tracking the residue through the Force, trying to separate the contamination from his own reactions. The cruelty wasn’t his. The malice wasn’t his. But feeling them meant carrying some fraction of their weight, and he hated that part of the job. His discipline tightened in response—the old Skywalker reflex, control as armor.

He moved closer to Sera without deciding to. Protective positioning. She noticed and said nothing.

Torin had stopped at a bank of dead monitors, his hand resting on the console. “She was systematic. Look at the cable routing—she pulled the data cores but left the housings intact. Someone who knew exactly what she wanted and exactly how long she had to take it.”

“How long did she have?” Ben asked.

“Three days. Maybe four.” Desh straightened from the empty shelf, scanner still running. “Long enough to strip the place of everything she needed. Not long enough to hide what she was doing.”

Kael had reached the workstation.

Ben saw him pick up the data tablet—an ancient unit, Sith-manufactured, its screen still dark. Kael’s thumb found the activation plate, and the display flickered to life in pale amber script. Sith-tradition notation. The characters were sharp and angular, arranged in a format Ben couldn’t read but recognized from the Codex fragments Sera had translated.

Kael read.

His face didn’t change.

Ben had watched Kael through enough tense moments to know the tells that usually gave him away—the slight tension at the corner of his jaw when he was calculating odds, the way his eyes moved when he was reading a room. None of that showed now. His expression was the controlled blankness of someone who had learned to hide reactions in places where showing them got people killed.

But his hands. When Kael set the tablet down, he did it with the careful precision of someone handling ordnance that might still be live. Slow. Exact. Silent. The tablet met the workstation surface without a click.

Then Kael walked out of the room.

He didn’t look at Ben. He didn’t look at Sera or Torin or Desh. He crossed the doorway and vanished into the dark corridor beyond, and his footsteps faded into the outpost’s silence without a word of explanation.

Ben stared at the tablet. The amber script still glowed, still unreadable, still waiting for someone who could understand what Veraine had written.

She’d left it for Kael. Not for the group, not for the Jedi who’d come looking for her—for Kael specifically. She knew who he was. And she’d written him a message in a notation only he could read, at a workstation in a Brotherhood outpost she’d spent three days stripping for parts.

Veraine wasn’t running from them. She was moving toward something, and she wanted them to follow. Wanted Kael to follow, at least, and Kael had just walked out of the room with whatever she’d told him locked behind his eyes.

The dark-side residue pressed against Ben’s awareness. Cruelty without a source. Malice without a target. And now this—a message without a reader, except the one who’d already read it and chosen silence.

Sera was staring at the doorway Kael had disappeared through. Torin’s hand had gone still on the console. Desh had stopped scanning, his attention fixed on the tablet with the flat assessment of someone who’d just watched a variable enter the equation and started recalculating the odds.

Ben didn’t move. The tablet glowed amber in the dark.

Kael knew something. And he wasn’t telling.

## Scene 10.02

Desh had been at it for the better part of an hour. The operations table no longer looked like a table. He’d turned it into a grid, every square holding something pulled from Veraine’s trail.

Equipment manifests lay in neat stacks, cross-referenced against the outpost’s original inventory. Disassembly photos sat beside them, each stamped with a time and tagged with the component’s designation. A tactical map occupied the center, spread flat and marked in Desh’s small, precise handwriting—the script of a man who wrote reports people would read looking for mistakes.

The room smelled faintly of cold dust, old insulation, and the metallic tang that clung to Brotherhood machinery after years of closed-cycle air. Ben stood at the edge of the table, close enough to see the ink strokes without leaning. The dark-side weight in the room sat on him like a pack he’d gotten used to carrying, and pushing through it made his thoughts narrow and sharp. He wanted to move. The evidence was laid out. The pattern was waiting. Standing still felt like wasting time.

Desh didn’t hurry. He set the last photo beside its manifest entry and straightened, scanner still in his off hand.

“Power regulators,” he said. “Field stabilizers. A full rack of signal boosters.” He tapped the manifest. “She left the redundant power couplings. Left the backup atmospheric processors. Took the things that let you build something that talks to something else.”

Sera stood on the far side of the table, both hands flat on the surface. She’d kept them there since Desh started laying out the photos, and Ben had noticed without saying anything. The weight in the outpost was heavier than it had been in the corridor—thicker, closer, enough to make him want a bulkhead or a wall at his back. Sera was bracing against it.

“She’s not scavenging,” she said. “A scavenger takes what’s valuable. She’s selecting.”

“Yeah,” Desh said. “She is.”

He reached across the table and dragged the tactical map toward the center. The Corridor stretched across it in a line of annotated stops—the outpost, the relay station they’d passed two days ago, the survey cache Veraine had hit before they arrived. Each site was marked with what she’d taken and what she’d left.

Desh traced the route with one finger.

“She hit this outpost first. Took the signal boosters and half the field stabilizers. Then she doubled back to the relay station—picked up a set of frequency modulators she’d left behind on the first pass, plus a power converter that wasn’t on the local inventory.” His finger moved again. “The survey cache was last. She took geological sensors and a deep-core mapping unit. Left the biological sampling kit and the atmospheric probes.”

Ben leaned over the map. The pattern was there, just at the edge of clarity, the way a ship’s silhouette came out of hyperspace—first shape, then class, then name.

“She knew what was at each stop before she got there,” he said.

“She had the original deployment manifests,” Sera said. Her voice had tightened. “The Brotherhood built these outposts. She would’ve known the standard loadouts.”

Desh shook his head once. “More than that.”

He slid one of the older sheets free from beneath a photo stack. The paper was a copy, not original—faded columns, old Brotherhood coding, annotations in a hand that wasn’t his.

“Archived transfer records,” he said. “Pulled from the outpost cache. Not complete, but enough. She wasn’t guessing standard loadouts. She was reading the revisions. What got moved, what got swapped, what got stored off-manifest after the Corridor started going bad.”

His finger tapped three separate entries in quick succession.

“She knew this relay station had modulators because they were rerouted here six years ago and never logged into central inventory. She knew the survey cache still had the deep-core unit because the recovery order got drafted and never executed. She knew this outpost kept spare boosters in secondary storage because the Brotherhood flagged the room in an internal maintenance note.”

Sera’s hands pressed harder against the table.

“She had archive access.”

“She had time,” Desh said. “And help.”

He said it without emphasis, the way he’d laid out everything else. Ben looked up.

Desh drew a short line on the map between two marked sites. “We were still pulling survivors out of the wreckage and trying to figure out what the Codex was telling us. During that window, she didn’t need to do all of this herself. She only needed the list.”

He touched the first stop. “A crew here, already in place to start stripping the relay as soon as they got her signal.”

Second stop. “Another team on the cache.”

Then the outpost.

“She comes to ground where she needs to be seen, checks what they missed, takes the pieces that matter, leaves the pieces that make the pattern visible, and moves.”

Ben felt his posture change, shoulders drawing tighter as the route on the table stopped looking like a trail and became timing.

“The Ash-Tithe Compact,” Sera said.

Desh nodded. “Split tasks. Split transport. Split risk. She doesn’t have to be physically present at every site to stay ahead of us. She just has to have assigned the work before we even started moving.”

He tapped the archived sheet again. “And the manifests tell her where to send them.”

The pressure in the room seemed to settle lower, not lifting, just changing shape. Ben looked down at the evidence and saw the blank spaces differently. Not delays. Not lucky guesses. Prep.

“She staged it before we knew there was a route,” he said.

“Before we had one,” Desh said. “We were reading archives and triaging survivors. She was reading archive inventories and assigning pickups.”

The words hit cleanly because there was nothing mystical in them. No aura, no feeling in the Force, no hidden meaning waiting to be translated. Freight. Timing. Labor. Ben felt the correction land in the room.

Desh traced the route again, slower this time.

“She’s not running,” he said.

No drama. No pause to soften it. Desh said it the way he’d deliver a threat assessment in a briefing room—the conclusion first, the evidence after, and no time wasted on how anyone felt about it.

“She’s curating. Every stop along this Corridor, she’s taking specific components and leaving specific things behind. The things she leaves aren’t junk—they’re working equipment she could’ve used. She’s leaving them because she wants us to know what she took.”

Sera’s jaw set hard enough to show. Ben saw the tension run up her forearms and stop at her shoulders, held there by discipline.

“She’s leading us,” Ben said.

It wasn’t a question. Desh nodded anyway.

“We’ve been reading this as a pursuit. She got ahead because she escaped first, and we’ve been following her trail.” He gestured at the map. “That isn’t what this is. She’s ahead because she pre-positioned people and started stripping sites while we were still trying to understand what had happened. Then she left us the route she wanted us to see.”

The room went quiet. The hum of Desh’s scanner was the only sound until he switched it off.

Ben looked at the map again. The stops along the Corridor made a line, but not a straight one. It bent around empty sectors and doubled back at the relay station before continuing outward. A flight path would’ve been direct. A retreat would’ve been fast. This was neither.

This was a route. Prepared. Marked.

Veraine had been in the outpost for three days. She’d had time to strip it clean or wreck what she left behind. She’d done neither. She’d taken exactly what she needed and left exactly what she wanted them to find. Even the order of the stops made sense now: not nearest first, but whichever pickups her crews could complete while she moved them forward.

The question they’d been asking was where she was going. That was the wrong question.

The question was what she was building.

Ben’s hands stayed steady on the table’s edge. His breathing had gone shallow—not from fear, but from the effort of holding the pattern in his head without trying to finish it. He could feel the shape of it, the way the components Desh had listed would fit together if you knew what they were meant to power. He didn’t know. The answer wasn’t there yet.

He pulled back from the table and straightened.

“If she wants us to follow,” he said, “then pursuing her along the Corridor is either an opportunity or a trap.”

“Yeah,” Desh said.

“Either way,” Ben said, looking at the line Veraine had written across the map with freight, crews, and lead time, “we stop treating this like we’re catching up. We’re on the route she made for us. We need to understand what she’s building before the next stop makes that choice for us.”

Sera lifted her hands off the table. She flexed them once, then let them fall to her sides. “We don’t have enough to tell which.”

“No,” Ben said. “We don’t.”

The weight in the room sat there like a third presence. Ben felt it against his skin, at the back of his throat, in the place where the Force should have been clear and wasn’t. Desh had done what the Force-users couldn’t—read Veraine’s intent through freight manifests and disassembly photos and the patient work of cataloguing what she’d left behind. The realization settled into Ben’s chest without fanfare. It was simply true.

He looked at the map one more time. The route curved away from the outpost and kept going, marked in Desh’s small, precise handwriting, each stop annotated with what Veraine had taken and what she’d chosen to leave.

She wasn’t just ahead of them.

She had written the chase.

## Scene 10.03

Kael was outside.

Ben found him on the outpost’s exterior steps, a long shelf of stone cut into the mountainside and dropping into blackness after the first few feet. The cold bit hard. It climbed through the stone, through Ben’s boots, and into his hands before he’d taken three steps from the inner door.

Kael sat near the edge of the platform with his legs drawn up and his arms resting on his knees. His head was tipped back. He was looking at the sky.

Ben paused at the door and let it hiss shut behind him. The sound carried in the thin air, and Kael must have heard it, but he didn’t look over.

The constellations were wrong. Ben had known that since their first night on the surface. These stars were not the ones above Coruscant or Yavin or any Core world he’d ever stood under. Still, he hadn’t really looked at them before. There had been the outpost, the operations table, Desh’s map, the dark side pressing at his temples like a headache he couldn’t shake.

Out here, the weight eased a little. It was still there—the whole mountainside held it—but distance and open air thinned it. Ben crossed toward the steps. His boots scraped stone. Kael still didn’t turn.

He sat beside Kael and left a deliberate arm’s length between them. Not crowding. Not putting on distance, either. Just enough room for Kael to close if he wanted, or leave alone if he didn’t.

The stone was cold enough to soak through Ben’s trousers at once. He ignored it.

Kael’s Force presence had been a knot ever since the stasis chamber. Guarded. Not hostile—Ben knew hostile, and this wasn’t that—but drawn tight, like a man holding himself still for the next blow. Tonight the knot had loosened a fraction. Not open. Just less clenched.

Ben said nothing. He let the silence stretch.

Overhead, the sky spilled unfamiliar stars. No constellations he could name. No landmarks. Kael had slept for a thousand years while the galaxy moved under him, and when he woke, the sky belonged to somebody else.

“It’s strange,” Kael said at last. His voice was low, roughened by the cold. “I knew it would be different. Precession. Stellar drift. I worked out the numbers once, in the chamber, when there wasn’t anything else to do.” He paused. “The numbers were right. It’s still strange.”

Ben glanced up. The stars were sharp in the thin air, bright enough to sting. He tried to find a pattern and failed.

“What were they?” he asked.

Kael lifted one hand without looking. “There. The Hook. It was a marker for the inner Corridor—traders used it to find the relay station. The Hook and the Eye.” His hand fell. “The Eye’s gone. Drifted off. The Hook is still there, but the shape is wrong.”

Ben found the cluster Kael had pointed out. Five stars in a curve that might have been a hook if you were being generous. Or might have been nothing at all.

The cold settled deeper. Ben’s breath clouded and vanished.

He could feel the moment where he could start talking—where the machinery of apology and explanation could begin turning, where he could say something about the Order’s responsibility, about the Reformations, about the thousand years of silence that had buried what Gavran had done. The words were there. They would be true. They would also be a way around the thing he’d come out here to say.

He didn’t take that path.

“What was done to you,” Ben said, “was indefensible.”

No qualification. No context. No lecture about how the Order had changed, or what the Reformations meant, or how the Jedi of today differed from the Jedi Lords who had sealed twelve people into living infrastructure and called it containment.

Just that.

Kael didn’t move. His head stayed tipped toward the sky. His breathing stayed even.

Then his hand went into his pocket.

Ben watched him draw out the data tablet—Veraine’s tablet, the one he’d found in the outpost, the one he hadn’t shown anyone. Kael turned it over in his hands once. Then again. The motion was slow and controlled, the way someone turns a stone over when he isn’t sure he wants to see what’s underneath.

Ben felt the moment sharpen.

The tablet turned again. Kael’s thumb traced its edge.

Then he slipped it back into his pocket.

“I know,” Kael said.

The silence came back, but it had changed. Less like waiting. More like the pause after a door closes.

Ben didn’t press. He sat with the cold and the strange stars and the loosened knot of Kael’s presence, and he waited.

“She’ll come for me,” Kael said after a while. “At some point. She’ll try to recruit me.”

Ben kept his hands still on his knees. The ground didn’t move, but something underneath it did—a shift he felt before he could name it.

“I’m not refusing what you said,” Kael went on. His voice was still quiet, still rough, but there was something in it now that hadn’t been there before. Not warmth. Not trust. Honesty, stripped bare. “You’re the first Jedi who’s treated me like a person instead of a problem. I’m not refusing that.”

He stopped. The cold filled the space between them.

“She might have a point.”

He didn’t say which point. The moral argument about the seal. The strategic argument for breaking it. The personal one—the parallel between them, the conscript and the alchemist, both used as infrastructure, both sealed away, both waking to a galaxy that had moved on without them.

Ben couldn’t tell. Kael wasn’t going to tell him.

The uncertainty settled in, cold and certain as the stone beneath them. Kael had been treated like a person, and the cost of that recognition sat here now in the dark under stars neither of them could navigate by—Kael caught between gratitude and the pull of someone who shared his wound, and Ben left with a choice he couldn’t control and couldn’t ignore.

The constellations turned overhead, silent and indifferent. The Hook held. The Eye was gone.
