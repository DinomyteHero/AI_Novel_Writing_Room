# Chapter 23

## Scene 23.01

Ben pulled the datapad from his belt and laid it on the grass between them.

Kael didn’t look at it. His gaze stayed on the valley, on the Scar’s ugly pulse below the ridgeline, on sunlight spreading gold across the far peaks. His hands rested on his thighs, loose and still. The grass he’d been worrying lay in a small green scatter beside his knee.

“The resonance structure needs three anchor points,” Ben said. “Three traditions. Jedi, Sith, and a third that doesn’t map cleanly to either—Sera’s tradition, the one Gavran tried to bury. The Codex called it Weave-Resonance. Ankhural thought it could stabilize the seal by spreading the load across incompatible approaches. They never tested it. They didn’t trust each other enough.”

He tapped the datapad. A schematic bloomed: three nodes in a triangle around the Scar’s mapped perimeter, with Desh’s amplification array marked at the fourth point, offset and exposed. Blue and amber lines sketched force and counterforce; beneath them, Sera’s precise notation packed the math tight.

“I anchor the Jedi component. Sera anchors the third tradition. Desh runs the array—he’s the practical anchor, the non-Force component that keeps the resonance from folding back on itself. Without him, the whole thing collapses in about ninety seconds.”

Kael’s expression didn’t change. His Force presence stayed shuttered—never hostile, just closed in the way Ben had learned to read over the weeks since the fortress. A door held shut from the inside.

“You’d be the third anchor,” Ben said. “Sith-trained. Brotherhood methodology. The resonance needs all three because the seal was built to contain all three. Without that third anchor, the spread goes asymmetrical. The seal fails. Veraine wins.”

He stopped. The words had come out flat and exact, the way a mission brief sounded once you stripped away everything that wasn’t true. He hadn’t meant to sound that way. It was what happened when you told someone the truth and left nothing out.

“The risk,” he said, “is that no one has ever done this. The resonance could cascade. It could kill all three of us. It could kill Desh, who’ll be standing at the most exposed point with no Force-sense to warn him. The math says the odds of a stable resonance on the first attempt are low. Sera’s best estimate puts them below forty percent.”

He let the number hang.

The Scar pulsed below them, a slow, ugly throb that made something behind Ben’s eyes tilt before settling again. Morning light had reached the valley floor now, picking out the dark line of trees, the silver thread of the stream, the wrong-colored seam that cut through everything like a wound that had forgotten how to close.

Kael’s fingers moved. Not toward the datapad. Toward the grass beside his knee—another blade, pulled slow, roots and dark soil clinging.

“You’re asking me to stand in a resonance structure built on math no one has tested,” Kael said. “With a Jedi and a Jedi Lord’s lieutenant and a pilot who can’t feel the thing that might kill him. Against a Sith alchemist who’s been planning her move for a thousand years. On odds below forty percent.”

His voice was dry. Level. The same register he always used—the monk’s brevity, the understatement that could land like a blade if you weren’t ready for it.

“Yes,” Ben said.

Kael pulled another blade of grass. Silence stretched between them—ten seconds, twenty, the pressure at the edges of everything making the air feel heavier than it should. Ben kept his hands on his knees. Open. Visible. The datapad between them like an offering, not a demand.

“What are the actual odds?” Kael asked.

The question came out flat. Not a challenge. Not a test. A man asking for the number because he’d been given numbers before and learned, over a thousand years of being used, that numbers were always the first thing institutions lied about.

Ben felt the weight of it settle in his chest. The answer he couldn’t soften. The trust he couldn’t manufacture. The invitation that could only be answered by telling the truth and letting Kael decide what it was worth.

“Honestly?” He met Kael’s eyes. “Lower than forty percent. Sera’s estimate assumes optimal conditions. We won’t have optimal conditions. Veraine will be actively working against us. The seal is degrading faster than the model accounts for. And the resonance has to hold long enough for the seal to catch—we’re not just triggering it, we’re sustaining it. Sera thinks maybe thirty percent. I think she’s being optimistic.”

The words came out the way they’d formed in his head—unvarnished, unspun, the same flat precision he’d used for the rest of the briefing. He didn’t add a qualifier. He didn’t say but we can do this or I believe in us or any of the things a commander said to make the odds feel less like a gamble. The odds were the odds. Kael deserved to know them.

Kael’s face shifted.

It was a small thing—a change in the set of his jaw, the focus of his eyes. The careful blankness he’d been holding cracked along a seam Ben hadn’t seen until it opened. Underneath was something raw and unfamiliar. Not hope. Not yet. The thing before hope—the recognition that the person asking had just handed him the truth and trusted him to hold it.

Ben felt the moment land. Not in the Force; the pressure made everything smear at the edges, perception sliding off surfaces that should have been solid. He felt it in his body. The tightness in his chest. The stillness in his hands. The knowledge that he’d done everything he could do and the rest wasn’t his to control.

He didn’t fill the silence.

Kael looked away. Back toward the valley, the Scar, the light spreading across the far ridge. His fingers found another blade of grass and pulled—slow, deliberate, the motion of a man turning something over in his head that was too heavy to hold still.

The wrongness pressed down. Ben’s stomach lurched—the Scar pulsed again, a low throb that made the morning light look wrong for half a second, the colors bleeding toward violet before snapping back. He steadied his breathing. Kept his hands on his knees.

Thirty seconds passed. Maybe more. Kael’s Force presence stayed shuttered, but something in the set of his shoulders had changed—a loosening, or a settling. The difference between a man bracing for a blow and a man deciding the blow was worth taking.

When he spoke, his voice was dry. Level. The driest Ben had ever heard it.

“You’re the first person in a thousand years who’s told me the truth about what it would cost.”

He didn’t say yes. He didn’t say no. The words hung in the thin air between them—an acknowledgment, not an answer. Recognition that this ask was different because it was honest, and that the honesty was what separated it from every other institution that had ever demanded his service.

Ben let the silence hold. The Scar pulsed below them. The light kept spreading across the valley, beautiful and wrong, and Kael sat with the weight of a choice that was actually his to make.

## Scene 23.02

Kael stood.

He rose without hurry, deliberate as ever, as if every motion had already been judged and found worth the effort. He didn’t look at Ben. His attention stayed on the valley, on the Scar’s ugly pulse below the ridgeline, on sunlight spilling gold across the far peaks. The datapad still lay in the grass between them, its schematic faintly lit against the morning glare.

Then he started walking.

Not toward camp. Toward the ridge edge, where the ground broke away into the long slope that fell to the Scar. His stride stayed even, unforced. A man moving because he needed room to think, not because he needed to go somewhere.

Ben’s body moved before his mind did. He was halfway up when he caught himself—one hand planted on his knee, every muscle gone rigid, the urge to follow hammering through his nerves like a proximity alarm he couldn’t shut off.

*Don’t.*

The word hit hard and stayed there. Ben lowered himself back onto the grass. His hands returned to his knees, open and visible, the same posture he’d kept through the whole briefing. The posture that said I’m not leaving and I’m not forcing you to stay, both at once.

Kael didn’t glance back. The space between them widened—ten meters, fifteen, the grass whispering under Kael’s boots. Ben tracked him through the Force without meaning to, feeling that familiar knot of tight-held walls and efficient stillness around Kael’s presence. Still shut. Still guarded. But the closed-off feel of it had changed. Not defensive now. Private. A door held from the inside while the person behind it dealt with something that needed silence.

The Scar pulsed below. Ben’s stomach tightened as the valley’s ugliness pushed up through the morning, a vibration too low to hear and too deep to ignore. For a heartbeat the light looked wrong across the slope, then settled again. He kept breathing. Kept his hands on his knees. Not following took effort. It sat in his shoulders and jaw, a hard ache he couldn’t let go, because letting go might mean moving, and moving was the one thing he couldn’t afford.

Kael reached the ridge edge and stopped.

He stood with his back to Ben, a dark shape against the bright spread of the valley. The Scar lay below like something patient—an old break that had waited a thousand years and could wait a few minutes more. Its edges throbbed with that off note in the land, making the trees look off-color and the stream’s silver thread bend in ways it shouldn’t.

Kael looked at it. Ben watched him look.

Then Kael’s gaze shifted.

It was only a turn of the head, a change of focus. He was no longer staring at the Scar. He was looking down at the camp, where Desh Rolan was working on the amplification array.

Desh had his back to the ridge. He knelt beside the primary node housing with a spanner in one hand and a diagnostic pad in the other, checking connections with the patient care of a man who made sure things worked before anyone asked them to hold under pressure. He had no idea he was being watched. His presence—the warm blankness Ben had learned to recognize in the first weeks of the mission—remained steady and ordinary, the feel of a mind locked on the work in front of it and a life that depended on hands, tools, and stubborn follow-through.

Kael stood motionless at the ridge edge, looking down at him.

Ben felt the shift before he understood it.

It began as a loosening—a small easing in the tight boundaries around Kael’s Force presence that had held since the moment Ben first sensed him in the stasis chamber. Not a collapse. Not surrender. Just a release, like a breath finally let out after being held too long. The edges of Kael’s presence, hard and deliberately closed for weeks, softened a fraction. Opened a little. For the first time since Ben had found him, it wasn’t braced for a blow.

Ben’s own breath caught. He didn’t move. Hands on knees. Back straight. Face neutral—the kind of discipline that came from years of close-hold trust and operational silence, from learning that some moments couldn’t be fixed. Only watched.

*What are you seeing?*

The question rose and vanished without answer. Ben couldn’t read Kael’s thoughts—the Force didn’t work that way, and the Scar’s distortion would have made any reading a mess anyway. But he could see where Kael was looking. Desh, kneeling in the dirt with a spanner. Desh, who had no Force-sensitivity and no destiny and no institution telling him where to stand. Desh, who had signed on for a survey mission that had turned into something else and stayed anyway, not because anyone had ordered him or persuaded him or dangled a reward in front of him, but because he’d decided the work mattered.

The non-Force-user who chose to be here.

Ben got it then. Not through the Force—the Scar smeared that, blurred the edges of everything—but through the way Kael held himself still, the angle of his head, the fixed attention on Desh. Kael Drenn, conscripted by Sith and captured by Gavran and turned into living infrastructure for a seal he’d never agreed to hold, was looking at the one person in the group who had no reason to be here except that he’d chosen it.

The parallel landed without words. Ben felt it settle in his chest, not as revelation but as recognition. Desh had shown Kael something he’d never had in front of him before. Not power. Not conviction. Not destiny. Just purpose, chosen freely and carried without ceremony. The freest person in the group was the one who couldn’t feel the Force at all.

Kael stood at the ridge edge for a long moment. The Scar pulsed below. Light kept spreading across the valley, beautiful and wrong. Desh kept working, unaware, his spanner ticking against the node housing in the quiet morning air.

Then Kael turned.

His stride was different. Ben saw it at once—the change in the way he moved, the shift from careful to deliberate. Not rushed. Not resigned. The walk of a man who had finally set something down in his own mind. His boots found the same path through the grass he’d taken on the way out, but the weight behind them had changed. Less guarded. More certain.

He stopped a few meters from Ben. Close enough to talk. Close enough that Ben could see his face clearly for the first time since he’d walked away.

The careful blankness was gone. The dry detachment Kael wore like armor had split along a seam Ben hadn’t seen until now. Under it was something raw and unfamiliar—not hope yet, but the ground hope came from. The knowledge that the man in front of him had told him the truth and trusted him to carry it. The knowledge that he could choose, and that choosing was the one thing he’d never truly been allowed.

Ben didn’t name it. Didn’t ask. He met Kael’s eyes and waited, hands still on his knees, posture still open, the space between them unfilled.

Kael’s face had changed. Not the careful blankness. Not the dry detachment. Something new. Something that looked, against all odds, like choice.

## Scene 23.03

Kael walked back.

Ben saw the answer in his stride before Kael got close enough to speak. The careful way he had been placing each step since the stasis chamber—the habit of testing the ground before he trusted it—was gone. What had replaced it was something more deliberate, more decided. A man who had stopped measuring and started choosing. His boots hit the same path through the grass he’d used on the way out, but they landed with a different weight.

Ben’s chest tightened. His hands stayed on his knees, the open posture he’d held through the briefing, but his shoulders shifted forward, readying themselves. The Force around Kael felt less clenched than before, the walls around him easing from private to something nearer open air. Still guarded. Still Kael. But the coiled expectation that had clung to him since the stasis ring was gone.

Ben didn’t put words to it. His body had already taken the measure; his mind was only catching up. The waiting was over.

Kael stopped a few meters away, close enough to talk. Ben could see his face clearly now—the careful blankness was gone, and the dry detachment had cracked along a seam Ben hadn’t noticed until it split. Beneath it was something raw. Not hope. The ground hope grew out of.

Sera had risen from her seat by the array housing. Desh had stopped working, spanner still in one hand, diagnostic pad in the other. Neither of them spoke. Morning light lay flat across the grass, and the Scar’s ugly pulse pressed up through the valley floor below.

Kael looked at Ben and held his eyes for a long moment. Not measuring now. Just acknowledging. Then his gaze cut to the valley, to the distortion visible at its base, and back again.

“Someone should probably do something about that.”

The line came out drier than anything Ben had ever heard from him. Level. Unhurried. It landed like a door shutting on a decision already made. The humor was real—Kael’s deadpan timing still as sharp as a snapped tool—but it carried something enormous. The deflection didn’t hide the commitment. It brought it forward.

Ben caught both at once. The dry delivery, the choice underneath it. A man who had never volunteered to put himself in danger for someone else’s work was presenting his commitment as if he were making an offhand remark about a problem in the distance. The armor was still there. It just had a different purpose now—not to keep people out, but to make the vulnerability possible to bear.

Sera let out a breath. Not a laugh. More like air she’d been holding since Kael turned back. She didn’t speak. Her Force presence, blade-sharp and disciplined, shifted in Ben’s perception—not softening, exactly, but settling into a new angle. The way a fighter adjusted her stance when the unknown finally picked a side.

Desh looked at Kael for a beat, then at Ben. His face stayed flat in that practical, all-business way of his, but something moved behind it. He rolled the spanner once in his hand, then set it down beside the node housing with the quiet care of a man who knew a moment didn’t need help to stand on its own.

Kael didn’t elaborate. Didn’t explain. The line hung in the morning air, dry and understated, and then he was moving—not back toward camp, but down toward the valley slope, his stride still deliberate, still decided.

Ben rose. His knees cracked from the long stillness in the grass, the sound plain and grounding. He didn’t look at Sera or Desh. He didn’t need to. The others were already pivoting around him—Sera gathering her pack in efficient motions, Desh doing one final check on the array connections before splitting off toward the first amplification node in the distance.

They moved.

The valley opened below them as they descended, and the Scar’s distortion grew from a background pulse into something Ben could feel in his teeth. Light bent oddly along the tree line. The stream’s silver thread twisted in ways his eye kept trying to reject. But they held formation—Sera on Ben’s left, Kael on his right, Desh already a dark figure on the far ridgeline, working his way toward the node position at a steady pace that said he knew his job and didn’t need reminding.

Ben felt them through the Force. Sera’s presence was a honed edge, centuries of discipline compressed into one bright line—blade-sharp, precise, carrying the weight of everything she’d been forced to hold and refused to drop. Kael’s was different. Still efficient, still tight-knotted, but the knot had loosened into something that could flex. The walls were still there, but they weren’t prison walls anymore. They were the boundaries of a self that had finally chosen its own shape.

And Ben’s own presence—tired, warm, the Skywalker bloodline running through it like a current he’d stopped trying to separate from—sat between them. Three traditions. Three ways of touching the Force that had never been asked to work together before.

Sera’s bright clarity, Kael’s grounded efficiency, Ben’s tired warmth—each distinct, each suited to the others. But they didn’t yet blend. They sat beside one another, three separate tones that ought to have made something larger and instead just occupied the same space. Ben could feel the gaps between them, the places where the harmony should have caught and didn’t.

The Scar pulsed below. The disturbance in the Force pressed up through the valley floor like a note played slightly flat, a vibration too low to hear and too deep to ignore. Ben’s stomach tightened. The strain of that incompleteness sat in his shoulders and jaw—not doubt, but the knowledge that what they were walking toward had never been tried. Three Force-users from three different centuries, carrying three different relationships to the thing they were about to hold together. The discordance was already waiting for them.

Desh’s figure reached the first node position on the far ridgeline and stopped. Ben couldn’t feel him through the Force—the warm blankness of his presence was too far now, swallowed by the Scar’s distortion—but he could see him. A small dark shape against the bright spread of the valley, a non-Force-user walking straight into the worst of it because he’d decided the work mattered.

The group kept moving. The slope leveled out ahead, the valley floor opening toward the source. The clock was down to minutes.

Ben walked between Sera and Kael, and the valley rose to meet them.
