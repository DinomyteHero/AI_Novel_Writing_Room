The ridgeline was bare rock and wind, a place that had never been meant for people. Below it, the valley held the new seal in a bowl of stone and scrub grass, and the seal hummed—a three-part chord Ben felt in his chest before his ears caught up. Sera’s lock-discipline, Kael’s monk-brevity, and something that was his own, braided together into a presence still settling into itself.

Torin sat at the ridge’s edge with his hands resting on his knees, facing the valley.

His posture was the same as it had been on the Steady Returns, in the common area, in the moments before a mission brief when he’d been the one who made everyone feel like a team. Shoulders back. Spine straight. The easy readiness of a Battle Master who never had to perform command because command was simply where he lived.

Everything else had changed.

Ben stopped twenty meters downslope and did not close the distance. The wind pushed at his back, cold and dry, carrying the mineral tang of exposed stone. He could see the angle of Torin’s shoulders from here, the way his fingers rested on his knees, slightly curled—not tense, not loose, just there, the hands of a man who had finished something and was sitting in what came after.

His Force-presence was lighter. That was the word Ben’s mind kept reaching for, and it was the wrong word, but it was the only one that fit. The heavy warmth that had radiated from Torin since the moment he’d woken in the stasis ring—the warmth that had made Ben trust him before he had any reason to, the warmth that had made the group feel like a team—was gone. Not dimmed. Not faded. Gone, replaced by something quieter, something that should have felt like relief after the old weight and instead felt like a room after the fire had died.

Ben’s mouth opened before he knew he meant to speak.

Master Hal, he almost said, because there had been a dozen briefings where Torin had answered that tone with a crooked look and some dry line that took the edge off whatever ugly thing came next. Ben stood there with the first shape of the words behind his teeth and felt, physically, the place where the answer should have landed.

Nothing came.

He shut his mouth. The cold bit the back of his throat.

He’s still in there, Ben thought. The thought arrived without permission, sharp and immediate, the way his mind reached when it wanted a problem it could solve. He’s still in there, and there’s nothing to fix.

The wind pushed again. The seal hummed below. Torin didn’t move.

Ben’s hands wanted to do something. Old instinct, older than the mission, older than the GAG, older than anything except the first time he’d watched someone he cared about slide toward a place he couldn’t follow. Intervene. Prevent. Find the right words, the right action, the right application of will that would put back what had been lost. His mother had been like that. His father was like that in a different way, the way that held still and waited, and Ben had never been able to decide which one was harder.

Neither would help here. Torin had made his choice in the valley, and the choice had taken something from him that Ben couldn’t return. The warmth. The ease. The laugh he’d had—self-deprecating and genuine, the laugh of a man who’d carried terrible things and still found the world worth smiling at. All of it gone into the seal, poured out like water into dry ground, and what was left was this: a man sitting on a ridgeline with his hands on his knees, present and diminished, alive and not whole.

Ben made himself breathe. The air was thin up here, cold enough to sting, and it helped. He let his attention settle on the small details—the way Torin’s tunic moved in the wind, the stillness of his hands, the angle of his head as he faced the valley. The absence of the warmth that had once radiated from him like heat from a banked fire.

He didn’t walk closer. There was nothing to say, and saying nothing was the only thing that wouldn’t be a lie.

Movement below caught his eye—a figure climbing the slope from the recovery shelters, field kit slung across one shoulder and a debrief slate tucked under her arm. Shelter lights glowed faint and practical behind her in the late afternoon, a recovery site and nothing else. Not an intake line. Not a Temple claim staked in canvas and good intentions. Just med tents, inventory crates, overworked heaters, and people being allowed to decide what happened to them next.

Sera walked with the same economy she’d always had, the same blade-like precision in every step, but something in her pace had shifted. She wasn’t hurrying. She wasn’t bracing. She was walking toward the ridgeline the way someone walked toward a place they’d already decided to be.

She set the field kit down at the base of the slope. The debrief slate went beside it, placed carefully on a flat stone where the wind wouldn’t take it. Neither motion was rushed. Neither motion was reluctant. She was putting them down because she was done with them for now, and she was done because she had chosen to be done.

That, more than any formal statement she could have made, fixed the practical shape of it in Ben’s mind. Temporary recovery here. Debriefing on her terms. No one bundling either of them into a transport because they were useful. No one claiming Torin as a recovered asset or Sera as a convenient Jedi problem to absorb. She had stayed, and she had stayed as herself.

Ben watched her climb. Her face held grief and peace without choosing between them—the same expression she’d worn when she’d told him about Gavran’s betrayal, the same stillness she’d carried through every argument and every fight and every moment when the weight of a thousand years had pressed down on her shoulders. She wasn’t setting the weight down. She was carrying it somewhere else.

She reached the ridgeline and sat beside Torin.

She didn’t speak. She didn’t reach for his hand. She didn’t turn to face him or shift her posture to signal comfort or do any of the things Ben’s instincts were screaming at him to do. She just sat, her shoulder a handspan from his, her eyes on the valley where the new seal hummed its three-part chord into the afternoon light.

The wind wrapped around them both and kept going.

Ben felt the shift in the air between them—not a lightening, but a settling. The pressure didn’t ease; it changed, the way a wound stopped bleeding and started to close. Not healed. Not restored. But no longer open.

Sera’s face was the same as it had been on the slope: grief and peace, held together without choosing. She wasn’t trying to fix him. She wasn’t offering comfort as a solution. She was sitting beside him because she had decided to be there, and the decision was the whole of the argument.

This is what staying looks like, Ben thought.

He stayed where he was, twenty meters downslope, the wind at his back and the seal’s hum in his chest. He didn’t walk closer. He didn’t turn away. He watched, because watching was the only thing he could do that wasn’t a violation, and because someone should see this—should register that Sera Varik had chosen presence over control, had chosen to sit beside a man who was partly gone and partly still there, had chosen a future smaller than the one she’d deserved and called it enough.

The seal hummed below. The wind pushed past. The light began its slow slide toward evening, gold going thin at the edges, and still neither of them moved.

After a long time—long enough that the seal’s three-part harmony became the only sound, long enough that Ben lost track of the minutes and let them go—Torin’s hand moved.

It was a small motion. His fingers uncurled from his knee, lifted, crossed the handspan of space between his body and Sera’s. The movement was slow, deliberate, the movement of a man learning the shape of his own body again and finding it lighter than he remembered.

His hand found hers.

The grip was lighter than it had been before. In the stasis ring, in the common area, in the moments when Torin had reached for Sera’s hand as if it were the only anchor he had left, the grip had been strong—desperate in its quiet way, the grip of a man holding on because he was afraid of what would happen if he let go.

This was different. His fingers closed around hers with a pressure that was barely there, the ghost of a grip, the memory of strength.

Sera’s hand turned under his at once, not to steady him, not to test him, only to meet him where he was. Her shoulders didn’t shake. Her face didn’t break. But Ben saw the small hitch low in her throat, saw her mouth press once like she had taken a hit she’d been braced for and still hadn’t softened.

It was there. Lighter than before, diminished in the way everything about Torin was diminished now, but present. Chosen.

Ben looked away. Not because it didn’t hurt—it did—but that wasn’t why. He looked away because he’d seen what he needed to see, and the rest was theirs.

The wind pushed at his back as he turned downslope. The seal hummed below. Behind him, on the ridgeline, Sera sat beside Torin. She didn’t speak. He didn’t either. After a long time, his hand found hers. The grip was lighter than it had been. But it was there.
