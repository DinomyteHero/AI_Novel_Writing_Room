The galley was too small for five people and an argument, but they’d crammed both in anyway.

Sera had been eating in silence since they’d cleared the thin place, her fork moving with the same disciplined economy she brought to everything. Ben had watched her work through half a ration bar before Torin spoke, and the moment he did, her fork stopped.

“The technique isn’t sacred or profane,” Torin said, reaching for the water bulb beside his plate. His voice stayed easy, conversational — the same tone he’d used telling stories about Gavran’s fortress two days ago. “It’s engineering. You have a structure under strain, you reinforce it. You have a wound that won’t close, you stitch it. The stitching isn’t the wound.”

Sera set her fork down.

Not dropped. Not placed. Set down with a precision that made Ben’s hand pause halfway to his own plate. The tines lined up with the edge of her tray, the handle straight, the whole gesture a hard stop at the end of a sentence she hadn’t spoken yet.

“It’s a lobotomy,” she said.

Torin spread his hands on the table. Open. Reasonable. The body language of someone who expected to be heard because he was being reasonable. “It’s a technique that deadens Force-sensitivity in a local area. Whether that’s a lobotomy or a tourniquet depends on what you’re trying to stop.”

“You’re describing the Weave as though it were a machine.”

“I’m describing it as something that can be damaged and repaired. The rest is vocabulary.”

Ben’s instinct shoved him toward the center of the table — intervene, cut in, do something — but his body stayed locked. The fork still sat there, Sera’s fingers resting beside it, and the space between her and Torin had gone quiet enough to feel dangerous.

“The rest is not vocabulary,” Sera said. Her diction had sharpened, every consonant landing cleanly, like someone used to giving orders before the invention of modern comlinks. “The Weave is the work of the Force. You’re talking about it like it’s a construction project.”

“I’m talking about it like something twelve people were sealed into chambers to maintain.” Torin’s warmth was still there, but thinner now — the humor gone, the ease stripped down to something deliberate. “If the work is sacred, what does that make the anchors?”

Kael hadn’t looked up from his plate through any of it. He ate the way he did everything else — efficiently, economically, giving nothing away. But Ben caught the stillness in his shoulders, the tight attention that meant he was tracking every word.

Desh stood in the galley doorway, arms crossed, watching the table with the flat, assessing look of someone who’d spent years reading rooms where the wrong word could get people killed. He caught Ben’s eye for half a second. Said nothing. The look was enough.

Sera’s hand moved toward her fork again, not to lift it — just to touch the handle, as if she were anchoring herself. “The anchors were a crime. You’re proposing to repeat it.”

“I’m proposing to understand the tool well enough to decide whether it can be used without repeating the crime.” Torin’s hands stayed spread on the table, his voice still calm, still measured, still making his position sound like the only adult one in the room. “The stitching exists. The wound exists. Refusing to look at how the first was applied to the second doesn’t make either of them go away.”

Ben felt the argument cinch tighter around the table. Sera’s fork. Torin’s hands. Two positions that couldn’t share the same air, and he was sitting between them with nothing to offer but the urge to table the whole thing and keep moving.

Sera turned toward Kael.

“And you?” Her voice hadn’t softened. “You’ve been silent since the fortress. What does the Brotherhood’s conscript think of the stitching?”

Kael’s fork paused. Not the deliberate stop of Sera’s gesture — just a beat, the smallest hesitation of someone deciding whether to engage.

Torin’s hands moved before Kael could answer.

Not dramatically. A slight shift, palms rotating outward, the motion pulling attention back to himself without seeming to. “Kael wasn’t in the chamber with us when Gavran made the decision. Neither were you, Sera. You were already sealed by then.”

The redirect was seamless. Sera’s question had been aimed at Kael; Torin’s answer landed as though she’d been speaking to him all along. His voice hadn’t changed. His posture hadn’t changed. He’d simply taken the heat and kept talking, and by the time he finished, the moment where Sera had been about to press Kael was gone.

Kael resumed eating. His expression didn’t shift.

Ben saw it. The way Torin’s hands had moved, the way his voice had lifted Sera’s question and turned it aside, the way Kael had been left untouched without ever being asked whether he wanted the help. Ben saw it, and his mind filed it without a label — a beat that meant something, a shape in the room he couldn’t name, a kindness Torin had given at no cost to himself and no visible reason.

He didn’t interpret it. He wasn’t sure he could.

“The problem,” Sera said, and now her voice had gone cold in a way that cut deeper than anger, “is that you think the stitching is separate from what it does. You think you can use the technique without becoming what Gavran became. That’s not engineering. That’s self-deception.”

Torin kept his hands on the table. The warmth had thinned further — still there, still recognizable, but stretched across something harder underneath. “And your position is that the Weave must be preserved exactly as it is, even where people have already used it as a weapon. That’s not reverence. That’s refusing to triage because you don’t like the look of the wound.”

“It’s refusing to make the wound worse.”

“It’s refusing to admit the wound exists in the way it exists, rather than the way you wish it did.”

The galley hummed around them. Recycled air. The faint vibration of the hyperdrive through the deck plates. Desh hadn’t moved from the doorway, but his attention had sharpened — not just the flat assessment from before, but something more focused, like he was cataloguing positions for a report he’d never file.

Ben’s plate had gone cold. He hadn’t noticed when.

Kael set down his fork.

The sound was small — smaller than Sera’s had been, smaller than the argument deserved — but it cut through the hum of the ship like a blade through static. He didn’t look at Sera. Didn’t look at Torin. His eyes stayed on his plate for a moment longer, and then he spoke.

“You’re all describing the same cage.”

His voice was flat. Not angry, not tired, not anything anyone could use against him. Just the same dry, cutting register he’d had since the fortress.

“Sera calls the cloth sacred and says anyone who touches it is a vandal. Torin calls it stitching and says anyone who won’t touch it is a coward. The Brotherhood called it a weapon and said anyone who wouldn’t wield it was prey.” He lifted his water bulb, took a sip, set it down. “Three different ways of decorating the bars. The cage doesn’t care what you call it.”

The silence that followed was different from the silence before.

Before, the quiet had been pressure — an argument waiting to start again, positions tensed against each other like drawn bowstrings. Now the quiet was empty. The argument had run into a wall none of them had seen coming, and on the other side of it there was nothing to say.

Sera’s hand had gone still beside her fork. Torin’s hands were still spread on the table, but the reasonableness had drained out of the gesture, leaving only the shape of it. Desh’s eyes moved from Kael to Ben and stayed there.

Ben felt the weight of the silence settle on his shoulders. Four positions in the room — Sera’s, Torin’s, Kael’s, and his own absence — and Kael had just named them all as the same thing. Including the one Ben hadn’t spoken.

He didn’t have an answer. He was the one person at the table who’d never had a settled position, and now the room had turned to look at him and found nothing there.

Nobody spoke.

The ship hummed. The plates cooled. Desh shifted his weight in the doorway, the first movement anyone had made since Kael stopped talking.

Nobody answered.

Because nobody had a good response.
