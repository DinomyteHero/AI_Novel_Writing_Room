The ramp sealed with a hard, final thunk, like a hatch that had been holding its breath and finally let go.

Ben climbed to the cockpit deck behind the others, his boots leaving pale dust prints across the plating. The Steady Returns smelled of recycled air, old caf, and the faint machine grease that always clung to Desh’s engineering crawlspace. Familiar. Still off.

Nobody sat where they usually sat.

Sera stood by the nav station with her back to the bulkhead, arms crossed, posture so tight it looked carved instead of held. Torin had dropped into the jump seat beside the engineering console—Desh’s seat, unless Desh was flying—and leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, warmth still on his face but worked down to something Ben had to squint to read. Kael took the corner near the galley hatch, hands open on his thighs, his expression the same closed door it had been since Veraine turned away from him.

Desh was already at the pilot’s station, running passive departure checks in a silence that said he was thinking hard enough to forget conversation existed.

Ben stopped at the threshold. His body wanted the familiar rhythm of leaving—strap in, run the checklist, feel the deck thrum as the drives came up and the ship pulled free of whatever world they’d just survived. The room refused to cooperate.

“Everyone in one piece?” Desh asked, not looking up from the sensor board.

No one answered. The silence stretched until Ben felt it under his ribs.

“I’ll take that as a yes,” Desh said, and went back to work.

Ben moved to the copilot’s station and sat. The seat still held Desh’s heat, which told him Desh had been on his feet since before they boarded. That wasn’t normal. Desh flew from the chair unless something was bad enough to make him stand.

Ben didn’t ask. He watched the passive track instead—Veraine’s ship a thinning blip at the edge of the display, its vector clean and steady, a departure that looked like nothing but departure.

The catalogue was already in motion.

Vess at the ramp, checking the sky before she climbed. Not nerves. Combat reflex; the sort you didn’t shake off. Pira’s crate, set where it could give cover if the meeting broke apart. Serran’s archive cylinder, tucked against his chest like it mattered more than his own skin. Mira, moving only when Veraine’s silence allowed it, her stillness the same fear-learned economy Ben had seen in Kael’s first days out of stasis.

None of it was proof. All of it stayed.

“The meeting did not change her,” Sera said.

Her voice came out flat and controlled, the old syntax giving each word the weight of a blade laid on stone. She hadn’t moved from the bulkhead.

Torin looked up. “No. It didn’t.”

He said it gently, the way he said most things, but the warmth took effort now, something he had to reach for instead of breathe.

“But we know her shape now,” he went on. “We know how she argues. What she reaches for. What she doesn’t.” He paused. “That matters, Sera.”

“Does it?” Sera’s gaze stayed on the forward viewport. “She asked questions she already knew the answers to. She counted the dead in front of me and called the arithmetic acceptable. She listened to testimony and sharpened her next question instead of flinching.” Her jaw tightened. “Knowing her shape does not make her smaller.”

“Knowing the shape of what you’re fighting is the first thing they teach you in—” Torin cut himself off, and Ben watched him choose another road. “In any war.”

Sera said nothing.

Ben angled his head just enough to catch Kael in his peripheral vision. Kael still hadn’t spoken since they boarded. His hands were still spread on his thighs, his breathing even, his face carrying the same disciplined blankness he’d worn since Veraine’s question hit and he chose not to answer.

The silence was no longer empty enough for Ben to mistake it for indifference.

“Kael,” Ben said.

Kael’s gaze shifted to him. Slow. Deliberate. Like someone surfacing from a depth he hadn’t meant to share.

“I don’t have a summary for you,” Kael said. His voice was quiet, dry, the monk’s brevity intact. “She asked me a question. I’m still holding it.”

Ben nodded. He didn’t push. The catalogue had room for one more unresolved line.

Desh straightened from the sensor board. It was a small movement, but Ben knew him well enough to read the shift in his shoulders—the moment a tactical problem stopped being background noise and became the thing in front of him.

“I’m running back-sweeps across the meeting window,” Desh said.

Ben turned. “Why?”

“Because the Ash-Tithe Compact moved like people with a job outside the conversation.”

Desh’s voice stayed even, the flat report he used when he didn’t like what he’d found and wasn’t ready to give it a name. “Vess checked the sky before the ramp. Pira put that crate where it could give cover. Serran stayed within arm’s reach of the cylinder the whole time. Mira didn’t move until Veraine’s silence told her to.” He met Ben’s eyes. “That’s not a crew that came to stand behind somebody. That’s a crew that came to do something and got it done while we were listening to the speech.”

Ben felt the words settle in him like cold water finding a crack.

The meeting may have been cover.

The thought wasn’t proven. There was no proof. The passive sensors showed nothing but a clean departure vector and a ship slipping away into distance that looked exactly like retreat.

But Veraine had used honest words in that clearing. Precise ones. Complete ones. Already certain of the answers she wasn’t asking for. Ben had felt the weight of her sincerity and filed it away as a threat in its own right—a woman so settled in her convictions that nothing Sera said could move her.

Sincere and ruthless. Both could be true. The proof of the ruthlessness wasn’t in her arguments. It was in whatever her people had done while she was making them.

“Run the sweeps,” Ben said.

Desh was already on it. His hands moved across the sensor board with the quick, practiced economy of someone who’d spent years finding what other people wanted hidden. “Passive only. I’m not pinging anything until I know what I’m looking for. Could be nothing.”

“It’s not nothing,” Ben said.

Desh didn’t answer. He didn’t need to.

Ben turned back to the forward viewport. Veraine’s ship was only a fading point on the passive track now, small enough to lose against the starfield if you weren’t watching for it. He watched until it vanished.

The meeting was not finished with them.

He carried the suspicion like a bruise he couldn’t explain—there, tender, waiting for something to press against it hard enough to prove it real. The back-sweeps would take hours. If there was proof, it would turn up in the next system or the one after that, or somewhere between here and the seal.

Ben sat in the copilot’s chair and didn’t strap in. Behind him, the group held their separate silences. Ahead of him, the passive track showed empty space where Veraine’s ship had been.

Desh started the back-sweeps.
