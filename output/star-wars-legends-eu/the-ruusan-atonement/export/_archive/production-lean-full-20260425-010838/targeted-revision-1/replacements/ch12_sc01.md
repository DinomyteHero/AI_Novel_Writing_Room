Sera pressed her palms against the stone and held them there.

The seal notation glimmered faintly under her fingertips—Gavran’s personal mark, the same characters that had resolved on her display aboard the Steady Returns. Up close, the light was cooler than Ben had expected. Not amber, not the warm pulse of a live system, but something steadier. Something patient.

The others waited in a loose semicircle behind her. No one spoke. High-altitude air made the space feel narrower than it was, all thin cold and mineral bite, with a faint metallic tang beneath it from ancient alloy. Beyond the plateau, the forest fell away into haze—scrub pines clinging to rock, their wind-twisted shapes the only living things Ben had seen since they landed.

Desh lingered at the rear, arms folded, his headache showing in the way he held his shoulders. Kael had taken up a position angled to the door, the way he always did: exits, sightlines, angles. Torin stood with his hands loose at his sides, patient in a way that looked almost drowsy. Ben knew better now.

He kept his breathing even. His instinct wanted action—check the perimeter, test the seal, do something with his hands besides stand here while Sera worked a lock that had held for a millennium. But Sera hadn’t asked for help. She’d gone to the door, set her palms against the stone, and gone still. The discipline in her posture told him interruption would cost more than patience.

So he waited.

The minutes dragged. Five. Seven. The cold seeped through his boots. Desh shifted his weight once, leather giving a faint creak under the movement. Kael didn’t move at all.

At nine minutes Ben felt the first give—not in the stone, but in the Force. A subtle loosening, like a knot easing a fraction under steady pressure. Sera’s presence had gone almost unnaturally still, the edge of her discipline drawn down to a point. She wasn’t forcing the lock. She was listening to it.

At eleven minutes her shoulders tightened.

At twelve, the seal yielded.

The sound was quieter than Ben expected—a low grind of stone on stone, then the hiss of pressure equalizing after a thousand years. Air spilled out carrying dust and the stale weight of a sealed place. Not the cold of a tomb. Something drier. Something that had been waiting.

Sera lowered her hands. She didn’t turn around.

“It’s open,” she said.

Her voice stayed level. Ben had learned how much effort that sometimes cost her.

The entrance led into a corridor cut from the mountain’s own stone—geometric, precise, the same cold discipline they’d seen from the air. The walls were bare. No carvings. No inscriptions. Just clean lines and the faint glow of ancient lighting strips that had woken with the seal and now washed everything in pale, even light.

Ben stepped through first. Dust motes turned in the air as the interior settled. His boots left prints in the fine grey powder covering the floor. No one had walked here since the door sealed.

The corridor opened onto the training ground without warning—one step through an arched doorway, and the space spread out around him. A broad circle of fitted stone under open sky, ringed by low structures whose purpose he could read from their proportions. Barracks. A small forge. What might have been a meditation chamber, its entrance facing east toward the sunrise. The compound was austere, but it didn’t feel military. No defensive walls. No kill-box design. Whoever built it had not been expecting attack.

Ben walked to the edge of the training circle and stopped.

The Weave flexed.

He’d expected the wrongness he knew. This was neither that nor anything he had words for. The Force here was present, fully present, light and dark arriving together without cancelling each other out. The boundary shimmered. Not a seam. Not a wound. A surface that seemed to tilt, like heat over stone, like the instant before a choice became a decision.

Something in the circle answered him.

He stepped into it.

His chest loosened. The reaction came through his body first—a release he hadn’t known he was holding, a settling in his shoulders and spine as if his body had finally found ground it recognized. Light and dark didn’t blend. They didn’t resolve. They simply occupied the same space, and the line between them shimmered with the suggestion that he could stop bracing against both.

His breath caught.

For one breath, it was beautiful.

The beauty landed before the warning did. His weight shifted. His feet wanted to root deeper into the stone, and for that single instant he understood, without wanting to, why anyone would come back here. The circle still held the resonance of practice, of repetition, of people stepping into this feeling and calling it revelation.

Then he heard footsteps.

Torin walked past him without a word. His pace was unhurried, his hands still loose at his sides, and he moved to the center of the training ground with the ease of someone crossing a familiar threshold. He stopped. Stood perfectly still.

His Force presence settled.

Ben had felt Torin’s presence before—the warm, jagged hum of it, the storyteller’s ease, the weight of a man who carried more than he said. Here, that weight lifted. Not gone. Lifted, the way a burden lifts when you finally set it down. His presence spread into the flexible Weave like water finding level. Calm. Complete. At home in a way Ben had never felt from him anywhere else on the journey.

The pleasure in Ben’s own chest cooled.

He stepped back from the circle. The shimmer receded—not vanishing, but pulling away, becoming something he observed instead of something he stood inside. The beauty didn’t disappear. It became a memory. Something that had happened to him, not something still happening.

Torin didn’t move. His eyes were open, his breathing slow, his face carrying an expression Ben couldn’t quite read. Not peace. Something close to it. Something that had stopped fighting.

Kael’s voice came from the edge of the training ground, quiet and dry.

“That’s not reassuring.”

Ben turned. Kael stood near the corridor entrance, arms folded, his gaze fixed on Torin with the flat patience Ben had come to recognize as his version of high alert. Desh had stopped a few paces back, one hand braced against the wall, the headache written in the tightness around his eyes.

Sera hadn’t stepped into the training ground at all. She stood in the archway, posture rigid, face unreadable. She was looking not at Torin now, but at the circle itself.

“Maetha warned about this place,” she said.

The words cut cleanly through the quiet.

Ben looked at her.

Sera’s jaw tightened once, as if against the shape of something she wasn’t ready to say out loud. “Not the record. Just a note in Gavran’s sealwork.” Her eyes flicked to Torin, then back to the stone. “She marked the central ground as dangerous.”

Dangerous. Ben looked at the circle again and felt the ghost of that loosening in his chest answer.

Torin opened his eyes fully and looked up at the sky—the high, thin blue of altitude, the wind stirring the pines beyond the compound’s edge—then turned and walked back toward the corridor without a word. His presence stayed settled. That was the worst part.

Ben followed.

The deeper chambers were smaller, more private. Workspaces rather than training halls. The lighting strips woke in sequence as they moved, illuminating narrow passages and doorways cut with the same geometric precision. Everything was clean. Everything was empty. Dust lay undisturbed on every surface, and the silence had the weight of a place sealed before it was abandoned.

Halfway down the second passage, Desh stopped.

“Hold up.”

Everyone checked instinctively. Ben’s hand had already gone toward his lightsaber before he saw what Desh was looking at: not movement, not a threat, just a scatter of something pale against the dark seam where wall met floor.

Desh crouched with a wince and touched it with two fingers. Dust came away, but something else stayed fixed in place.

“Not stone,” he muttered.

Ben moved closer. Set into the seam was a narrow strip of translucent material, no wider than a finger joint, fused into the fitted rock as if it had been worked there on purpose. Under the dust, faint lines ran through it in a repeating geometric pattern. Hairline. Precise.

Sera knelt beside it. Her expression changed the instant she saw the pattern.

“That’s hers too,” she said.

Ben looked from the strip to her face. “A marker?”

“No.” Sera brushed more dust away with the side of her hand. The pale material caught the corridor light and, for an instant, threw back a soft patient sheen like the outer seal. “A replacement piece.”

The word sat wrong in the air.

Kael glanced down the corridor. “For what?”

Sera was already on her feet, moving toward the final door at the end of the passage. Her gaze fixed on the seal cut into the stone there—cooler, steadier, unmistakably personal.

“The archive chamber,” she said. “Maetha sealed her own records.”

Ben looked once more at the pale strip in the floor seam before he followed. It was shaped too neatly, fitted too cleanly into old stone, as if something had been removed and this had been made to take its place.
