Torin lifted the fragment without ceremony.

One hand closed around the dark stone and raised it from the case Sera had opened on the table. Ben watched the motion, and the wrongness of it hit him before the thought did. Everyone else had flinched. Ben had, too—his hand jerking back from the stone’s stripped-bare emptiness, the Force under his fingertips gone directionless and cold. Sera had gone rigid when she’d first handled the fragment in the archive. Even Kael, who touched nothing he didn’t have to, had pulled back a fraction when the case opened.

Torin didn’t. His fingers closed around the stone like it was a tool he’d been waiting to use.

His Force presence shifted. Ben had grown used to the warm, jagged hum of Torin’s signature—the thing that read as confidence, as competence, as the easy authority of someone who’d been a Battle Master before Ben’s Order existed. Now the warmth narrowed. The jagged edge smoothed out. What remained was lighter and more dangerous: a focused attention that made the air around the table feel thin.

“The records are intact,” Torin said. His voice stayed warm, but it was the warmth of a man reading something he already understood. “Ankhural was documenting what he found at Veranthos. Not theorizing. Recording.”

Sera stood at the opposite side of the table, arms crossed, jaw tight. She’d been the one to carry the Codex case from the fortress. She’d kept it sealed through the thin place, through the debate in the galley, through every argument that had circled the thing inside without naming it. Now she watched Torin hold the fragment, and her stillness told Ben exactly what it meant.

“Read it,” she said. “Don’t interpret it.”

Torin looked up from the stone. “Those are the same thing.”

“Not with this text.”

The exchange hung there. Kael had folded into the corner couch again, his back to the bulkhead, his face giving away nothing. Ben felt him there as a quiet presence—not withdrawn, exactly, but held apart, the way Kael always seemed to hold himself apart while everyone else committed to positions he hadn’t chosen.

Torin turned the fragment over in his hands. The characters cut into its surface were dense and precise, deep enough to hold shadow, and Ben watched Torin’s eyes track across them with the ease of someone reading a language he’d known longer than the people in the room had been alive.

“The wound at Veranthos,” Torin said, “isn’t a scar. It’s a void. The technique didn’t just damage the Force at that site. It took the local structure away entirely. What Ankhural found was a region where the weave had been pulled apart thread by thread and left that way—no reknitting, no healing, just an open gap where the Force should have been.”

Ben felt the words hit his stomach before they reached his head. A void. Not damage that could heal. A hole.

“Gavran’s solution,” Torin continued, “was containment. Twelve Force-sensitives placed around the wound’s perimeter, their presence used as structural reinforcement—living anchors holding the gap closed against the pressure of the Force trying to reassert itself around the void. The seal wasn’t a cage. It was a bridge. Twelve people holding a wound shut that would otherwise have torn the entire sector apart.”

The vertigo hit then. Not the disorientation of the thin place—something sharper, more personal. Ben felt the mechanism settle into his understanding the way a physical weight settled onto his shoulders. Twelve people. Not volunteers. Prisoners. Sera and Torin and Kael and nine others who’d died in their chambers over the centuries, their Force presence drained year by year as the seal consumed what it needed to stay closed.

His throat tightened. The recycled air of the Steady Returns’ common area, which he’d stopped noticing days ago, suddenly tasted thin and metallic. The hyperdrive’s hum, steady under the deck plates, seemed to recede, pushed back behind the pressure building in his chest.

“The technique,” Torin said, and his voice had gone quieter now, almost reverent, “Ankhural understood what Gavran refused to. The void wasn’t damage. It was revelation. The local structure around Veranthos—the weave everyone assumed was natural—was imposed. Built into the site by the practitioners who first shaped it. The technique didn’t create a wound. It removed an artificial architecture and exposed what the Force actually is without it.”

Sera’s voice cut through the common area. “That’s heresy.”

“It’s evidence.” Torin set the fragment down on the table, his fingers lingering on its surface. “Ankhural documented it. The void at Veranthos isn’t a scar. It’s a window. What Gavran called damage was simply the Force returning to its natural state—unshaped, undirected, free of the structure the Jedi Lords imposed and called sacred. The technique wasn’t a war crime. It was a correction. And the anchor system—” He looked at Sera, and his warmth was genuine, and something under it had gone hungry. “—the anchor system wasn’t a prison. It was a structural solution to a structural problem. Twelve people holding a bridge closed because no one had the courage to ask whether the bridge should exist at all.”

The logic was tight. Ben felt it close around the evidence like a well-machined seal. If Ankhural was right about the imposed structure, then the technique wasn’t violation—it was restoration. If the void was the Force’s natural state, then Gavran’s containment wasn’t mercy—it was refusal. If the anchor system was a bridge, then Veraine’s attempt to break it wasn’t destruction—it was liberation.

The argument was beautiful. It fit the evidence perfectly. And Ben felt his head begin to nod.

The motion was small—a fractional dip of his chin, almost unconscious. His body agreeing before his mind had finished weighing the cost. Part of him wanted Torin to be right. Wanted the technique to be correction rather than atrocity, because if it was correction then the twelve anchors weren’t victims of a crime that had been repeated in Ben’s name, and the seal wasn’t a cage, and the weight in his chest could lift.

Sera stepped forward.

She didn’t speak. She placed her hand flat on the table between Torin and the fragment—a deliberate motion, quiet and final. Her palm pressed against the worn surface like she was closing a door. Her Force presence, which Ben had come to recognize as blade-like discipline held in constant tension, went very still.

Torin looked at her hand. Then at her face. “Sera—”

“No.” The word was quiet. Not a refusal. A boundary.

Ben caught himself. The nod stopped mid-motion, his chin still angled toward agreement, and the awareness of what he’d been doing hit him like a second shock. He’d been nodding. He’d been agreeing. The argument had been coherent and elegant and perfectly wrong, and he’d been nodding along while Torin built a bridge from evidence to catastrophe without once checking whether it led somewhere Ben was willing to go.

His hand, resting on the table, curled into a fist.

Sera’s eyes met his. She’d seen it. The nod. The catch. The fist. Her expression didn’t change—her jaw stayed tight, her posture stayed rigid—but something passed between them in that glance. Recognition. She pulled her hand back from the table and turned toward the corridor.

“Ben,” she said. “A word.”

It wasn’t a request.

He followed her into the corridor. The common area door slid shut behind them, cutting off the hum of the ship and the silence Torin had left in his wake. The corridor was narrow—durasteel walls, dim running lights, recycled air cooler here than in the common area. Sera stopped with her back to the door and turned to face him.

Her voice, when she spoke, was very quiet.

“I have heard that argument before.”

Ben didn’t ask when. He didn’t need to. The shape of it was already clear—the coherence, the elegance, the way it took evidence that should have been a warning and turned it into justification. The way it made atrocity sound like courage. The way it asked you to nod along before you noticed you were agreeing.

“From a man I followed into a war,” Sera said. “He made the same case. The same logic. The same warmth.” Her jaw tightened. “He was wrong. And I did not see it until the bodies were already counted.”

She didn’t say the name. She didn’t need to. Ben heard it anyway—in the space between her words, in the stillness of her posture, in the way she’d placed her hand on the table like a door closing on something she’d opened once before and couldn’t close again.

The pattern was there. Not just Torin’s logic. The shape of how that logic had moved through history, how it had justified catastrophe before, how it was doing it again right now in the common area behind them while Torin held the fragment and waited for someone to agree with him.

Ben met her eyes. “I was nodding.”

“I saw.”

“I didn’t—” He stopped. The sentence he’d been about to say—*I didn’t mean to*—wasn’t true. He’d meant to. The logic had been that good. “I caught it.”

“Yes,” Sera said. “You did.”

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she turned and opened the door, and the hum of the ship rushed back in, and Ben followed her into the common area carrying a weight that had nothing to do with the fragment and everything to do with the name she hadn’t said.
