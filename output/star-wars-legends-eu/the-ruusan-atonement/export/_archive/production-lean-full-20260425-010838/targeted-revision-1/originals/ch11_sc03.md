Desh adjusted the approach vector and frowned at the board.

The sensor display bloomed with a pattern Ben hadn’t seen before — none of the dead-zone dropout of a thinned node, none of the jagged spike-and-flatline of a breached chamber. This climbed in smooth, steady increments, like something breathing faster as it neared a line the navcomputer couldn’t name.

“That’s new,” Desh said.

Ben leaned forward in the co-pilot’s seat. The cockpit felt tighter than it had a minute ago, not crowded, just alert, as if the instruments had all turned to watch with him.

The Force ahead wasn’t thinned.

He’d been waiting for the familiar wrongness without quite admitting it — the missing-handrail feeling that had become the background of every node so far. His body had already made room for it, shoulders set against a drop that never quite came, feet braced for footing that should have gone slick. The absence of that absence threw him.

What he felt instead was harder to pin down.

The boundary shimmered. Not to his eyes — the starfield ahead was the same starfield they’d been flying toward for fourteen hours — but in the Force, like heat rippling over durasteel. The space ahead seemed to lean, not break. Not thin. Wait.

He didn’t trust it.

“Readings are stable,” Desh said, voice flat in the way that meant stable was the problem. “No chrono distortion. No gravimetric drift. Whatever’s ahead, it isn’t degrading.”

“Not degrading,” Ben said. “Just different.”

“Yeah.” Desh kept both hands on the controls and made the kind of unnecessary adjustment that meant he was thinking hard. “Different usually costs more.”

Ben didn’t answer. The shimmer held him the way a question held him — not urgent, not threatening, just there. A surface that wanted to tilt. A field that leaned a little toward the ship, as if waiting to see whether they’d commit.

His instinct pushed toward action. Readiness. The part of him trained to assess a threat and meet it wanted to do something — alter course, raise shields, reach with the Force and test the boundary like a lock. But this didn’t feel like a lock. It felt like a choice laid out in front of them, and he had no idea what choosing meant.

So he waited.

Behind him, the cabin stayed quiet. Torin and Kael had gone still when the navcomputer locked onto the approach vector — Torin because he’d learned to read Ben’s silences over the past week, Kael because he never wasted words when silence would do. Sera sat at the sensor station, eyes on the secondary display, her posture carrying the same rigid discipline she’d worn since Veranthos.

The sensor board chirped.

Ben turned.

Sera’s hands were off the console. Both of them. Pulled back to her lap, fingers curled against her palms as if she’d touched something hot and decided not to let anyone see the flinch. Her face had gone still in a way he knew. Not confusion. Recognition.

“What is it?” he asked.

She didn’t answer. Her eyes stayed on the display, where a notation had resolved out of the data — characters Ben couldn’t read, rendered in a script older than the modern alphabet by a thousand years. The seal notation. Gavran’s personal mark.

“Sera.”

She pulled her gaze from the screen. It cost her something; he saw it in the tightening of her shoulders, the set of her jaw before she met his eyes. She was bracing.

“That’s Gavran’s seal,” she said. Her voice stayed even only by effort. “Personal notation. Not part of the anchor system.”

Desh glanced back from the pilot’s seat. “Separate from the network?”

“Yes.”

“Meaning what?”

Sera didn’t answer. Her hands stayed in her lap, still curled, and Ben watched her choose not to say whatever had risen to the back of her throat. The silence stretched.

He wanted to push. The question was right there — what does it mean, what did Gavran seal separately, why are you afraid — and his training said information came first. You didn’t walk into a room without knowing what waited on the other side.

But Sera’s posture stopped him. Not the rigidity — he’d seen that before, had learned the difference between her combat stillness and her argument stillness and the heavier stillness she wore when she was carrying something she didn’t want to hand over. This was the third one. The burdening kind.

He didn’t ask.

Desh caught his eye, one eyebrow lifting in the question Ben had decided not to voice. Ben shook his head once. Not now. Desh let the brow stay up for another beat, then turned back to the controls without comment.

The ship dropped through the haze.

The compound emerged from the high-altitude mist like something cut out of the mountainside instead of built into it. Geometric lines carved the rock — sharp angles, clean planes, a discipline in the architecture that read as cold even from a distance. Outside, the air looked rinsed clean, scrubbed of smell by height and isolation. Nothing grew on the approach. No landing markers. No beacon lights. No sign anyone had been here in a thousand years except the structure itself, which seemed to need no sign.

The sealed entrance was impossible to miss. A single door set into the mountain face, framed by stone cut to fit it so neatly the seam between rock and metal almost vanished. Above it, the same notation glowed faintly — not active, exactly, but present. Waiting.

The group gathered at the viewport. Torin stood with his arms folded, unreadable. Kael hung back by the cockpit door, looking at the compound with the flat patience he used for every new environment — exits, angles, distances, probably already calculating how fast they could get back to the ship if things went bad. Desh stayed at the controls, the engines cycling down toward standby.

Ben stood at the viewport and waited.

Sera stood beside him. She hadn’t spoken since the seal notation appeared on her display, and she didn’t speak now. Her eyes stayed on the door.

The silence in the cabin had weight to it. Not the easy quiet of a long transit, or the focused stillness before a landing. Something heavier. Something that belonged to Sera alone, and that the rest of them were only borrowing.

She looked at the sealed entrance.

“This was Maetha’s,” she said.

Her voice was very quiet. The name hit like a stone dropped into still water — small, precise, the ripples spreading before anyone could stop them.

Ben waited for the rest. The explanation, the context, the reason the name ought to mean something to him. It didn’t come.

The name meant nothing to Ben.

Sera’s expression said it should.

She didn’t elaborate. Didn’t turn to him, didn’t offer the history sitting behind her eyes, didn’t soften the silence or explain it away. She just stood at the viewport, looking at the door Gavran had sealed apart from everything else he’d built, and let the name hang in the cold clear air.

Ben looked at her face. At the set of her jaw. At the way her hands had gone still in her lap, the stillness of someone paying for a decision.

He didn’t ask.

The door waited. The Force ahead leaned toward them, patient, offering something Ben couldn’t name and wasn’t sure he wanted. Sera’s silence filled the cabin like a held breath.
