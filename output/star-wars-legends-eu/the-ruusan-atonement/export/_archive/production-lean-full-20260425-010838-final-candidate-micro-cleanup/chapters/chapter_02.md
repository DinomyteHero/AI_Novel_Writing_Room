# Chapter 2

## Scene 02.01

The *Steady Returns* dropped out of hyperspace like a groundcar hitting a pothole at speed.

Ben’s caf cup—a spare durasteel mug wedged against the copilot console—jumped. Brown liquid splashed across the instrument panel, spattering the nav display and comm board and streaking the sleeve of his tunic before he could get a hand up.

“Sorry,” Desh said, sounding not sorry at all. “Nav recalibration.”

Ben wiped caf off his wrist. It was still hot enough to sting. “You could warn a person.”

“Where’s the fun in that?”

The cockpit was cramped the way all light freighters got after too many modifications—extra sensor packages crowding the overhead, a jury-rigged coolant bypass running along the starboard bulkhead where some previous owner had decided not cooking the hyperdrive mattered more than looks. The *Steady Returns* smelled of recycled air, old upholstery, and the faint metallic tang of a ship that had carried people a long time. Ben had been aboard for three days, and already the smell had faded into the background.

Desh’s caf sat in its own cup—a chipped ceramic thing with a Twi’lek dance hall logo—wedged into a bracket he’d installed beside the pilot’s station. It hadn’t spilled. Ben suspected that wasn’t an accident.

“You timed that,” Ben said.

“I’ve been flying this ship for eight years. I know exactly when reversion’s going to rattle.” Desh tapped the nav display, still beaded with caf. “You’re the one who put your caf on my console.”

“Your console.”

“I’m the pilot. Everything’s my console.”

Ben pulled his sleeve down over his hand and started mopping the instrument panel. The nav display’s touchscreen flickered under the pressure, cycling through readouts—gravimetric stability, spatial positioning, hyperspace emergence variance. Most of the numbers were green. A few had gone yellow.

One was amber and edging toward orange.

“Diagnostic’s running,” Desh said, watching him. “You don’t have to do that with your sleeve.”

“I’m already wet.”

“That’s what towels are for.”

“You don’t own towels.”

“I own towels. They’re in the locker.” Desh lifted his mug and took a sip. “Clean towels. I’m a civilized person.”

Ben kept wiping. Caf had crept into the seam between the nav display and the console housing, a dark line his sleeve couldn’t quite reach. He pressed harder. The display flickered again.

The tug under his ribs hadn’t let up.

It had been there since Coruscant—before Coruscant, if he was honest, a slow pressure he’d been able to shove aside until it had become impossible to ignore. In hyperspace it became a pull, a direction under his ribs drawing him toward a point he couldn’t name. During reversion it had spiked, a sharp lurch like the deck dropping away beneath him, and now it was settling back into something steadier.

Something with edges.

“Diagnostic’s done,” Desh said.

“And?”

“And the nav-computer thinks I’m lying to it.” Desh spun the display so Ben could see. “Gravimetric drift on emergence. Point-three percent off the charts. Spatial positioning variance is worse—the computer keeps recalculating and coming up with a different answer each time.”

“That’s a sensor problem.”

“That’s what I thought.” Desh tapped the panel. The numbers reset, ran again, and came back with the same amber warning. “So I ran it three times. Same result. Then I ran it on the backup array.” Another tap. “Same result. Then I pulled the last six emergences from the log and ran a comparison.”

Ben stopped wiping. “And?”

“And the drift isn’t random.” Desh’s voice had gone dry, the voice of a man who had spent years in intelligence and knew when data was telling him something he didn’t want to hear. “It’s directional. Every emergence, the variance points the same way. Same bearing. And it’s getting stronger the farther we go along the route.”

The pull beneath Ben’s ribs tightened.

“Show me,” he said.

Desh brought up the log on the main display. Six emergences, six sets of variance data, all clustered around a single vector. The pattern was plain enough that Ben didn’t need the numbers; the scatter plot told the story. Tight grouping, each point a little farther from center than the last, all leaning the same direction.

“That’s not instrument error,” Ben said.

“No.” Desh took another sip of caf. “Instrument error doesn’t have a bearing.”

The cockpit went quiet. Hyperspace was gone now; stars held steady through the viewport, and the nav-computer was cycling through recalibration. In another minute they’d jump again. The first survey world was still two hours out.

Ben knew the bearing. He’d known it since Coruscant—the direction the unease kept pulling him, the line his instincts came back to no matter how often he shoved them aside. He’d been trying not to name it, trying to make it background noise, something he could outrun by staying in motion.

Desh’s instruments had named it for him.

“You’re quiet,” Desh said.

“Thinking.”

“That’s the bad kind of quiet.”

Ben almost smiled. It didn’t quite make it to his mouth. “You’ve got a sensor array reading a directional anomaly along our exact route, and you’re not worried?”

“I didn’t say I wasn’t worried.” Desh set his mug down. “I said the data has a bearing. I didn’t say I know what it means.”

“What do you think it means?”

“I think the nav-computer’s confused, and I think the backup array’s confused in the same way, and I think that’s interesting.” He looked at Ben. “I also think you’ve been twitchy since we left Terminus, and you’re not telling me something.”

The words hung in the recycled air. Ben felt the weight of them—the years of shared history between him and Desh, the things they didn’t discuss because discussing them would mean naming what they’d been part of. The GAG. The gray uniforms. The narrow corridors and the rules that only made sense if you didn’t stare too hard.

Desh knew what it meant when a Jedi Knight didn’t call his own Order for backup. He’d known since the first comm call. He hadn’t asked then, and he wasn’t asking now—just leaving the door open, the way he always did, in case Ben wanted to walk through it.

Ben didn’t.

“I’ve been sensing something,” he said. “Since Coruscant. A disturbance in the Force. I don’t know what it is.”

Desh took that in. His expression didn’t change; he just stayed calm and practical, the way he always did when a crisis turned up. “And this disturbance has a bearing.”

“It seems to.”

“The same bearing as my sensor drift.”

Ben’s hands had gone still. He made them stay that way. “It seems to.”

Desh glanced at the display, then at Ben, then back again.

“Well,” he said. “That’s unsettling.”

The nav-computer chimed. Recalibration complete. The first survey world was resolving on the long-range scanners—a small, rocky body around an unremarkable star, the sort of place the Jedi Order surveyed by the dozen every year.

The drift on the sensors didn’t stop. It climbed instead, the amber warning creeping toward red, the variance numbers rising, the bearing holding steady and true and aimed straight at the planet ahead.

Ben’s hands tightened on the armrest.

The pull had teeth now.

## Scene 02.02

The ground under Ben’s boots was the color of old rust, broken into plates that shifted and skated with every step. He picked his way across it with one hand hooked on the strap of his survey pack, the other out for balance. Dry wind came off the east, laden with grit fine enough to work between his collar and his neck.

Behind him, Desh’s boots crunched on gravel at a careful distance. Close enough to keep pace. Far enough not to crowd him.

“Survey record for this one goes back sixty years,” Desh said. “Uninhabited. No structures. No prior occupation. Filed by a Knight named Tarnis.”

“Never heard of him.”

“Her. And neither has anyone else. I checked the Temple registry while you were packing the scanner.” A pause. “She filed three surveys in one year and then transferred to the AgriCorps.”

Ben slowed, scanning the slope ahead. The ruins were visible now—low shapes breaking the rise, half-swallowed by wind erosion and time. Stonework. Definitely stonework. On a world that, according to the record, had been empty sixty years ago.

“AgriCorps,” he said.

“That’s what the file says.”

“That’s one way to bury a report.”

“I didn’t say it was buried. I said she transferred.” Desh’s voice had a dry edge to it. “I’m just giving you the facts.”

“Since when?”

“Since you asked me to come on this survey and I started paying attention to what the Order’s records say about the worlds we’re standing on.”

The ruins were larger than they’d looked from approach. What Ben had taken for a single collapsed structure resolved into a complex—three buildings, maybe four, arranged around a central space that might once have been a courtyard before wind and age chewed it down. Volcanic stone. Dark, dense blocks fitted together without mortar. The seams were tight enough that Ben couldn’t have slipped a fingernail between them.

Desh stopped at the edge of the courtyard and drew out his scanner, a compact civilian model with scuffed edges, something he’d had since his GAG days. “I’m getting nothing. No power signatures, no alloys, no active tech. Just rock.”

“That matches the survey.”

“That matches the survey that said there was nothing here at all.”

Ben walked into the courtyard. The wind dropped as he crossed the threshold, blocked by the surviving walls, and the sudden quiet pressed at his ears. The sensation was here. Not strong—nothing like the spike he’d felt during reversion, when the nav-computer had struggled to place them in space—but present. A low vibration without a source, as if the whole place were still ringing from a blow long after the strike had ended.

He didn’t mention it to Desh.

“The tool-marks,” he said instead, crouching beside an exposed block. “Look at the edges. These weren’t cut with modern equipment.”

Desh leaned over his shoulder. “Old Republic?”

“Older.” Ben ran a finger along the stone without touching it. The cut was clean, precise, made by something that had sliced through volcanic rock like soft wood. “I don’t recognize the technique.”

“That’s a short list.”

“It’s still a list.” Ben straightened and moved deeper into the ruins. The central building had held up better than the others—three walls still standing, the fourth collapsed inward, the roof long gone. Inside, the floor was stone flags, some cracked, most intact. On the far wall, sheltered from the wind by the angle of the surviving stone, were markings.

Ben stopped.

They were carved at chest height—a series of shapes that didn’t resolve into any alphabet he knew. Interlocking circles. A jagged line cutting through them at an angle, deliberate and precise, the edges still sharp after what had to be centuries. Whatever had cut them had been harder than the stone. Harder than anything in a standard survey kit.

“What is that?” Desh asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Guess.”

“I don’t guess.” Ben pulled the datapad from his pack and started recording. Wide shots first, establishing the wall, the context, the relation of the markings to the rest of the structure. Then closer. The circles. The line. The way the cut deepened at the intersection points, as if the carver had pressed harder there.

His professional curiosity ran clean and focused, the way it always did when a puzzle presented itself. Catalog first. Hypothesize later. The markings were data. The tool-marks were data. The age of the stone, the erosion patterns, the absence of any known architectural tradition—all data.

The low vibration sharpened.

He finished the visual record and lowered the datapad. The markings were right there at eye level, the stone dark and cool in the shadow of the wall. He could see the faint striations inside the cuts, the tiny variations in depth that spoke to a hand-guided tool rather than a machine. Whoever had carved this had done it slowly.

He reached out.

His fingers brushed the edge of the jagged line.

The jolt ran up his arm.

Not a shock—nothing electric, nothing that burned or stung. A vibration. Low, steady, with no physical source, climbing from his fingertips to his wrist to his elbow like a plucked string that refused to die away. The stone was stone. The cut was a cut. But the distance between his hand and the wall had gone bad, a fractional error his body registered before his mind could catch up. The stone was here. His fingers were here. The space between them wasn’t right.

He pulled his hand back hard enough to leave room between himself and the wall.

His breathing had changed. He could hear it. One breath. Two. He forced the air out slowly the way he’d been trained, and the vibration thinned to a hum, then to an aftertaste he couldn’t name.

Desh was watching him. “Ben?”

“Give me a minute.”

“You pulled back like it burned you.”

“It didn’t burn.” Ben stood. His legs worked. His hand was steady when he looked at it. “I’m fine.”

“You’re a terrible liar.”

“I’m an excellent liar. You’re just hard to fool.” He turned back to the datapad and started the scanner. The markings resolved on the screen—shapes, dimensions, depth measurements, mineral composition of the surrounding stone. Data. Clean, objective, transmittable data.

The vibration was still in his hand, a phantom sensation like the memory of a sound he couldn’t quite hear.

He recorded the shapes. He recorded the tool-marks. He recorded the age estimate based on erosion patterns and the geological context and the dimensions of the structure and every other piece of information the standard survey template asked for.

He did not record what the Force had told him.

The omission was deliberate. He knew that. He knew it before he finished the first data field, before he attached the images, before he keyed in Luke’s comm code on the transmission header. He was leaving something out. Choosing to leave it out.

He didn’t ask himself why. He just did it.

“Report going in?” Desh asked.

“Now.”

“You’re including the markings.”

“Yes.”

“You’re not including whatever made you jump.”

Ben’s thumb hovered over the send key. The wind had picked up again, sliding through the gaps in the walls, and the grit it carried stung the back of his neck. High overhead, a freighter’s contrail cut across the sky—someone moving supplies through the sector, unaware and uninterested in what was buried on this rock.

“No,” Ben said. “I’m not.”

Desh didn’t answer. He didn’t need to. The silence between them was the same silence they’d learned in narrow corridors years ago, wearing gray uniforms and following rules that only made sense if you didn’t stare too hard. The quiet of a man who had decided something and wasn’t going to explain it.

Ben pressed send.

The transmission went. The datapad chimed confirmation. Somewhere in the Temple on Coruscant, a server received a survey report that was technically accurate and structurally incomplete, and Luke Skywalker would read it and see the markings and the measurements and the careful catalog of an anomaly his son had found on a world that wasn’t supposed to have one.

He would not see the sensation behind it. He would not know that Ben had touched the stone and felt something that didn’t belong in any report.

The vibration stayed in Ben’s hand. He flexed his fingers, trying to shake it loose, and failed.

“Let’s finish the site,” he said. “We’ve got another two hours of daylight.”

“Ben.”

“Later, Desh.”

He started toward the next structure before Desh could answer. The wind followed him, dry and full of grit, and the sensation followed with it. Somewhere behind his ribs, the unease that had been building since the nav-computer first showed him the bearing settled into something harder. Not fear. Not yet.

Dread. The specific dread of a man who had just hidden something from his father and didn’t know what it meant about either of them.

## Scene 02.03

The second world was colder than the first, not because of season, but because of something older buried in the rock—something that didn’t care what the weather thought it was doing.

Ben crouched in the lee of a broken wall and ran his thumb over stone that should not have been here. Same markings. Interlocking circles. A jagged line slashing through them at the same angle, same depth, same slight overcut at the intersections where the carver had pressed too hard. The stone was different—granitic, not volcanic, darker, harder, wrong for the work—and the cuts were still clean, still sharp enough to snag the edge of his glove.

His chest tightened before he noticed it. He forced a breath in anyway.

“Same hand,” he said.

Desh stood a few meters back, scanner in hand, jacket zipped against a wind that had teeth. “You’re sure.”

“I’m sure.”

“Same tool?”

“Same hand.” Ben straightened. His knees ached from the cold seeping through the stone flags. “The tool I can’t speak to. The pressure, the angle, the way the cut deepens at the intersections—that’s a person, not a machine. Same person.”

Desh studied him for a long moment, then the markings, then him again. “One site was interesting.”

“I know.”

“Two sites is a pattern.”

“I know.”

“And a pattern means someone put these here on purpose.”

Ben didn’t answer. The tightness in his chest had climbed into his throat. The feeling here was stronger than on the first world—not a spike that jolted through his hand and left him shaking, but something steadier, a pressure that didn’t belong to the wind or the cold. It sat in the stone with no source he could point to, a vibration that seemed to live in the rock itself. The space between his body and the wall felt off, as if his eyes and his spine were giving him slightly different answers.

He didn’t touch the carvings this time. He didn’t need to.

“Record it,” he said. “Full imaging. I want the tool-mark analysis run against the first site before we break atmosphere.”

“Already started.” Desh tucked away the scanner and pulled out his datapad. “I grabbed the first site’s data while you were in the courtyard. The computer’s running the comparison now. Should have results by the time we’re back aboard.”

Ben turned from the wall. The ruins here were smaller than the first site—a single structure, maybe a waystation, built from the same dark stone and fitted with the same impossible precision. The roof had collapsed centuries ago. Wind and ice had chewed the remaining walls raw, but the carvings were sheltered in the deepest corner, protected by an overhang that had held through everything the climate could throw at it.

Deliberate placement. Someone had wanted these to last.

“Desh.”

“Yeah.”

“If you were building something you wanted found a thousand years later, how would you do it?”

Desh considered that. “I’d put it somewhere I knew someone would look.”

“That’s what I thought.”

They finished the site in forty minutes. Ben catalogued everything the survey template wanted—dimensions, materials, erosion patterns, geological context, mineral composition of the surrounding stone, atmospheric readings, local vegetation analysis. He recorded the markings from three angles and ran the scanner over them twice. He did not record the pressure in his chest or the vibration in the stone or the way the feeling had sharpened from a hum to something almost directional, a pull he could feel in his teeth.

Leaving that out was easier the second time.

---

The *Steady Returns* was warm. Recycled air, the hum of environmental systems, the faint smell of caf from the galley. Ben stood behind Desh’s chair in the cockpit and watched the nav console build its model.

Two systems. Two survey worlds. Two sets of ruins that shouldn’t have existed on worlds his father’s Order had marked empty for decades.

Desh overlaid them on the main display. The first world glowed blue. The second glowed amber. Between them stretched a line of empty space—no charted systems, no mapped routes, no survey markers, nothing. Just void.

“That’s the bearing,” Desh said.

“I can see that.”

“The bearing you’ve been tracking since we left Coruscant.”

“I can see that too.”

Desh extended the line. It cut through the empty sector and kept going, straight as a rule, into a region of the chart that was blank in a way that didn’t match the surrounding data. Stars on one side. Stars on the other. Nothing in between—not even the standard notation for uncharted space, the gray hash that meant they hadn’t looked there yet. This was deliberate absence. A hole in the map someone had cut out and thrown away.

“That’s not normal,” Desh said.

“No.”

“That’s fourteen systems that should be there and aren’t.”

Ben leaned over the console. The line Desh had drawn ran straight through the center of the void, no deviation, no curve. Whatever was out there, it sat exactly where the bearing said it would.

“Punch the coordinates,” he said.

Desh keyed them in. The nav-computer took a long moment to answer—longer than it should have, long enough that Ben felt the silence in the cockpit settle into something heavier. Then the display shifted. The void stayed void. The coordinates hung in the middle of it, attached to no system name, no survey designation, no navigational hazard marker.

Nothing there. According to every chart in the Order’s database, there was nothing there.

Ben stared at the empty sector. The feeling had a shape now. A direction. A destination. It wasn’t just something he felt anymore—it was somewhere he could go.

“Luke’s survey route,” he said.

“What about it?”

“It runs right along this bearing. Every world we’ve stopped at. Every world still on the schedule.” He kept his eyes on the display. “Coincidence or design.”

Desh was quiet for a moment. Then: “You don’t want to know which one it is.”

“I don’t want to know yet.”

“Ben.”

“I know.”

He didn’t explain. He didn’t need to. The silence between them was the same silence they’d learned years ago, wearing gray uniforms and following rules that only made sense if you didn’t stare too hard—the quiet of a man who had decided something and wasn’t going to defend it. Desh read him the way Desh always read him, and chose not to press.

The bearing pointed to a cluster of systems that did not appear on any navigation chart Ben had ever seen. Something was out there.

This one did.

Ben’s breath caught. He stared at the hyperspace tunnel beyond the viewport, the endless blue-white stream of a dimension with no geography and no direction except the one the nav-computer assigned it. But the disturbance had a direction now. It had a bearing. It was drawing him toward something specific—something waiting at the end of the survey route his father had given him, the route that ran straight along that bearing, the route Luke had chosen for the possibility that he wasn’t imagining it.

The pressure stopped being a problem.

It became a destination.
