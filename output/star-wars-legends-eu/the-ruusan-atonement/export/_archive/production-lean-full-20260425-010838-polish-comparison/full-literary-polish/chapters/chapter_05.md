# Chapter 5

## Scene 05.01

Ben’s hand closed around the release lever.

The metal was cold. He had half expected warmth—the way the monitoring panels had warmed under his touch, the way the fortress systems had answered with grudging recognition. But the lever stayed cold in his palm, resisting for an instant before the mechanism gave. Then it moved, and the sound that followed was not mechanical.

It was breath.

The first chamber’s stasis field dissolved with a long, low hiss, like pressure finally giving way after a thousand years. Blue light shimmered behind the transparisteel, flared once, and went out. The seal irised open. Cold air spilled into the ring, carrying a smell Ben couldn’t place: dry, faintly metallic, like old stone after rain.

The woman inside opened her eyes.

She didn’t blink. She didn’t hesitate. She came out of the chamber in one smooth motion, legs folding under her, weight dropping into a combat crouch. One hand cut toward her hip before her fingers closed on empty air. No sidearm. No rank-tab. Her hand stayed there for a full second, as if she could will the missing weapon into existence, before her gaze found Ben’s face.

Her presence hit him a moment later. Sharp. Disciplined. Not the smooth, settled imprint he’d felt from modern Jedi, but something harder, shaped by older training. The Force around her felt like a blade laid flat on a table: still, but ready to be snatched up. Under the discipline lay another tension, the weight of someone who had learned to obey orders she didn’t agree with.

She straightened with a soldier’s precision.

“Identify yourself.”

Her voice was rough from a millennium of silence, but the syntax was intact. Complete sentences. Formal address. She didn’t ask where she was or what had happened. She asked for identification and chain of command, because command language was the only solid ground she had left.

Ben released the lever. “Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight.”

Her eyes narrowed, not at the name, but at the title. At the way he wore it. She was reading him the way she would read a battlefield, measuring posture and equipment and the weight of his presence in the Force, and whatever she found didn’t fit what she expected.

“Jedi,” she said. The word held both recognition and question. “Whose command?”

“The New Jedi Order.”

The second chamber’s field failed before she could answer.

The man who came out was taller than Ben had expected, broad through the shoulders, his robes the same faded gray as the fortress walls. He staggered on the threshold, one hand snapping to the doorframe, and for an instant his weight hung there the way a climber’s might at the end of a long ascent.

Then he looked past Ben. Past the woman. His eyes swept the ring, counting.

His presence reached Ben like heat from a furnace door. Warm. Heavy. It settled into the room the way a body settled into a chair after a long day—not relaxed, exactly, but fully present. There was something deep in it that Ben couldn’t name. Not dark. Not light. Just settled, like old stone. Like grief carried long enough to become part of the one carrying it.

“Eight,” the man said. His voice was steadier than the woman’s, a low baritone with an archaic courtesy in the vowels. “Eight dark. Three lit.” He straightened, still holding the frame. “And one open.”

The woman turned her head. Her eyes went to the empty bay—the peeled alloy, the silver gouges catching the emergency light—and her jaw tightened. She didn’t speak.

Behind him, Ben heard Desh shift, a small scrape as he changed stance. Desh had taken up position near the stairwell, out of the immediate line of approach, and he hadn’t said a word since Ben pulled the lever. That was training. That was still the GAG year living in his bones: don’t spook the assets, don’t complicate the contact, let the point man work.

The woman’s eyes came back to Ben. Her assessment was done, and whatever she had found hadn’t settled her.

“You’re Jedi-trained,” she said, “but not of my era. Your presence is…” She paused, searching. “Thinner. Quieter. The Force doesn’t move the same way around you.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was still feeling the man’s presence, still registering how sharply it cut against the woman’s edge. Two signatures. Two completely different histories, two different kinds of training, and both of them had just woken a thousand years removed from everything they knew.

The woman took a step forward, her hand still at her hip where the absent sidearm should have been.

“What year is it?”

The question hit like a fist. Ben had known it was coming. He had known it since he touched the release lever. That didn’t make the answer any easier to give.

He told her.

The number hung in the cold air. The woman took it in through her body first—her spine straightening a fraction, her shoulders squaring, the way a soldier received a casualty report. The man took it differently. His hand tightened on the frame, and his gaze went to the dark chambers. Something moved behind his face that Ben couldn’t read.

The third chamber’s field dissolved.

The man who stepped out was smaller than the others, lean, dark-haired, his robes cut closer to the body. He didn’t stagger. He didn’t speak. He found the wall with his shoulders and stopped, back against the alloy, eyes sweeping the ring in one efficient pass: chambers, exits, personnel, threats.

His presence was the quietest of the three. Clipped. Guarded. It didn’t project; it drew inward, the way a man drew his coat tighter against cold. Ben felt the shape of it without being able to pin it down: a signature trained into efficiency by someone who had learned that visibility was dangerous, that being sensed was a liability, that Sith instructors noticed strong opinions and punished them.

The silence stretched.

The woman—she still hadn’t given her name—was holding the year in her posture, doing the private math of what a thousand years had cost her. The tall man had stopped counting chambers and was looking at Ben with an expression that wasn’t quite curiosity. The third man hadn’t moved from the wall.

Ben realized he was holding his breath. He let it out slowly.

The woman spoke first.

“My name is Sera Varik. I was field lieutenant under Gavran’s command at Veranthos.” The words came out formal, almost ritual, like a soldier reporting for duty. “These men are—”

“Torin Hal.” The tall man released the frame and straightened fully. There was an archaic courtesy in his tone, but something steadier under it. “Campaign commander. I knew Gavran. I knew what he was trying to do here.” His eyes flicked to the empty bay. “I didn’t know it would come to this.”

The third man said nothing.

Ben looked at him. The man looked back. Dark eyes, unreadable, and they didn’t drop. It wasn’t a challenge. It was waiting. The way someone waited who had been taught that speaking first was a mistake, that answers came when ordered, that survival depended on knowing when to stay invisible.

“Your name,” Ben said.

A pause. Then, quiet and flat: “Kael Drenn.”

Nothing else. No rank. No unit. No explanation for why a Sith-trained conscript was sealed in a Jedi containment ring. Just the name, delivered in a voice shaped by obedience drills and survival kata, and then silence.

Torin Hal looked at the eight dark chambers, then at the empty one, then at the emergency indicators blinking amber on the panels. His presence shifted—the warmth in it flickered, like a flame stirred by an opening door, and steadied again.

“Who got out?” he said quietly.

The question was for the room—for Sera, still holding the year in her shoulders, and for Kael, still pressed to the wall, and for the eight dark bays that had stayed dark far too long.

Ben didn’t answer. He was looking at the empty chamber—the peeled alloy, the silver gouges, the blinking amber warning—and doing his own arithmetic. Twelve chambers. Eight dead. Three survivors. One escapee with a three-week head start.

Sera turned from the empty bay and looked at Ben. Her eyes were the color of old steel, and they held the same question Torin had asked, though she didn’t voice it. She was waiting for Ben to answer, because Ben was the one who had woken them, and that made him the one who owed them the truth.

Torin was still looking at the empty chamber. His presence had settled again, but something lay under it now. Pressure, maybe. The tension of a man who had just counted the dead and found one missing.

“Who got out?” he said again.

Ben met his eyes. The answer felt heavier than the year, heavier than the eight dark bays, heavier than the weight of three people waking up a millennium removed from everything they knew.

“We don’t know yet,” he said. “But we’re going to find out.”

## Scene 05.02

Sera crossed to the archive console without asking permission.

Her stride was shorter than Ben had expected—compact, efficient, the gait of someone who had spent years threading ship corridors and command decks alike. She didn’t bother with the console’s markings. Her fingers found the activation points from memory: three presses in sequence across a panel that had been dead for a millennium, and the console hummed awake under her touch.

Ben’s chest tightened. The sound was too clean, too ready, as if the archive had been waiting for exactly those fingers. He’d spent an hour on Level 2 before waking the survivors, trying every access protocol his Order had taught him, and the console had given him nothing but amber standby lights and administrative lockouts in a register he couldn’t read. Now Sera was three keystrokes in, and the main display was already resolving into a command index.

Desh shifted near the doorway. Ben didn’t turn, but he felt it anyway: weight redistributed, the easy stillness of a man who had learned to disappear in rooms where something important was happening.

“Veranthos containment protocol,” Sera said. Her voice was flat, a field report forced through discipline. “Gavran’s seal architecture. The index is here.”

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t have to. The words hit Ben like a door slamming shut—the abstract unease he had been chasing since the survey run was suddenly concrete, archived, and named by someone who had lived inside it.

Torin stepped forward before Ben could answer. His hand moved over the display with the same thousand-year-old familiarity, pulling files into the holographic field, and Ben’s skin registered the strangeness of it before his mind did. Not the Force—there was still that, pressing at the edges of his awareness like a note held a hair flat. This was something else. A man moving through a room he’d helped build, touching controls he had last touched when the Old Republic was still an experiment.

“Twelve chambers in the ring,” Torin said, his voice carrying the warm baritone Ben had heard in the stasis bay. “Gavran was specific. Twelve anchors, one central keystone—himself. Thirteen total, if you count the man who designed the lock.” He paused, something flickering behind his expression. “We called it containment. Gavran called it penance. The distinction mattered more to him than it did to the rest of us.”

Ben’s focus narrowed. The public timeline rose in his mind without effort—the Seventh Battle of Ruusan, the aftermath of the New Sith Wars, the Reformations that dissolved the Army of Light, ended the Jedi Lords, centralized training, and bound the Order more closely to Republic and Judicial oversight. He’d learned it from his father’s Order, from the records Luke had rebuilt after the Purge, from the history every Jedi Knight was supposed to carry. The Veranthos index predated all of it.

“The Reformations came after,” Ben said. The words felt slow in his mouth, a sentence assembling itself while his mind still reached for the edges of it. “After Ruusan. After the New Sith Wars. The public reforms—the Army of Light, the Jedi Lords, the training changes, the oversight—those were real. They happened.”

Sera kept her eyes on the display. “I don’t know what happened after Ruusan. I was sealed before the aftermath.” The words came out clipped and exact, a soldier reporting facts she had no room left to turn over. “I know what Gavran built at Veranthos. Containment. Protocol. Seals. Human cost. The rest is your history, not mine.”

Ben heard the line she had drawn. Not evasion. Discipline. She was stopping at the edge of what she could testify to and refusing to pretend otherwise.

Kael moved.

It was the first voluntary thing Ben had seen him do since the stasis chamber opened. He reached past Sera—not quickly, not aggressively, just a lean figure in close-cut robes extending one hand toward the display—and tapped a designation on the Veranthos index. The text shifted. An archaic administrative term gave way to another.

“Keeper,” Kael said. His voice was quiet, spare, the same register he’d used when he gave his name in the ring. “Not architect. Gavran was the keeper. Custodian of the protocol. He didn’t design the seal. He engineered it from something older.”

He stepped back. No explanation. No glance at Torin or Sera. Just the retreat to the wall, arms loose at his sides, and the silence he left behind weighed more than the sentence.

Ben felt the correction land before he sorted out its meaning. Kael’s movement—the reach, the tap, the step back—read as a person coming into focus. The monk’s brevity landed harder than explanation would have. He had been waiting for something worth saying, and the distinction between architect and keeper was worth saying to him in a way nothing else had been.

Torin’s expression changed. Something passed across his face—surprise, maybe, or recognition of a detail he had missed a thousand years ago—and then it was gone, replaced by that steady warmth. “He’s right,” Torin said quietly. “Gavran was the keeper. I’d forgotten he used that word.”

Sera finally turned from the console. Her eyes met Ben’s, and her composure remained absolute—the discipline of someone who had learned to deliver hard facts without breaking stride.

“Private penance-and-containment,” she said. “That’s what the Veranthos protocol was. Not public reform. Not legislation. A concealed layer under what came after. Gavran buried it because he couldn’t live with what we’d done, and he couldn’t let anyone else know we’d done it.”

The sentence landed cleanly. No apology. No cushioning. Just the institutional fact, delivered in a voice shaped by command reports and battlefield triage, and Ben felt the connection strike before his intellect could keep up.

The public Ruusan Reformations were real. The Army of Light had dissolved. The Jedi Lords had ended. Training had centralized under Republic and Judicial oversight. All of that was true—his father’s Order had taught it, the archives confirmed it, the modern Jedi Order was built on it. But beneath that public history, carried in silence for a thousand years, was a concealed penance. A containment system built from twelve living anchors and one central keystone. A wound the Order had chosen to bury instead of name.

The drop through Ben’s center of gravity felt almost physical. His mind raced ahead: if the private protocol was penance, what was it penance for? What had Gavran done at Veranthos that required twelve people to be sealed alive? What had the old Order chosen to hide so completely that even the history his father had rebuilt after the Purge didn’t mention it?

He looked at the three survivors. Sera, still at the console with her shoulders squared and one hand at her hip where the absent sidearm should have been—the instrument of precision, used by a system that had weaponized her discipline. Torin, whose warmth carried responsibility without softness, the keeper of narrative texture who had been voluntary in a way the others hadn’t been. Kael, still near the wall, whose first contribution had been a correction—the quiet voice that had waited a thousand years to say: you’re naming him wrong.

The archive around them was no longer a room full of records. It was testimony. Three people who had been made into infrastructure, now standing in the room where their making had been documented, and the penance remained unnamed.

Ben did not know what to do with that yet. He only knew the history his Order had inherited was true but incomplete, and the missing piece was the one that mattered most.

## Scene 05.03

The monitoring station on Level 1 was a narrow slot carved into the fortress wall, lit by a single display that flickered amber with the exhaustion of systems that had outlived their makers by centuries. Ben stood close enough to feel the panel’s heat on his face, while the recycled air left the back of his neck cold.

The logs for Chamber Seven crawled across the screen in archaic administrative Basic, clipped and procedural, their timestamps pegged to a calendar that had been obsolete before the Republic had its name. He read by shape more than by word, letting the headings and spacing tell him what the glyphs refused to.

The readable portion ended twenty-three standard days ago.

Ben stopped the scroll. The cursor blinked at the break, and beyond it the display dissolved into scrambled blocks and warped timestamps, the visual equivalent of static. Not wear. Not the slow decay of dying storage. The break began cleanly at a precise point and ran on from there, as if something had reached into the record and yanked the thread free.

“Desh.”

Desh was already at the secondary terminal, his hands moving over a panel that had never been built for someone who couldn’t touch the Force. He had found the access paths anyway, the way he found most things—by treating a locked system as sequence, not permission.

“I see it,” Desh said. “Clean cut. Twenty-three days. The rest is slag.”

“Accident?”

“The sort that knows exactly where to stop being readable.” Desh tapped a line of text Ben couldn’t see from where he stood. “Terminal access logs for this chamber show activity after the break. Someone was still working the system after the monitoring went blind. Different access pattern—careful, repeat visits. They knew what they wanted.”

Ben felt the weight of that settle across his shoulders before the rest of him caught up. Not an escape. An operation. Someone had broken out of a stasis chamber built to hold Force-sensitives for millennia, and their first move had been to sit down at a terminal and start reading.

“What did they access?”

“Navigation records. Supply inventories. The fortress schematic.” Desh kept his tone flat, the way he did when things were bad enough to leave no room for emphasis. “They were planning a trip.”

The word hung in the recycled air. Trip. Desh had chosen it on purpose—a plain word for something that was anything but plain, the way an intelligence man used understatement to carry more weight than he wanted his voice to show.

Ben turned from the monitor. Sera had come into the chamber behind him without making a sound. Her attention was already on the walls.

The damage inside the empty chamber wasn’t frantic scoring from someone who had panicked and clawed their way out. It was orderly. Layers removed in repeating patterns, the work of someone who had spent centuries worrying at a prison one careful pass at a time, patient as water on stone.

Sera’s hand stopped an inch from the wall. She didn’t touch it. Her fingers hovered over a section where the erosion had bitten through to the structural alloy beneath the surface coating, and Ben saw her precision falter.

Only for a heartbeat. Her shoulders tightened, her jaw set, and then the discipline was back in place—a soldier’s control, hard-won and polished to a shine. She lowered her hand.

“Sera.”

She shook her head once. Not no. Not yet. Not here. The kind of gesture that said she needed one more look before she put the thing into words.

Ben didn’t push. The silence between them took on the weight her voice had refused to carry, and he let it stand.

Kael was farther back, near the doorway, his arms loose at his sides. He was looking at the same marks Sera had studied, but his expression had gone flatter, the look of someone who recognized another captive’s discipline and didn’t forgive what it had become. He said nothing. The silence around him changed texture anyway. Not ignorance. Recognition held back.

Ben filed that away.

Torin stood at the doorway. He hadn’t come inside; he leaned one shoulder against the frame, arms crossed, casual in a way that still managed to feel motionless. His gaze was on the erosion marks, and there was something warm in his expression.

“You have to admire the patience,” Torin said.

The words were easy, conversational, the kind of observation he had been making since waking—the storyteller’s habit of finding the human angle in any room, of putting shape around a thing so everyone else could breathe.

But the inflection was off.

Ben caught it the way a musician caught a note a hair flat—not enough to stop the tune, not enough to name on the spot, but enough to make the back of his neck tighten. A warmth that sat one degree off from where it should have been. As if Torin were describing not a threat, but something he understood.

The dissonance passed before Ben could pin it down. Torin was still in the doorway, still wearing that easy half-smile, and the moment had already moved on. Ben didn’t try to explain it to himself. He filed it the way he filed sensor glitches that might matter later and might not mean anything at all.

He turned back to the display.

“Overlay the access timestamps on the fortress schematic.”

Desh worked the terminal. The map bloomed across the screen—Level 3, the stasis ring, the corridors running upward toward the landing bays. The timestamps gathered into a pattern that told the story without a name attached to it. Chamber Seven. Terminal access on Level 3. Supply stores on Level 2. The landing bay. Then nothing.

“Twenty-three days,” Ben said. The words came out steadier than he felt. “They’ve had twenty-three days.”

“Minimum,” Desh said. “If the logs went blind when they left, we don’t know what they’ve been doing since. Could be longer.”

The map sat there, and the pattern was methodical. The escapee hadn’t fled in a blind rush. They had prepared. Studied the fortress. Taken what they needed. Walked out with the calm of someone who knew exactly where they were going.

The direction was clear. The Corridor.

Ben’s breath evened out. His mind was already running ahead—the next step, the next move, the thing that had to happen now because the alternative was standing still while someone else got farther away.

“Pull every monitoring packet from the ring,” he said. “Every chamber, every timestamp, every access log you can reconstruct. I want to know when they started, what they touched, and whether there’s anything in the corrupted data we can recover.”

Desh nodded once. He didn’t ask when Ben wanted it. The answer was already in the room.

“First light,” Ben said.

The weight of what he knew, and what he still didn’t, settled across his shoulders. An escapee without a name. Twenty-three standard days of silence on the logs. Torin’s one inflection that he couldn’t quite place, sitting at the edge of his awareness like a note held a fraction too long.

He still didn’t have a name. He had a direction. And the direction was already ahead of them.

## Scene 05.04

The command table in the Steady Returns’ common area had been built for two people to review survey data over caf. Ben spread the fortress material across it anyway—Corridor maps overlapping at the corners, seal readings spilling onto the bench seat, the chamber file from Level 3 laid beside his left elbow with a name-shaped blank where the occupant designation should have been.

He arranged everything the way he’d been trained to arrange intelligence before a briefing: terrain first, then threat, then the thing nobody knew yet. The blank in the chamber file was that thing. His eyes kept snagging on it.

Sera and Torin had already taken over the bench along the starboard bulkhead without discussing it. The archive materials they had hauled up from the fortress—data cards in formats the Steady Returns’ reader barely recognized, handwritten notes on flimsi that had outlasted the civilization that made them—moved between their hands in a rhythm that looked practiced.

It wasn’t practiced. It was muscle memory. Ben watched Torin pass Sera a stack of cards without looking up, watched her take them with her left hand while her right kept sorting, and understood he was watching two people who had done this exact work together a thousand years ago. The ease of it gave the compartment a warmer feel, which somehow made it smaller.

Sera’s sorting was exact, each item placed where it belonged in a system Ben could see but not yet read. Torin’s arrangement held the same information, but grouped by what followed what, the way a storyteller lined facts up so they would land in order. Ben let the difference sit. He didn’t comment.

He didn’t need to. The choice was already being made around him. These two were organizing for departure, not for archive. They were assuming he would follow.

His fingers tightened on the datapad.

Desh climbed up from the engineering crawlspace with grease on his knuckles and a data slate in his off hand. He looked at the spread across the command table, looked at Ben, and didn’t ask the question Ben could see forming behind his eyes. Instead he set the slate on the only clear corner of the table and tapped the display.

“The corrupted packets from Chamber Seven,” Desh said. “It isn’t random degradation. Somebody ran a selective wipe before they left—targeted their own designation, the access timestamps for the last six months, and the supply manifest for what they took. Everything else is intact or recoverable.”

Ben’s eyes went to the blank in the chamber file. “Can you pull it back?”

“Not here. The fortress terminals are too degraded for that much processing. But if I queue it through the Steady Returns’ response-quadrant architecture overnight, the packet structure should resolve by morning.” Desh paused. “The name will be there.”

Overnight. The word sat in the recycled air like a weight. Ben did the math without letting himself look at it too closely. Luke’s reply window on the survey report was open now. If he waited for the name, he’d be waiting past the point where his father would expect confirmation that the survey was still on schedule. If he lifted now, he could send a partial report before the window closed—enough to explain the delay, not enough to—

He cut the thought off before it finished.

“First light,” Ben said. “We lift at first light. The packets can run while we’re in transit.”

Desh nodded once. He didn’t ask about the report. The way he didn’t ask was information in itself—the calm of a man who recognized another man’s decision not to call home.

Kael had been standing near the cockpit access ladder for the last several minutes. Ben hadn’t seen him come in. He was braced against the bulkhead with his arms loose at his sides, watching the archive sorting with the flat expression he wore when he was cataloguing things he didn’t intend to discuss. His eyes moved from Sera’s hands to Torin’s, tracking the rhythm the way Ben had tracked it, and whatever he found there made him look away.

“Twenty-three days,” Kael said.

It wasn’t a question.

“Minimum,” Ben said.

Kael took that in. His face didn’t change, but something in his shoulders shifted—a tightening that read less like fear than recognition. He didn’t spell it out. Ben didn’t expect him to.

Sera finished sorting and stood. The movement was economical, no wasted motion, the way she did everything. She looked at the command table, at the Corridor maps overlapping at the corners, at the chamber file with its blank.

“You’re not waiting for the name,” she said.

“No.”

She held his gaze a beat longer than necessary. Her expression stayed unreadable—not hostile, not warm, the look of a soldier weighing a commander’s decision without volunteering approval. Then she nodded once, the same exact motion, and turned back to the archive materials.

Torin was already watching Ben. His expression was easier than Sera’s, the warmth still there, but something behind it was heavier than the moment required—attention that seemed to carry more than curiosity.

“You’re composing the report now,” Torin said.

It wasn’t a question either.

Ben didn’t answer. He picked up the datapad and walked to the cockpit.

The pilot’s seat was familiar in a way the rest of the compartment wasn’t. Ben sat down and brought up the comm interface. The standard survey update template filled the screen—location, status, findings, recommendations. He filled in the location. He filled in the status: delay, survey complication, continued investigation. He did not fill in the findings.

His thumb hovered over transmit.

The Force around him was still deadened, still spread through the wider sky like a pressure he couldn’t pin down. It gave him nothing. No warning, no confirmation, none of the subtle cues he’d learned to trust since he was old enough to tell when something was off. The disturbance was just there, present and diffuse and unreliable.

He sent the report before he could change his mind.

The confirmation chime was soft. Ben sat in the pilot’s seat for another thirty seconds, staring at the blank screen and pointedly not thinking about what he had just done. Then he stood and went back to the common area.

Desh was already in the copilot’s seat when Ben returned to the cockpit an hour later. The fortress plateau stretched below the viewport in predawn gray, the mountain rising behind it like a tomb marker. Ben ran the preflight checks with his hands while Desh called out the numbers in a flat voice that didn’t invite conversation.

The engines caught. The Steady Returns shuddered—the extra weight of five passengers registering in the drive pitch—and then the ship climbed, the plateau shrinking, the fortress resolving into what it had always been: a lid over something still failing underneath.

Sera came forward as they cleared atmosphere. She stood behind the copilot’s seat with one hand resting on the headrest, not touching the controls, eyes on the sensor display. Her fingers hovered near the interface without making contact—close enough to read the data, restrained enough to acknowledge that the ship was not hers.

Torin stood in the cockpit doorway. He didn’t try to come forward. He leaned against the frame the way he had leaned against the chamber doorway on Level 3, arms crossed, watching the starfield open ahead of them with an expression Ben couldn’t read.

Kael had taken the jump seat in the common area. Through the cockpit access Ben could see him sitting with his hands on his knees, deliberately still, like a man keeping himself from touching things he didn’t understand.

The Steady Returns leveled out. The Corridor stretched ahead on the nav display, a chain of anchor nodes through uncharted space. Somewhere along that chain, twenty-three standard days ahead of them, an escapee without a name was moving.

The monitoring packets were already queued. By morning they would resolve. By morning Ben would know who he was chasing.

He set the heading and engaged the hyperdrive.
