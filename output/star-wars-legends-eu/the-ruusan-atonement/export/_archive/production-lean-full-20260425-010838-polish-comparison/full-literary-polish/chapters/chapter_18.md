# Chapter 18

## Scene 18.01

Torin was laughing.

It was a good laugh—warm, easy, landing exactly on the beat Desh had set up for it. Desh had said something about the coolant valve in the engineering crawlspace, the one that had seized twice since they’d left the bunker, and Torin had leaned back in his chair and let the sound roll out of him like he’d just heard the funniest thing in the galaxy.

Ben watched and filed it away.

The timing was flawless. So was the modulation. The way Torin’s shoulders loosened and his hand lifted in a loose, *you know how it is* gesture had the practiced ease of someone who’d told stories in common rooms for years and knew exactly when to let the laugh land. It was generous. It made people want to deserve the next one.

Ben’s mouth was dry. He’d been in the corner of the common area for twenty minutes, nursing a cup of caf that had gone cold a long time ago, and somewhere in the last five minutes he’d stopped following the conversation and started reading it. The way his training had taught him to read a face. The way Sera read archival text. The way he’d once watched a different man hold a room and not understood what he was seeing until it was too late.

“When did you start doing that?” Desh asked.

Not really a question. Desh was leaning against the galley counter, arms folded, his attention on Torin but his voice pitched low enough to stay inside Ben’s corner.

Ben didn’t answer. He didn’t need to. Desh had been watching him watch Torin, and Desh had seen the same thing Ben had seen. The only difference was that Desh already knew the name for it.

Torin was telling a story now. A battle story—one of the New Sith Wars engagements, the kind he’d told a dozen times since the fortress and never in quite the same way twice. His hands moved with the words, sketching formations over the common-area table, and his voice kept the easy rhythm of a man who had been there, survived it, and was generous enough to let the rest of them survive it in the telling.

“I decided,” Torin said, and his hand came down flat on the table. “I acted.” A second gesture, a sweep that took in the whole room. “I bore the cost.”

The cadence hit Ben in the chest like a hand against a locked door.

*I decided. I acted. I bore the cost.*

It was the shape under the story, not the words themselves. The skeleton beneath the warmth. Every beat of Torin’s narrative traced the same arc—the choice framed as solitary and necessary, the action framed as clean and complete, the cost framed as something he’d carried alone so no one else had to. It was the pattern of a man who believed that shouldering the burden himself was the same thing as fixing it.

Ben had heard that pattern before. In a voice he hadn’t heard in years, in a room he hadn’t stood in since he was a teenager, from a man who had smiled the same way Torin was smiling now—warm, certain, convinced the hard choice belonged to him alone.

The caf cup was cold in Ben’s hands. He set it down. Careful. Deliberate. The way you set something down when you didn’t trust your fingers not to betray you. His breathing stayed even. His posture didn’t change. But something inside him had tightened around a shape with a name, and the name sat behind his teeth like a word he had no right to say.

Torin kept talking. The story had moved into the aftermath now—the wounded he’d carried, the line he’d held, the cost he’d borne without complaint. His voice stayed warm. It always stayed warm. That was what made the pattern harder to hold in place even as it became impossible to ignore. Jacen’s warmth had gone thin as the war went on, had turned polished and distant, light seen through duraglass. Torin’s warmth was still full. Still present. Still the thing that made the room feel like a team.

Then, mid-sentence, Torin reached across the table and picked up the caf pot.

He didn’t hurry. He didn’t look at Sera. He didn’t break stride in the story. His arm stretched across the table, found the pot, and poured into her cup before she could reach for it herself. Steam curled up in the cool air of the common area. Sera took the cup with a small Dantari inclination of her head—automatic, almost unconscious—and the moment passed without a word.

Torin kept talking.

Ben couldn’t move.

The gesture was nothing. That was the infuriating part. The smallest kindness in the galaxy—a man pouring caf for a woman who had been his comrade a thousand years ago and was his comrade still. It should have vanished the second it happened. It should have been the kind of detail Ben would note and forget, the way he noted everything else in the room, the way he’d been trained to read a space without pinning meaning to any one object.

But it didn’t vanish. It stood there and refused to be filed away.

Because the pattern Ben had just recognized—the solitary decision, the action taken alone, the cost borne personally—didn’t have room for a man who reached across a table and filled someone else’s cup before she asked. The pattern was Jacen’s pattern. And Jacen had never done that. Not once. Not in all the years Ben had known him, before the fall or during it.

Ben met Desh’s eyes across the common area.

Desh was still leaning against the galley counter. His arms were still folded. His face still held that steady calm he wore through every crisis since Terminus Station. But his eyes stayed on Ben’s, and Ben knew—with the certainty of a bond forged in a place neither of them talked about—that Desh had seen the same thing. That Desh had been tracking the same pattern. That the only question left was which of them would say it first.

One of them thought it. One of them said it.

“Like Jacen.”

Barely audible. Two words dropped into the space between them, too quiet for Torin to catch over his own voice. Ben wasn’t sure whether he’d said it or Desh had. It didn’t matter. The name was in the room now, spoken once, and it would stay there.

Torin laughed at something he’d just said. The laugh was warm. The laugh was perfect. Sera cradled the second cup in both hands, fingers wrapped around the ceramic, and her face held the same disciplined stillness it always did when Torin was talking—listening, measuring, giving nothing away.

Kael sat in the corner, silent, watching the room the way he always watched a room. He hadn’t spoken since the story began. His eyes moved from Torin to Ben to Desh and back again, and his expression stayed as dry and detached as it had since the fortress. Ben couldn’t read him. He never could.

The story went on. Torin’s voice rolled through the common area, warm and easy and perfectly timed, and Ben sat in the corner with a name he now had and a gesture he couldn’t place, carrying both of them like weight against his ribs.

## Scene 18.02

Desh didn’t frame it as a warning. He framed it as a debrief.

“Let me walk you through the team status.”

He pulled out his datapad and laid it on the console between them, angling the screen so Ben could read the summary columns without leaning in. The cockpit sat in night-cycle dimness, all instrument lights and blue glow: the nav display turning Veranthos’s system in a slow wireframe spin, the countdown timer Desh had stuck to the bulkhead chewing off seconds in pale blue digits. Ben had been staring at that timer for ten minutes before Desh came in. Every time he looked back, it seemed a little smaller.

“Sera’s holding,” Desh said. “Barely. She’s running on discipline and the fact that nobody’s asked her to make a call she can’t square with her oaths. That window closes the second we hit Veranthos and she has to choose between the seal and the man she spent a thousand years fighting beside.”

Ben’s jaw tightened. He kept quiet.

“Kael’s steadier than he was, which isn’t the same as steady. He’s still watching Torin like a man who knows what a bomb looks like and hasn’t decided whether to say so. He’s also the only person on this ship who’s been inside Veraine’s operational headspace, and he hasn’t volunteered a word of it.”

Desh swiped to the next entry. The gesture looked casual. The precision behind it did not.

“Torin’s the problem you already know. He’s not going to break before Veranthos. He’s going to do exactly what he thinks is right, and he’s going to do it alone, and he’s going to call it necessity. You’ve seen the pattern. You named it.”

Ben kept his hands flat on his thighs. He didn’t look at the datapad. He looked at the timer on the bulkhead instead.

“And you,” Desh said.

He set the datapad down and tapped the bulkhead timer without looking at it, a familiar motion so clean it looked like habit.

“You’re carrying this like it’s your weight to hold and nobody else’s. You’re making calls in your head before anyone else gets a say. You’re curating reality to manage the outcome you want.”

His voice stayed steady. Professional. The voice of a man delivering a threat assessment the way he’d delivered a hundred threat assessments in a career Ben had shared only the worst parts of.

Then it changed.

“You know who else did that.”

Not a question. Desh’s eyes held Ben’s, and the space between them filled with everything neither of them had said since Terminus Station.

Ben’s breath caught.

The name wasn’t in the room. It didn’t need to be. It had been there since the common area, since the caf cup and the gesture Ben couldn’t quite file away, and Desh had put a finger on it without speaking a syllable. Jacen. Torin. The pattern that started with a warm voice and a solitary decision and ended with a body count Ben had spent years trying not to count.

And himself. The third term in the equation. The part Desh hadn’t said out loud and didn’t need to.

Ben didn’t answer. There was nothing to say. Desh was right, and Desh knew he was right, and the silence between them was full of everything Ben couldn’t throw back at him.

Something shifted at the edge of Ben’s vision.

Sera stood in the cockpit doorway. Her hand had found the frame, fingers curled around the durasteel edge, knuckles pale in the instrument light. Her weight had tipped forward onto the ball of her front foot, then stopped. Settled back.

She had heard Desh’s last line. Ben knew it the way he knew the timer was still running, the way he knew the problem in the Force was still there, tugging at the edge of his senses. She had heard it, and she had started to speak.

The breath came. A small intake, the start of one of Sera’s precise, principled sentences—the kind she would have delivered in Chapter 14 without hesitation, a defense of Torin’s character, a reminder of his sacrifice, a line drawn between the man she’d fought beside and the pattern Desh had just named.

The breath went out again.

No words.

Sera’s hand stayed on the frame. Her face was still—Dantari discipline locking every muscle in place—but her eyes were on the space between Ben and Desh, and something behind them had shifted. Not broken. Shifted. The certainty that had held since the fortress was off by a fraction now, and Ben could see the difference.

He watched her. Desh didn’t turn. He didn’t need to. He’d known she was there the moment she arrived—Ben had caught the tiny adjustment in his posture, the old GAG awareness that never quite shut off—and he’d chosen not to acknowledge it. The line had been for Ben. Sera hearing it was incidental. Or maybe not. Maybe Desh had known exactly who stood in the corridor and said it anyway, because the assessment didn’t change just because someone else was listening.

Sera’s hand lifted from the frame. Her weight shifted back. She turned, and her footsteps moved away down the corridor—measured, not quite steady, the rhythm of someone walking away from a sentence she hadn’t managed to speak.

Neither Ben nor Desh said a word about it.

Desh picked up his datapad and slid it into his jacket. His voice was steady again, but the steadiness had changed—not professional detachment, but the calm of a man who had said what he came to say and wasn’t going to soften it.

“The group needs a reason to stay that isn’t you telling them to. You don’t have that reason yet.”

He paused at the cockpit hatch and didn’t look back.

“Find it.”

The hatch closed behind him.

Ben stayed in the cockpit, alone, the nav display still turning Veranthos’s wireframe in slow silence. The timer kept ticking. The problem in the Force pressed at the cockpit like strain against a hull seal, patient and wrong.

His hand went to his datapad. The Codex file was still bookmarked—the resonance passage he’d flagged and never revisited, the note Gavran had written in a hand that shook. He’d told himself he’d go back to it when he had time. When the crisis eased. When he understood enough to read it properly.

Now he knew he’d been avoiding it. The same way he’d been avoiding the name Desh hadn’t needed to say. The same way Sera had walked away from a sentence she couldn’t finish.

The bookmark blinked on the screen. Waiting.

Ben didn’t open it. Not yet. But his hand stayed on the datapad, and the weight of the knowledge settled into his shoulders—the answer he needed wasn’t in Desh’s assessment or Sera’s silence. It was in the notes he’d been too afraid to read, and the reason he’d been afraid was the same reason Desh had been right.

The corridor was quiet. Sera’s footsteps had faded. Her unsaid line hung in the cockpit air like a note held too long, and Ben sat with it while the nav display turned and the timer kept counting.

## Scene 18.03

Ben slid down the corridor bulkhead until he was sitting on the deck plates, legs stretched across the narrow passage, his back against cold durasteel. He hadn’t planned it. His knees had folded, the wall had caught him, and the deck was cold through his trousers. Fine. For once, that was fine.

The ship sat in night-cycle quiet around him. Its hum lay just under hearing, the *Steady Returns’* small-ship heartbeat, steady enough to vanish until something broke it. Nothing had broken it. The nav display in the cockpit still turned Veranthos in its slow wireframe loop. Desh’s timer kept eating seconds off the bulkhead. Torin was three compartments away, asleep or pretending. And Sera’s unfinished sentence still hung in the cockpit air where she’d left it.

Ben let his head settle against the bulkhead. The vibration moved through his skull, a low, constant thrum from the engines doing their work without caring about cockpit arguments or unfinished talks. His shoulders ached. The back of his neck had been tight for days, and he’d stopped noticing it until the deck’s cold gave him something else to feel. His body had been waiting for permission to stop. He hadn’t given it. It had taken the permission anyway.

The datapad was in his hand before he decided to pick it up. Muscle memory. The Codex file was still bookmarked—the resonance passage he’d flagged weeks ago and not come back to, the note Gavran had written in the margin in a hand that wobbled. He’d told himself he’d return to it when he had time. When the crisis let up. When he knew enough to read it right.

He’d been avoiding it. The same way he’d been avoiding the name Desh hadn’t needed to say. The same way Sera had walked away from a sentence she couldn’t finish.

The screen glowed dimly in the corridor. Ben scrolled to the bookmark. The words were familiar—he’d read them too many times for them to be anything else—but familiarity wasn’t understanding. He’d been reading the note the way he’d been reading everything else on this trip: as proof of a pattern he already believed in.

*We lacked the will or the trust.*

Gavran’s hand had shaken when he wrote it. Ben had always read that as grief. Regret. A dying man admitting failure. *We weren’t brave enough.* That was how it had landed every time. The Jedi Lords had the technique, the knowledge, the vergence mapped, the seal designed, the anchors in place—and at the last moment, they hadn’t had the nerve to do what needed doing. So Gavran had done it alone. Carried the burden alone. Died alone.

That was the pattern Ben had been pulling out of the note. The pattern he’d been living inside without naming.

He looked at the sentence again.

*We lacked the will or the trust.*

Or.

Not *and*. Not one failure of courage. Two separate problems, set side by side like entries on a manifest. *Will* was one thing—the willingness to act, to pay the price, to do what needed doing. Gavran had had will. He’d died at his post. He’d held the seal alone for years after the others were gone.

*Trust* was different.

*We couldn’t trust each other enough.*

The emphasis shifted under his eyes. Not a confession of cowardice. A diagnosis. Gavran hadn’t been saying *we weren’t brave enough.* He’d been saying *we couldn’t work together.* The Jedi Lords had the knowledge and the technique and the vergence mapped, and when they’d needed to act as one, they hadn’t been able to. So one man had done it alone. The seal had held—for a thousand years, it had held—but it had held as a solitary thing, a weight carried by one person and then by twelve prisoners who’d never agreed to carry it, and now it was failing because solitary things always failed in the end.

Ben’s hand stilled on the datapad.

The trust problem was the one he’d been avoiding. Not *am I worthy enough.* Not *can I carry this alone.* Those were the questions he knew how to ask because they were the questions he knew how to answer. The real one was harder. *Can I build trust with the people I’m working with?* Can Sera trust him enough to finish the sentence she’d walked away from. Can Kael trust him enough to say what he knew about Veraine. Can Torin—

Torin was a different problem. Torin was the problem Desh had named without naming, the pattern Ben had recognized and couldn’t un-recognize. But Torin was also someone Ben had been treating as a threat to manage instead of an ally to reach. And that was the trap. Exactly the trap.

His mother’s voice came back to him. Not a Force-ghost. Not a visitation. Just the remembered shape of the way she would have said it, practical and stripped down to what mattered: *Stop wondering if you’re worthy and figure out what works.*

One short sentence. Move.

Ben had been a teenager when she’d said it—something about a training exercise he’d overthought, a problem he’d circled instead of solving. She’d had no patience for self-reproach. Self-reproach was only another way of making the problem about himself instead of about the work.

The memory tightened something in his chest. Not pain. Not the old grief that sat in the background of everything. Something closer to focus. His mother had been a pragmatist. She solved problems by looking at what was actually in front of her and doing the thing that worked, not the thing that proved she deserved her name.

Ben had been trying to prove something since Terminus Station. Maybe longer. Maybe since Jacen.

The lightsaber on his hip pressed against his thigh—a familiar weight, solid and unremarkable. He didn’t reach for it. He just let it be there, something real to brace against while the question changed shape in his head.

*We lacked the will or the trust.*

The will he had. He’d had it since the fortress, since the moment he’d decided not to call Luke. The trust was the piece he hadn’t figured out. The one he’d been reading as a failure of courage when it was really a failure of something else.

Different emphasis. Different problem.

Maybe a different solution.

He didn’t know what that solution looked like. He didn’t have a plan. The exhaustion was still there, settled into his shoulders and the back of his neck, and the timer in the cockpit was still counting down, and Veraine was still somewhere ahead of them, and Torin was still three compartments away being a problem Ben didn’t know how to solve. None of that had changed.

But he’d moved one file from the archive stack to the open one. The resonance note wasn’t a confession of failure anymore. It was a diagnosis. And a diagnosis was something he could work with.

Ben closed the datapad. The corridor stayed quiet. The ship’s hum stayed steady. The cold of the deck plates still pressed through his trousers, and his legs were still stretched across the narrow passage, and he was still tired in a way that had stopped being about sleep days ago.

He didn’t get up. Not yet. But his hand stayed on the datapad, the weight of the lightsaber stayed against his thigh, and the question stayed open.
