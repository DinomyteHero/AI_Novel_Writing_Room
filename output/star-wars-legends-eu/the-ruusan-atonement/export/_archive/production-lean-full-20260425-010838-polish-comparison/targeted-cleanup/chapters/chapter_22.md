# Chapter 22

## Scene 22.01

The datapads sprawled across the camp table like a sabacc hand nobody had bothered to collect.

Ben had been at it for three hours. The chrono on his wrist said so, but the number had stopped meaning much once Torin had walked into the Scar’s bad light. Time on Veranthos had gone strange after that. Ben trusted the ache behind his eyes more than the display, and the stiffness in his lower back from hunching over Gavran’s marginalia. He trusted, too, the way Sera’s handwriting had started turning up on his datapad without either of them bothering to say that was all right.

She worked across from him, her side of the table as crowded as his: three screens, three traditions, one problem refusing to sit still. Kael’s framework notes on her left. Gavran’s resonance theory in the center. Her own technique-lineage fragments on the right, the archaic NSW notation Ben had spent the first hour squinting at, the second hour learning to read, and the third hour treating like a language he should have known from the start.

She hadn’t asked permission. Two hours ago she had simply reached over, dragged his primary datapad closer, and started annotating Ankhural’s fragment in her own hand. Her script flowed beside his modern Force analysis—her angular pale-blue strokes beside his white standard characters, two disciplines sharing the same screen without grinding each other flat.

Ben watched her for a few seconds.

Three. Four.

She didn’t look up.

“The third harmonic won’t hold,” she said. Her stylus tapped the screen once. “Not if we treat it as a single anchor point. The NSW framework assumes a central keystone—one practitioner carrying the seal’s shape while the others reinforce it. Ankhural’s fragment doesn’t use a keystone. It spreads.”

“I saw that.” Ben pulled the datapad closer and angled it so they could both read. “The spread is the point. No single tradition dominates the containment. The resonance comes from the overlap, not from anybody holding the center.”

“Then the NSW notation is wrong for this.”

The words hung between them.

Ben looked at her. The blue glow from the datapad left her face hard to read, but her hand had gone still. The stylus hovered over the screen as if she’d reached the edge of a ledge and was deciding whether to step off.

“The notation isn’t wrong,” Ben said. “It’s built for a different shape. The question is whether you can bend it.”

Sera’s jaw tightened. Not anger. The look Ben had learned to read as thinking. She was weighing something, testing it, deciding whether it would hold.

“The NSW discipline,” she said at last, “was made to contain breaches. Single-point failure. One practitioner anchors the seal. The others support. It works because someone stays in the center and does not let go.” Her eyes met his. “Ankhural’s fragment has no center. If I force one onto it—if I make the keystone model fit a distributed structure—the resonance will fail. The traditions will fight instead of reinforcing each other.”

“Can you work it on its own terms?”

The question settled between them.

Sera lowered her gaze to the datapad. To her handwriting beside his. To the fragment she’d been circling for two hours, trying to press it into a framework that had kept her alive through a war and a millennium of stasis and a waking nightmare she still hadn’t named aloud.

She set the stylus down.

Then she picked it up again and wrote.

Ben watched the change happen. Not a translation. An adaptation. She wasn’t forcing Ankhural’s fragment into NSW notation anymore. She was letting the fragment’s logic set the terms, building a new notation around it that borrowed from her discipline without belonging to it. Her handwriting changed as she worked. The angular precision stayed—Sera would never stop being precise—but the rhythm shifted. Faster. Less guarded. The strokes came in bursts, as if she’d stopped checking each line against some internal standard and was simply following the shape of the answer.

Something in Ben’s chest eased. He didn’t put a name to it. He didn’t need one.

He pulled up the seal-pressure readings Desh had been logging since they made camp and started cross-referencing them against the resonance requirements Sera was sketching. The numbers were ugly. The power draw alone would strain every portable generator they’d brought, and if the containment had to hold longer than a few minutes, they’d need to route through the *Steady Returns*’ reactor by way of a relay Desh hadn’t built yet.

“Desh.”

Desh looked up from his own datapad. He’d been on the supply manifests for the last hour, his face flat in the way of a man who’d done this math before and disliked where it led.

“Tell me we have enough.”

“We don’t.” Desh turned the datapad so Ben could see the figures. “Portable generators get you maybe twelve minutes at the draw you’re projecting. After that, you’re living off whatever’s left in the cells, and the cells weren’t built for sustained resonance output. They’ll overheat. You lose containment.”

“Can we route through the ship?”

“Through a relay, yeah. I’d have to build it. Six hours, maybe eight. The parts are there—barely—but it won’t be pretty. If the relay drops mid-containment, you lose everything.”

Ben took that in. Twelve minutes of portable power. A relay that might fail. A resonance structure that needed three traditions and had never been tested outside Ankhural’s theory.

“The relay’s the right call,” he said. “Build it.”

Desh nodded once and went back to his manifest. No argument. No hesitation. Just the clipped efficiency of someone who’d spent years making impossible logistics work on timelines that didn’t care what he thought.

Sera finished her notation and pushed the datapad across the table.

Ben looked down.

The resonance structure was there—not as theory, not as a fragment, but as a plan. Three anchor points, spread out. Modern Jedi perception at one vertex, NSW discipline at another, Sith-trained precision at the third. The overlap would build a containment field no single tradition could make alone. The seal would hold because nobody was trying to dominate it.

It was beautiful.

It was also impossible without Kael.

Ben felt the weight of that settle into his chest. Not an idea, but a pressure under the ribs that made the next breath harder than the last. The resonance needed three traditions. They had two. Sera’s willingness to adapt had turned a theoretical fragment into something they could use, but the plan had a condition, and the condition was a man who had spent a thousand years as a tool and had no reason to trust anyone asking him to be one again.

Without Kael, there was no resonance.

Without resonance, there was only Torin’s sacrifice—the Battle Master going into the wound alone, holding the seal with his body the way Gavran had, the way the Jedi Lords had, the way Ben had been trying to since the first bad note in the Darrpa Sector. Or Veraine’s destruction—the wound opening on her terms, the transition casualties she’d already counted and accepted, the seal breaking because a Sith alchemist had decided the arithmetic justified the price.

Ben kept both possibilities in mind at once. The plan that worked. The plan that failed. The distance between them was Kael’s choice, and Kael’s choice wasn’t something Ben could control.

He looked at Sera. She was watching him, hands still on the datapad, her face unreadable.

“It works,” he said.

“I know.”

“Kael—”

“I know.” She held his gaze. “Ask him, Ben.”

The name landed cleanly. Not Skywalker. Ben. A small shift, almost nothing, but it was there. She’d said it the way she might have said Desh’s or Torin’s, like the name belonged to someone she trusted.

Ben didn’t comment. Neither did she.

The Scar’s light touched the edge of the camp through the tent flap, that vertical seam of warped dawn still bleeding its bad color into the valley below. The seal was still failing. Veraine was still moving. Torin was still walking toward something Ben couldn’t stop.

But the plan was real. The datapad on the table held an answer that hadn’t existed three hours ago, built by two people who had stopped working alone.

Ben stood.

“Desh. The relay.”

“Already on it.”

“I need to find Kael.”

Desh looked up from his manifest. His face stayed flat in the way Ben had learned to read anyway—the stillness of a man who knew exactly what hung on the next conversation and wasn’t going to say it out loud.

“Good luck,” Desh said.

Ben headed for the tent flap. The plan worked in theory. It required three Force traditions. They had two who were willing.

Kael was the variable.

## Scene 22.02

The kilometer to the ridge passed in silence.

Not the silence of people with nothing to say. The silence of people who had already said it back at the camp table, with datapads spread between them, and were now walking toward the thing that would make the plan real or break it.

Ben felt the folded Codex notes in his jacket pocket, a small pressure against his ribs, pointless now. The intellectual work was done. Sera had rewritten the notation. Desh had run the power calculations. The resonance structure sat on the datapad, three anchor points spread across traditions that had spent a thousand years trying to kill each other.

Now came the physical part.

Sera’s stride matched Desh’s on ground neither of them had grown up on. She moved like someone who had learned to walk where the terrain wanted you dead and never lost the habit. Desh moved like someone who had spent years in places where a wrong step got you noticed, and getting noticed got you dead. Different wars. Same economy of motion.

The mast rose ahead of them, taller than Ben had expected from the schematic. The load-distribution antennas curved out from the central column in a pattern that didn’t match any manufacturer’s standard loadout. Hand-shaped, not stamped. Someone had stood here and bent metal to a purpose that had nothing to do with efficiency and everything to do with amplification geometry.

Sera stopped ten meters from the base.

Ben stopped with her. Desh peeled off toward the clearing’s edge without being asked, datapad already out, approach telemetry overlaid on the array schematic. Three component locations lit up as his fingers traced them. He didn’t look up.

Sera circled the mast once. Slow. Her eyes moved over the base plate, the assembly marks, the salvage labels half scraped away. Ash-white tally lines at the bottom edge—Pira Kul’s pressure-cut neatness, Vess Oran’s salvage tags, the crew’s handwriting all over the lower third of the structure.

She didn’t pause for any of it.

Her hand found the base plate at a point two-thirds up from the ground, where the harmonic geometry was subtle enough that Ben had to look twice to catch it. Not the crew’s work. The design logic underneath the assembly. The thing that had decided where every component would sit before anyone touched the metal.

She laid her palm flat against it and closed her eyes.

Ben watched her face.

The change was small. A slight shift at the corner of her mouth. The skin around her eyes tightening, then easing. Recognition settling into certainty the way a key settled home in a lock.

She exhaled, a small Dantari breath, the end of the reading.

“This is her mind.”

She didn’t open her eyes. Didn’t add anything. The four words landed and stayed there.

Ben felt them hit his chest. Not fear. The weight of understanding that Sera had just named something that couldn’t be unnamed. Veraine’s authorship of the array was no longer a deduction, a tactical probability, a thing they had reason to believe. It was there. In the metal. In the curved antennas. In the geometry Sera had read with her palm flat against the base plate while the ridge wind tugged at her sleeves.

He didn’t answer. The statement didn’t ask for one. Sera had read something he couldn’t read, and she had delivered it without softening it. The isolation of her expertise stood plain in the space between her hand and the metal, in the fact that she still hadn’t opened her eyes.

Desh straightened from his datapad.

Ben turned. Desh’s expression had gone clinical—the shift from observer to actor that Ben had seen before, in other places, under other pressures. The moment when someone stopped waiting and started moving. His posture had changed too. Shoulders set. Weight balanced. The datapad held at his side like a tool he was done with.

“That’s my job.”

Ben opened his mouth.

Desh cut him off. “Let the Force-users do the Force thing. I’ll handle the part that requires hands.”

The words came matter-of-factly. Not heroic. Not grim. The tone of a man who had done the math and made the choice before anyone asked him to, and was now simply reporting the result.

Ben looked at him. The three component locations on the datapad. The approach telemetry. The relay Desh was supposed to build back at camp—six hours, maybe eight, the parts barely there. And now this. The sabotage. Alone. Against armed opposition. The most exposed task in a plan that depended on both parts working.

Desh met his eyes. The clinical expression wasn’t coldness. It was focus. The same focus Ben had seen in the *Steady Returns*’ cockpit when Desh had mapped the bearing that pointed toward uncharted space. The same focus in the fortress control room when Desh had read the containment network display and understood what nine dead nodes meant before Ben finished explaining it.

He’d already calculated the cost.

Ben didn’t argue. Desh had volunteered before anyone asked, which meant the choice was his. Arguing would have been for Ben’s comfort, not Desh’s. And Ben had spent enough of his life letting other people protect him from the weight of their choices.

“All right,” Ben said.

Desh nodded once and looked past him, toward the mast. Toward Sera, who had opened her eyes and was watching them both with her hand still flat against the base plate.

Nobody commented on her four words. Nobody needed to.

The ridge wind picked up, pulling at the antennas with a sound like breath across a bottle’s mouth. Below them, the valley stretched toward the Scar—that vertical seam of bad light still bleeding its wrong color into the sky. The seal was still failing. Veraine was still moving. Torin was still walking toward something Ben couldn’t stop.

But the plan had two parts now. The resonance trio needed Kael. The sabotage needed Desh alone against armed opposition. Both plans failed without both parts.

Sera lifted her hand from the base plate. The metal where her palm had rested was warm—Ben could see the faint condensation fading at its edge. She didn’t look at it. She turned and started back toward camp, her stride matching the ground the way it had on the walk out.

Desh fell in beside her. Ben followed.

The silence on the return walk wasn’t uncomfortable. It was the silence of three people who had just made choices that could not be unmade, carrying the weight of them without comment because the weight was the point.

Sera’s hand on the mast base still echoed in Ben’s chest. The warmth of her palm on the metal. The small exhalation when the design signature resolved. The four words she had delivered without opening her eyes.

This is her mind.

Two plans, both dependent on trust. The resonance trio needed Kael. The sabotage needed Desh alone against armed opposition. Both plans failed without both parts.

Ben walked. The Codex notes in his pocket pressed against his ribs, pointless now. The intellectual work was done.

## Scene 22.03

Kael sat at the camp’s edge with his legs dangling over the ridgeline, facing the valley and the Scar below.

He didn’t turn when Ben approached. He’d felt him coming—the way anyone with Force-sense felt another presence in a place this thin, where the wound leaked through everything and made distance between minds shorter than it should have been. His shoulders were loose. His hands rested on his thighs. He looked like a man who’d been sitting long enough for the cold to work into his joints and had decided he didn’t care.

Ben stopped three paces back. First light touched the far ridgelines now, gold on gray stone, while the valley floor stayed pooled in shadow. The Scar cut through it like a seam of bad color—not quite red, not quite violet, the sort of wrongness that made his eyes want to slide away. It pressed at the edges of his Force-sense, a low throb he’d learned to ignore and had learned, just as quickly, that ignoring was still a choice.

His instinct was to speak. To fill the space between them with the plan, with urgency, with the weight of what had been building since the mast and Sera’s palm flat on the metal and Desh’s matter-of-fact volunteer. The impulse rose in his chest—explain, persuade, arrange the next few minutes so they could only end one way. He saw, with abrupt clarity, how easy that would be. A trimmed version of the truth. The right emphasis. Kael slotted where Ben needed him, one more hidden brace under a structure only Ben could see.

Jacen would have known how to do that.

Ben let the thought burn off and took one slow breath. Not that. Not here.

He walked the last three paces, sat down beside Kael at arm’s length, and let his legs hang over the ridge. His hands settled on his knees. Stone chilled through his trousers. Below them, the Scar gave one slow, ugly pulse that made something behind Ben’s eyes tilt, then steadied again.

Kael said nothing.

Ben let the silence stand. Thirty seconds. Maybe longer. The place made everything heavier than it should have been, but he’d learned to carry weight he hadn’t chosen. The first light kept creeping down the far ridge. Somewhere back in camp, Sera would be checking the notation one last time. Desh would be going over his equipment. Torin was gone—walking toward something Ben couldn’t stop and couldn’t follow. The plan had three commitments and one open question.

Kael was the open question.

“There’s a resonance structure,” Ben said. “Three anchor points. Three traditions. The Codex called it Weave-Resonance—Gavran’s note said Ankhural thought it could strengthen the seal by spreading the load. They never tried it. They didn’t trust each other enough.”

He kept his eyes on the valley, on the Scar’s slow bleed of light, and laid it out the way he had for Sera and Desh at the camp table. The theory. The requirements. The risks—and there were plenty of them, starting with the fact that no one had ever done this before and ending with Veraine trying to stop them. His own role: the Skywalker anchor, the Jedi component, the inheritance he hadn’t asked for and couldn’t refuse. Sera’s role. Desh’s role—alone at the array, exposed in a plan that depended on both parts holding.

And then Kael’s.

“You’d be the third anchor,” Ben said. “Sith-trained. Brotherhood methodology. The resonance needs three traditions because the seal was built to hold all of them—Jedi, Sith, and the ones that didn’t fit either category. Without the third anchor, the spread collapses. The seal fails. Veraine wins.”

He stopped. The words had come out flat and precise, like a mission brief stripped down to the bone. He hadn’t planned the flatness. It was simply what happened when you told someone the truth and didn’t leave anything out—no spin, no pressure, no clever scaffolding built to make the answer you wanted feel like the only one on the table.

The ask was structural. The content was the persuasion. If Kael said no, the plan failed, and Ben would have to find another way or watch the seal come apart.

He didn’t say that. Kael already knew it.

The silence stretched. Kael’s Force presence stayed guarded—not hostile, never hostile, but closed in a way Ben had learned to read over the weeks since the fortress. The wound made it harder to read than it should have been, smearing the edges of perception the way heat haze blurred a horizon. He could feel Kael thinking. He couldn’t feel what Kael was thinking about.

His breath steadied. He waited.

Somewhere below, a bird called—thin and reedy in the thin air, carrying too long, too hollow. The Scar pulsed again. Ben’s stomach lurched for half a second, then settled.

Kael’s fingers moved.

It was a small thing. His right hand, resting on his thigh, shifted to the grass beside him. His fingers closed around a few blades and pulled—not hard, not angry, just the deliberate motion of a man turning something over in his head. He did it again. A third time. Small green tufts came up, roots and dark soil clinging together.

Ben watched without really watching. His peripheral vision caught the rhythm—pull, pause, pull, pause—and he knew what it meant. Kael wasn’t listening to the plan anymore. He’d stopped listening somewhere around the third sentence and started weighing the person offering it.

The shift was tangible. Ben felt it the way he’d learned to feel a room full of people who didn’t trust each other—a subtle change in pressure, the moment when the decision stopped being about what was being asked and became about who was asking. His chest tightened. Not fear. The knowledge that he’d done everything he could do, and the rest wasn’t his to control.

He didn’t fill the silence.

Thirty seconds. Maybe a minute. Light spread farther down the ridge, picking out the valley contours, the dark line of trees, the silver thread of a stream Ben hadn’t noticed before. The wound pressed at the edges of everything, making the scene feel slightly off, like a holorecording played a hair too slow. His hands stayed on his knees. His breath stayed even.

Kael pulled another blade of grass.

Then he turned his head. Not all the way toward Ben—just enough to acknowledge him, enough to show the line of his profile against the lightening sky. His expression was unreadable. His Force presence stayed shut down, but something in the set of his shoulders had changed—a loosening, maybe, or a settling. The difference between a man bracing for a blow and a man deciding the blow was worth taking.

His question came without preamble.

“Why should I believe this one is different?”
