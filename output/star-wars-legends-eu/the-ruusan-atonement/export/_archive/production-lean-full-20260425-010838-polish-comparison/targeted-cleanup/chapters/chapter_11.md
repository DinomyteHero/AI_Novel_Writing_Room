# Chapter 11

## Scene 11.01

Desh set his datapad on the galley table with the screen angled outward so everyone could see the tally column. It had grown long enough to need scrolling.

“That’s fourteen,” he said.

Sera broke off mid-sentence. Her hand stayed on the caf dispenser, which had been the center of the argument for the last four minutes. “Fourteen what?”

“Arguments since we broke atmo on WR-7741.” Desh tapped the screen. “I’m counting the one about the nav-computer’s accent as half. That was mostly agreement.”

Torin leaned back against the bulkhead, arms folded, the corner of his mouth lifting. “You’re keeping a ledger of our disagreements.”

“Someone should. For the record.”

“The record of what?” Sera’s voice had the edge of someone who had been right about the galley rotation and knew it. “The record of a ship built for a pilot and a passenger carrying five people and their opinions?”

“Six,” Kael said from the corner.

Everyone looked at him.

He didn’t look up from the ration bar he was breaking into pieces. “Six people. You counted the pilot and the passenger separately. That’s two. Then you said five people and their opinions. That’s seven. Unless the opinions are people.”

Desh snorted. “Fourteen and a half.”

Ben stood near the cockpit door with one shoulder against the frame, a half-finished cup of caf cooling in his hand. The air had gone stale three days ago and wasn’t getting any better. The scrubbers were rated for two occupants. They’d been running hard since Fondor, and the smell sat in the back of his throat every time he breathed in.

It made him restless. He wanted to move, to do something, anything, before the friction caught. He’d spent the morning running diagnostics that didn’t need running, checking the nav-computer’s course projection three times, then standing in the cockpit doorway because there wasn’t room to stand anywhere else.

Sera set the caf dispenser down with a click. “The rotation was established on departure. I took the second watch. It is Torin’s turn.”

“I made caf this morning,” Torin said.

“That is not the rotation.”

“It’s caf, Sera. The water’s recycled. The grounds are the same grounds we’ve been using for four days. The rotation is theoretical at this point.”

“The rotation is the principle.”

Desh made another mark on the datapad.

Ben pushed off the doorframe and crossed to the table. The motion came on instinct, the need to get between them before the argument sharpened. “Torin, take the watch. Sera, the caf’s yours tomorrow. Desh, stop keeping score.”

“Fifteen,” Desh said.

“That wasn’t an argument.”

“You sounded argumentative.”

Kael laughed.

It was short. One rough exhale that caught in his throat and got out before he could stop it, genuine and almost instantly gone. His head stayed down. His hands stayed on the ration bar. The sound had already done its work.

Ben felt the room change before he could name it. The air didn’t get any fresher — the scrubbers still labored, the stale smell still clung to everything — but the mood shifted. It was as if a hatch had cracked and light had slipped through before anyone decided to move toward it.

No one commented. No one turned. But every conversation on the ship had stopped, and none of them started again right away.

Kael finished breaking the ration bar and ate a piece.

Desh set the datapad down facedown. The motion was casual enough to pass, but Ben saw it. The tally was done for now. Kael’s laugh had done what the count couldn’t — reset the room without making anyone lose.

Torin straightened from the bulkhead. “There was a pilot in the NSW,” he said, and the story came on its beat the way his stories always did, warm and easy, filling the silence before it could open into a gap. “Flew a rusted-out Aurek that should have been decommissioned before the war started. She kept a log of every argument her crew had. Called it the Grievance Index.”

“That’s a better name,” Desh said.

“She said it was the only way to settle disputes on a long haul. Whoever had the higher index score at the end of the run bought the first round.”

Sera turned from the dispenser. “Did it work?”

“Until the run where they all had the same score. They spent three hours arguing about who owed who and missed their departure window.” Torin’s smile landed right where it was supposed to. “The squadron commander made her delete the file.”

The galley eased. Kael had finished the ration bar and was wiping his fingers on his trousers, still not looking at anyone, but some of the tightness had gone out of his shoulders. Ben noticed because he was watching for it — not thinking about it, just filing it away with the smell of the ship, the whine of the scrubbers, and the half-beat lag in the Force that never quite went away.

The conversation drifted. The technique came up — Sera said something about the seal’s architecture, and Desh asked a question that was half practical and half needling, and the room opened around the old argument the way it always did. Ben stayed by the table, listening with half his attention while the other half tracked Kael in the corner.

An opening surfaced.

Torin was mid-sentence, talking about the lattice and the way the technique had felt when Gavran first demonstrated it — not the wound, but the correction, the moment before the damage set in. The argument had bent toward the institutional question: whether the technique was wrong in itself or wrong in its use, whether the Jedi Lords’ mistake lay in the tool or in the hand that used it. Kael’s head had come up. Ben could see the shift in him, the slight stillness that said he was listening closely, the way he treated arguments about Jedi authority like a sabacc player watching a dealer’s hands.

Torin could have pulled him in. One question. One invitation to answer. The cost would have been almost nothing — Kael was already half-engaged, already leaning toward the critique, and Torin’s warmth was the right key for that door.

Torin didn’t take it.

He finished his sentence and turned to Sera instead, redirecting the conversation with a question about the seal’s original anchor configuration. The opening shut. The room moved with him, smooth and clean and just a little too perfect, the way a musician lands a note that is correct and somehow late.

Kael’s eyes flicked to Torin. Once. Then back to Desh.

Ben took it in and kept going. The eye-flick and the pivot and the timing of Torin’s warmth landing a touch too neatly all went into the file he was keeping, the one that still didn’t have a name. Torin had passed up a free pitch. Ben didn’t know why. He didn’t try to solve it. The ambiguity stayed where it belonged: noted, not examined, something he’d turn over later when the ship wasn’t pressing in on him and the scrubbers weren’t whining and Kael’s laugh wasn’t still hanging in the back of his mind.

He pushed away from the table and headed for the cockpit. The caf in his hand had gone cold.

The cockpit was quiet. Hyperspace streamed past the viewport in its usual blue-white smear, steady and indifferent. Ben dropped into the pilot’s seat and left the door open behind him. The nav-computer’s course projection glowed on the console. No deviations. Fourteen hours to Maetha.

He heard Desh’s footsteps before the door shut.

Desh dropped into the copilot’s seat with the same easy looseness of a man waiting for the room to clear. He didn’t look at Ben. He looked at the console, then the viewport, then his hands.

The cockpit door closed behind him.

Ben waited. The silence stretched. Desh’s hands stayed on his knees, still in a way that wasn’t like him — Desh usually had to be doing something, checking a setting, tapping a panel, keeping his fingers busy the way operatives did when they were thinking.

“Can we talk about something that isn’t the Force?”

His voice was too casual. The words came light, but the weight was underneath them, and Ben recognized it. Desh had been building up to this. The tally on the datapad, the joke about the Grievance Index, the careful way he’d turned the screen facedown after Kael laughed — all of it had been marking time until the cockpit door closed.

Ben turned in his seat to face him. “Yeah,” he said. “We can do that.”

## Scene 11.02

Desh didn’t start with Jacen.

He started with a raid they both remembered — a day with too much shouting, one arrest gone bad, and a face Ben still saw when sleep slipped sideways. Desh kept both hands on the flight controls while he talked, steady as always, nudging trim he didn’t need to nudge.

“The apartment block on Level 87,” Desh said. “The one with the blue stairwell doors. You remember those?”

Ben did. He remembered the doors because he’d spent forty minutes staring at one while the breach team stacked up, waiting for a signal that never came. The paint had been peeling around the handle. Someone had scratched a name into it — he couldn’t remember the name, only that his eyes had kept snagging on it while his heart hammered too fast and his blaster stayed cold in his hand.

“Yeah,” Ben said. “I remember.”

“The arrest was supposed to be clean. Intel said the target would be alone. Intel was wrong.” Desh’s voice stayed level, not cold — just flat, the way he might have read a mission summary into a recorder. “There was a family in the back room. The target’s sister and her kid. Maybe four years old.”

Ben’s jaw tightened. He knew where this was going. He’d replayed that day often enough to know each beat before Desh named it — the sister screaming, the kid crying, the target reaching for something that turned out not to be a weapon, the shot that went off anyway because the breach team had been nineteen and scared and told the man was dangerous.

“I know what happened,” Ben said. “I was there.”

“I know you were there. That’s why I’m telling you.”

Ben’s first instinct was motion. Stand up. Pace the narrow strip of cockpit floor. Do anything besides sit here and let the words settle. The cockpit was too small for pacing. He stayed put and let the tension drive down into his shoulders instead.

Desh glanced over, then back to the console. “The kid’s face. You still see it.”

It wasn’t a question.

“Sometimes,” Ben said. The word came out flatter than he meant. “Less than I used to.”

“I see it every time someone tells me a story that’s too clean.” Desh’s hands made a small adjustment on the controls, unnecessary but precise. “Every time someone lands a beat exactly where it’s supposed to land, like a good version of a bad thing.”

The cockpit went quiet.

Ben felt the shift before his mind caught up. His stomach dropped — that sick, hollow lurch when the floor gives way under a step that should have been there. Desh hadn’t said the name yet. He didn’t have to. The shape of the sentence was enough.

“Torin,” Ben said.

“Yeah.”

“You’re saying Torin sounds like—”

“The good version of him.” Desh kept his voice even, but careful had crept into it. “The version who tells stories instead of giving orders. The version who makes people want to follow because he’s warm, because he sees them, because he knows exactly what to say and when to say it.”

Ben’s fingers tightened on the armrest. He felt the pressure of his grip and couldn’t make himself ease off. The name was in the cockpit now. Jacen. It had been there since Fondor — since before Fondor, since the first time Desh had answered a comm call he shouldn’t have and agreed to a mission he hadn’t asked enough questions about. They’d carried it in the spaces between words, in the shorthand that let them talk around what they’d both done at sixteen. Now it had a shape.

“You’ve been thinking this for a while,” Ben said.

“Since the galley argument. The story about the Grievance Index.” Desh shook his head once. “It landed too well. Everybody laughed, everybody relaxed, and I thought — I’ve heard this before. Not the story. The timing.”

Ben wanted to push back. The answer rose hot and immediate — that Torin wasn’t Jacen, that the comparison was unfair, that Desh was reaching. But his body had already taken the verdict. His stomach was still down around his boots. His fingers still dug into the armrest. He’d been filing Torin’s warmth-on-a-beat for days without admitting what the file contained.

He didn’t argue.

Desh let the silence sit. He turned his attention to the console, checking the nav-computer’s course projection, the fuel readouts, the comm panel. His hands moved with the same practiced economy he brought to everything, a man who kept his fingers busy while his mind worked through something painful.

The datapad on the console beside him blinked.

Desh looked at it. His hands stopped.

“The reconstruction finished,” he said.

Ben glanced at the datapad. The screen glowed at low brightness, text small and dense, but the shape of the display was plain enough from the copilot’s seat. Desh had been piecing together fragments for days — archive references, Sera’s testimony, the scraps of Brotherhood doctrine Kael had been willing to share. The reconstruction had been running in the background, cross-checking every scrap against the framework they’d built.

It had resolved.

Desh turned the datapad so Ben could see. “The Brotherhood wasn’t trying to destroy the vergence. They were trying to flip its polarity. Corrupt it into a permanent dark-side well — a wound that would feed their adepts and starve everyone else.”

Ben read the display. Cold data scrolled past: the ritual’s structure, the number of participants, the Force draw needed to attempt a Weave-polarity inversion at a vergence the size of Veranthos. The numbers were staggering. The Brotherhood had thrown everything into one working — every adept, every artifact, every scrap of power they could gather.

And Gavran had stopped them.

Not by countering the ritual. By deadening the site before it could finish. He’d used the removal technique — the same technique that had scarred the Force and silenced the vergence — as an emergency cut-off. He’d severed the Brotherhood’s working before the polarity could flip, before the wound could open fully, before the dark-side well could start to feed.

The price was the seal. The price was twelve anchors in stasis. The price was a thousand years of silence.

But the alternative had been worse.

Ben stared at the datapad. The framework settled into place like a weight he’d been carrying without knowing it. The Jedi Lords weren’t corrupt. They hadn’t built the seal out of fear or greed or rot. They’d been competent people under catastrophic pressure who’d made the wrong emergency choice. Gavran had been right about the emergency and catastrophically wrong about the cost.

The silence in the cockpit held both things at once. Jacen’s name, still hanging where Desh had left it. The Brotherhood ritual, resolved into cold data on a dim screen. Two reckonings that belonged together in a way Ben couldn’t have put into words and didn’t need to.

Desh didn’t say anything. He turned the datapad back toward himself, let the glow spill across his face, and stared at readouts he wasn’t really reading.

Ben stared at the console. The nav-computer’s course projection glowed steady. Fourteen hours to Maetha. Amber and green instrument lights painted the cockpit in the dim hush of a ship where everyone else was asleep.

Neither of them said they should watch Torin.

They didn’t need to.

On Desh’s datapad, the ritual reconstruction sat in the quiet — the Jedi Lords turned into tragic peers in the same breath Jacen became a named shape in the room. Ben felt the weight of both settle into his chest and stay there. He was steadier than he’d been when the cockpit door closed. He was not lighter.

The silence held.

## Scene 11.03

Desh adjusted the approach vector and frowned at the board.

This time the sensor display didn’t break the way the others had. No dead-zone dropout. No ragged spike and collapse. The readings climbed in clean increments and held there, then climbed again as the ship ate through the last stretch of approach, as if they were closing on something active enough to notice distance.

“How long?” Ben asked.

Desh glanced at the chrono in the nav strip. “Twelve minutes to visual if I keep this line. Nine if I stop babying the edge.”

“Keep the line.”

Desh gave him a brief look and made another small correction. The numbers steadied, then twitched upward again anyway.

“That’s new,” he said.

Ben leaned forward in the copilot’s seat. The cockpit felt tighter than it had a minute ago, not crowded, just keyed up. Even the instrument glow seemed harsher, every display insisting on itself.

The Force ahead wasn’t thinned.

He’d been braced for that without admitting it. For the familiar sense of footing gone unreliable, of a drop where a floor should be. His body had already set against it, shoulders tight, breath held shallow. When it didn’t come, the absence hit him harder than the thing itself might have.

What waited out there was different.

The boundary shimmered. Not to his eyes — the stars ahead stayed fixed, the planet hanging where the nav said it should — but in the Force, space seemed to waver like a surface deciding whether to tilt. Not broken. Not hollowed out. Leaning.

Watching it made the lean more obvious.

“Readings are stable,” Desh said, in the same tone he used when stable had become suspicious. “No chrono distortion. No gravimetric drift. No structural shear on the perimeter band.”

Ben kept his eyes ahead. “But.”

“But the sensor ghosts are wrong.” Desh tapped a cluster of returns with one finger. “I sweep the same sector twice, I get the same mass and the same coordinates, but the energy profile slides half a degree and settles someplace else. Not random. Patterned.” Another beat. “Like it’s answering the sweep.”

Ben didn’t like the way that sat in his stomach. The practical part of the problem was almost worse than if everything had simply failed. Their instruments were working. That meant the bad news counted.

“How far does the pattern extend?”

“Hard to say until we’re in it.” Desh checked the chrono again. “Ten minutes.”

Ben nodded once. The mission had begun to feel like a narrowing corridor since they left the last node; every stop seemed to take something from their choices. Veraine’s hand was in the route now too plainly to ignore. Not just in the old design, not just in what had been hidden, but in the order itself — in the way each answer pushed them toward the next place before they’d finished dealing with the last one.

Ahead, the leaning shimmer held.

His instincts wanted something concrete to do: alter the angle, bring shields up another fraction, reach out and test the boundary the way he’d test a sealed hatch before trusting it. But this didn’t feel like a hatch. It felt like ground that might slope under his weight the moment he stepped onto it.

So he waited.

Behind him, the cabin had gone quiet. Torin had stopped pretending not to listen. Kael stood near the cockpit door with that detached stillness that never meant ease, only attention sharpened down to essentials. Sera sat at the sensor station, eyes on the secondary display, posture locked into the same controlled line she’d worn since Veranthos.

The board gave a soft chirp.

Ben turned.

Sera’s hands were off the console. Both of them. Drawn back to her lap, fingers folded hard into her palms as if she’d touched live current and refused to show the recoil. Her face had gone very still.

Not confusion. Recognition.

“What is it?” Ben asked.

She didn’t answer. Her eyes stayed on the display. A notation had resolved there out of the scan data — characters Ben couldn’t read, old and sharp and deliberate. Not an automatic systems tag. A mark.

“Sera.”

She looked up at him at last. The movement cost her; he saw it in the tightening at the corners of her mouth, the way her shoulders set before she spoke.

“That’s Gavran’s seal,” she said. Her voice was level only because she was forcing it to be. “Personal notation.”

Desh half-turned in his seat. “Not anchor code?”

“No.”

Ben watched her another second. “Separate.”

“Yes.”

The single word landed harder than any explanation could have. Gavran had built the larger containment. Ben knew that much. If he’d marked this place apart from the rest, he’d done it on purpose.

Desh looked back to the display, then to the chrono. “Eight minutes.”

No one said what Ben was thinking: that if Veraine had driven them here, she had driven them not just to another node, but to one even Gavran had treated differently. The route no longer felt like discovery. It felt like confinement, each turn chosen for them farther in advance than Ben wanted to admit.

“What kind of seal?” Kael asked from the doorway.

Sera’s gaze stayed on the old characters. “Personal.”

Kael waited, as if giving her room to add the part that mattered.

She didn’t.

The silence stretched.

Ben wanted to push. Training said get the information while you still had altitude, distance, a way to break off clean. He could feel the minutes ticking now, not abstractly but as a set of decisions closing one by one: continue approach, commit to landing, step out, touch the door. Every answer delayed became an answer they’d have to live without.

But Sera’s posture stopped him. He knew the difference by now between her readiness and her refusal. This was neither. This was the stillness she wore when she was carrying something sharp enough to cut anyone she handed it to.

He didn’t ask.

Desh met his eyes, one brow up in a silent question. Ben gave a short shake of his head. Not here. Not while she was holding herself together by force.

Desh turned back to the controls and brought them down another notch.

The ship slipped through high haze. The world beneath them sharpened all at once.

The compound emerged out of the mountain like an argument made in stone. Geometric lines cut into the rock with austere precision — clean planes, strict angles, no ornament anywhere to soften the effect. It was disciplined and beautiful in the coldest possible way, as if beauty had been permitted only where it served control. The high-altitude light washed everything pale. Even through the viewport Ben could imagine the air outside: sharp, thin, rinsed of smell.

No outbuildings. No traffic. No beacon sweep. No sign of life.

Just the structure and the mountain holding it.

“There,” Desh said quietly.

The entrance sat set into the rock face so exactly it looked less installed than revealed. Stone and alloy met with almost no seam. Above it, faint against the mountain, the same notation glimmered.

Not amber. Not warning-red. A cooler light than that. Steady. Patient.

Desh guided them toward the landing shelf with care Ben hadn’t heard him use on any of the previous approaches. The sensor board chirped twice more, each time with those same sliding returns that refused to behave like ordinary scans.

“Still doing it?” Ben asked.

“Worse now that we’ve got line of sight.” Desh exhaled through his nose. “The facility stays where it is. The energy signature keeps leaning around it.”

Leaning. That was the word for all of it. The place felt as if gravity itself had an opinion.

The engines cycled down. Ben unstrapped and moved to the viewport with the others as the last of the exterior haze drifted past the transparisteel.

Torin stood with his arms folded, face unreadable. Kael hung back enough to keep the whole compartment in sight. Desh remained by the controls, running shutdown in quick, efficient motions that still somehow looked impatient. The chrono on the panel kept counting, indifferent and exact.

Ben stood beside Sera and looked out at the sealed door.

The silence in the cabin had changed again. It wasn’t transit quiet anymore, and not the focused hush before movement. It had weight now. A shared awareness with nowhere to go.

Sera had not spoken since naming the seal. She stared at the entrance as if distance had done nothing to make it easier.

Ben watched her in profile. The set jaw. The restraint. The knowledge that she was choosing not to tell them yet.

Outside, the compound waited in the thin clear light. Cold. Disciplined. Remote enough that Veraine’s reach felt sharper for having found it. This far out, with Gavran’s private mark over the door and the sensors refusing to behave, there was no pretending they had drifted into the next step by chance.

Sera looked at the sealed entrance.

“This was Maetha’s,” she said very quietly.

The name meant nothing to Ben.

Sera’s expression said it should.
