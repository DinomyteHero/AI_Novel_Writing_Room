# Chapter 8

## Scene 08.01

The Force dropped out from under him.

Ben’s hands hit the cockpit armrests before the sensation had fully registered—something less like the clean hush after hyperspace and more like a presence breaking into strips, gone, back, gone again. Like a comm channel losing signal. Like reaching for a handhold and closing on air.

His chest tightened. His first impulse was to do something about it—change course, check the systems, pull up the nav readouts and find the fault he could fix—but there was nothing to fix. The ship was fine. The displays were fine. What was failing was the thing they’d been flying through for days, and it clearly had no interest in whether he was ready for it.

Behind him, Sera went still. Not composed stillness. Braced-against-a-moving-wall stillness. Her Force presence drew in tight, all hard edges and discipline, like she was locking down every instinct she had and remembering older, uglier lessons while she did it.

Kael didn’t speak. He’d been quiet before the thinning started; now he was quiet in a different way, the silence of someone who knew how to make himself smaller in a room where the wrong glance could get him killed. His eyes tracked the cockpit without seeming to. Ben caught the direction anyway. Kael was watching Torin.

Torin had settled into his seat.

Not braced. Not tense. Settled. His shoulders had dropped a fraction, his head tilted as if he were listening through the static. He looked less like a man enduring the zone than one easing into a chair he’d occupied for years. His Force presence, that warm, jagged thing Ben had been counting as stability since the fortress, shifted. The heaviness in it eased. Something in Torin relaxed into the thin zone as if it belonged there.

Ben’s stomach dipped before his mind caught up.

He couldn’t have said what was wrong about it. It wasn’t a thought yet, just his body sounding an alarm before language could catch up. His eyes found Torin anyway, and what he saw was a man listening to the gaps between the Force’s fragments with an expression that wasn’t fear and wasn’t strain and wasn’t anything Ben wanted to name.

“Ankhural called it the natural state,” Torin murmured.

His voice was low, almost absent, like he was noting weather he’d seen before. No lecture. No performance. Just the tone of someone who’d spent a long time somewhere like this and hadn’t forgotten what it had cost to leave.

The cockpit seemed to hold the words for a beat too long.

Sera’s face emptied of expression. Not blank—emptied, like a room cleared before a fight. She’d heard exactly what Ben had heard. Filed it somewhere colder.

Kael’s stillness sharpened. His eyes hadn’t left Torin, and now his attention had the edge of assessment. Ben had seen that look before—on Desh, usually, when things were about to go bad and the only question left was how fast.

Desh himself was at the nav station, hands steady on the console, doing what Desh did when the Force-users started getting strange: flying the ship and keeping his mouth shut. The numbers on his display flickered, degradation stacking into a nastier baseline, the signal from the next node arriving in pieces with holes between them.

“Zone’s thinning out,” Desh said. His voice was flat, practical. “Another two minutes, maybe three.”

Ben nodded without turning. His grip on the armrest hadn’t loosened.

The two minutes dragged.

The Force came back in fragments—a pulse of presence, then nothing, then a thread of something that almost felt normal before it frayed again. Ben kept trying to compensate for a lag that wasn’t in his body. Every time he reached and got silence, his chest clenched harder. Every time the Force returned, the relief lasted less.

Torin didn’t tense at all. His head stayed tilted, his breathing even, his presence in the Force easing with each gap—as if the thinning were stripping away something he’d carried since the fortress. Since before the fortress. Since a thousand years ago, in a war Ben was only beginning to understand.

The sensation in Ben’s gut didn’t bother becoming words.

The zone cleared.

The Force settled back into its normal density—still wrong in the deeper places, still tugging toward the bearing, but whole again. The half-beat hitch in Ben’s reflexes smoothed out. His chest loosened by degrees.

Sera rose without a word and left the cockpit.

Ben waited until her steps faded into the corridor, then pushed out of the pilot’s seat. His legs held. His hands were less reliable. He flexed them once, twice, and followed her.

She caught him in the corridor.

Her hand closed around his arm above the elbow—hard, deliberate, enough to leave marks. Ben stopped. Sera’s face was still emptied of expression, but up close he could see the tension in her jaw, the fine lines around her eyes that hadn’t been there before the thin place.

“Watch him,” she said.

Her voice stayed low. Measured. The same tone she used for battlefield orders and tactical calls.

“In the thin places. Watch what happens to his face.”

She didn’t say Torin’s name. She didn’t need to.

Ben felt the grip as anchor and warning at once. Sera’s hand was steady—steadier than his arm under it—and the pressure of her fingers was the sort of thing you didn’t forget. A message in the only language she trusted him to hear.

He met her eyes. “I saw it.”

“I know.” She let go. The marks stayed. “That’s why I’m telling you.”

She moved past him toward the common area, her steps even and unhurried, the same disciplined cadence she’d had since waking up a thousand years out of time. Ben stayed in the corridor a moment longer.

The ship hummed around him. Recycled air. The faint tang of the thin place fading from the bulkheads. Through the wall, he could hear Desh running post-transit checks and Kael’s silence—a different silence now, one that had already put Torin somewhere Ben didn’t want to look.

Torin’s calm voice still echoed in his head. Ankhural called it the natural state. The tone more than the words. The ease more than either.

Sera’s grip still lived in the muscle memory of his arm.

Ben flexed his hand once and headed back to the cockpit. He didn’t look at Torin when he sat down. He didn’t need to. The warning was in his arm and in his stomach and in the empty space Sera had left behind in the corridor, a space that now felt heavier than the thin place had.

## Scene 08.02

The galley was too small for five people and an argument, but they crammed in both anyway.

Sera had been eating in silence since they’d cleared the thin place, her fork moving with the same disciplined economy she brought to everything. Ben had watched her work through half a ration bar before Torin spoke, and the moment he did, her fork stopped.

“The technique isn’t sacred or profane,” Torin said, reaching for the water bulb beside his plate. His voice stayed easy, almost conversational. “It’s engineering. You have a structure under strain, you reinforce it. You have a wound that won’t close, you stitch it. The stitching isn’t the wound.”

Sera set her fork down.

Not dropped. Not quite placed. Set down with a precision that made Ben’s hand stop halfway to his own plate. The tines lined up with the edge of her tray, the handle straight, the whole gesture a warning all by itself.

“It’s a lobotomy,” she said.

Torin spread his hands on the table. Open. Reasonable. “It’s a technique that deadens Force-sensitivity in a local area. Whether that’s a lobotomy or a tourniquet depends on what you’re trying to stop.”

“You’re describing the Weave like machinery.”

“I’m describing it as something that can be damaged and repaired. The rest is vocabulary.”

Ben’s first instinct was to cut in before the room locked down any further, but he stayed where he was. The space between Sera and Torin had gone too still.

“The rest is not vocabulary,” Sera said. Her diction sharpened on every word. “The Weave is the work of the Force. You talk about it like a construction job.”

“I’m talking about something twelve people were sealed into chambers to maintain.” Torin’s warmth was still there, but thinner now, the humor gone from it. “If the work is sacred, what does that make the anchors?”

Kael hadn’t looked up from his plate. He ate the way he did everything else—efficiently, without waste. But there was a stillness in his shoulders that said he was listening to every word.

Desh stood in the galley doorway with his arms crossed, watching the table with the flat, assessing look of someone reading a room because the room mattered. He caught Ben’s eye for half a second. Said nothing.

Sera touched the handle of her fork without lifting it. “The anchors were a crime. You’re proposing to do it again.”

“I’m proposing to understand the tool well enough to know whether that’s true.” Torin kept his hands where they were, voice calm, making the argument sound plain. “The stitching exists. The wound exists. Refusing to look at how one was used on the other doesn’t make either vanish.”

Sera turned toward Kael.

“And you?” she said. “You’ve been quiet since the fortress. What does the Brotherhood’s conscript think of the stitching?”

Kael’s fork paused.

Torin’s hands moved before Kael answered. Barely a movement at all, just a small turn outward that pulled the line of the exchange back to himself.

“Kael wasn’t in the chamber with us when Gavran made that decision,” Torin said. “Neither were you, Sera. You were already sealed by then.”

The redirect slid into place so smoothly Ben almost missed it. Sera’s question had been aimed at Kael; somehow it landed on Torin instead. His tone never changed. His posture never changed. He had simply taken the heat, and by the time he finished, the opening for Sera to press Kael had closed.

Kael resumed eating.

Ben noticed and filed it away without knowing what to do with it.

“The problem,” Sera said, and her voice had gone cold now, “is that you think the stitching is separate from what it does. That you can use the technique and stay clean. You can’t.”

Torin left his hands on the table. “And your position is that the Weave must be left untouched even where it’s already been used as a weapon.”

“It does not become less sacred because someone abused it.”

“No. But abuse doesn’t stop being real because the thing abused matters to you.”

For a second nobody spoke. The galley hummed around them, recycled air and the steady vibration under the deck plates.

Then the ship gave a hard little shudder.

It was enough to rattle utensils against trays and send a thin splash of water over the rim of Ben’s bulb onto his knuckles. Desh’s head snapped toward the corridor on reflex. From somewhere forward came the brief complaining whine of a panel taking the vibration badly, then settling.

Nobody got up. But the interruption cut across the argument just long enough to remind Ben they were still on a ship held together by parts, stress, and habit.

Desh pushed off the doorway, listened for another sound, then stayed where he was when none came.

Kael set down his fork.

The click against the tray was small, but it cut straight through the hum. He didn’t look at Sera or Torin right away. When he did, his expression hadn’t changed.

“You’re all describing the same cage.”

His voice was flat. Not heated, not weary, nothing anyone could get a grip on.

“Sera calls the cloth sacred and says anyone who touches it is a vandal. Torin calls it stitching and says anyone who won’t touch it is afraid. The Brotherhood called it a weapon and said anyone who wouldn’t use it was prey.” He took a sip from his water bulb and set it down. “Three ways of naming the bars. Same cage.”

Silence dropped over the table.

Sera’s hand stayed beside her fork. Torin’s were still spread on the table, but the reasonableness had gone out of the gesture, leaving only the shape. Desh looked from Kael to Ben and said nothing.

Ben didn’t have anything either.

The ship hummed. Food cooled on plates. Somewhere forward, the panel gave one last thin buzz and fell quiet.

Nobody answered, because nobody had a good response.

## Scene 08.03

Ben heard the exchange before he saw it.

Not the words—the crawlspace sat too far down the corridor for that, and the ship’s steady mechanical hum swallowed anything below a certain volume—but the rhythm. Two voices trading short, unhurried sentences. Not an argument. Not a briefing. Just two people talking about something concrete enough that neither had to raise a voice.

He slowed without meaning to. The corridor outside engineering was narrow enough that slowing meant stopping, his shoulder grazing the bulkhead while the hyperdrive throbbed through the deck plates under his boots. The crawlspace door stood half-open. Through the gap he could see Desh’s legs extending from the access panel, boots braced against the grating, the rest of him buried in the ship’s innards.

Kael stood in the doorway. Not hovering—he’d folded himself against the frame with the easy patience of someone who had been there for a while, arms crossed, head tipped slightly as he watched Desh work.

“—draw pattern,” Kael was saying, his voice carrying the same dry, economical register he’d used in the galley. “The power-cycle draw. It’s been fluctuating on a three-second interval since the last transit. Not enough to trip the warning system, but it’s consistent.”

Desh’s reply came from inside the crawlspace, muffled by machinery. “Three seconds exactly?”

“Three point two.”

A pause. The clink of a tool being set down on metal. Then Desh’s legs shifted, and he slid out of the access panel far enough to look up at Kael. His face was streaked with something dark—lubricant or coolant, Ben couldn’t tell from the corridor—and his expression held the same flat, measuring look he’d worn in the galley doorway.

“Show me,” Desh said.

Kael unfolded from the frame and stepped inside.

Ben stayed where he was. The corridor around him had gone quiet; the rest of the ship was empty, Sera and Torin having retreated to their own corners after the argument, the galley’s tension still bleeding away like heat from a cooling plate. He should keep moving. The cockpit was waiting. The nav-computer needed updating before they reached the next Corridor node.

He didn’t move.

Through the half-open door he watched Kael crouch beside the access panel, his hands moving over the diagnostic readout to indicate what he’d found. Desh listened without interrupting, head angled, eyes tracking whatever Kael showed him. The exchange was quick—a few sentences, a nod from Desh, and then Desh was sliding back into the crawlspace while Kael reached for the toolkit without being asked.

Ben felt the shift the way he’d learned to feel tactical changes in a firefight: not as a thought, but as a nudge at the back of his awareness that said something had changed. Kael had approached Desh. Not the other way around. Not because Desh had asked, not because Ben had arranged it, not because anyone had told Kael where he belonged or what he should contribute.

He’d just gone.

The crawlspace had been built for one person. Desh filled most of it—his shoulders, his toolkit, the sprawl of cabling and conduit housing that made up the ship’s power-distribution system. Kael folded himself into the remaining space with the economy of someone used to making room where there wasn’t any, his body fitting the gaps without hesitation, his hands already sorting through the open toolkit on the grating.

Ben had seen Kael fight. He’d seen him move through the fortress on WR-7741 with a predator’s efficiency, every motion measured, every step placed. This was different. The same precision, but without the tension that had sat under it since the moment he’d woken from stasis. His shoulders had dropped half an inch. His jaw had loosened. He was handing Desh tools before Desh asked, his hands moving with the rhythm of work he knew too well to think about.

“Hydrospanner,” Desh said.

Kael’s hand closed around the tool. Then stopped.

Not for long. Just a beat. The smallest hesitation, deliberate enough that Ben saw it even from the corridor, even through the half-open door. Kael’s fingers wrapped around the hydrospanner’s grip, and for a moment he didn’t move. His eyes stayed on the tool. His breathing stayed even. The ship hummed around them, the hyperdrive pulsed through the deck, and Kael held the hydrospanner like he was deciding something.

Then he passed it.

Desh took it without comment. His hand closed around the grip—the metal still warm from Kael’s palm—and reached back into the access panel to keep working.

Ben let out a breath he hadn’t realized he’d been holding.

The hydrospanner. That was the moment. Not the observation about the power-cycle draw, not the ease with which Kael had folded himself into the crawlspace—the tool. Desh had asked for it without thinking, the way he’d ask anyone working beside him, the way he’d asked Ben a hundred times on a hundred survey runs. And Kael had held it for that extra beat, that deliberate stillness, because no one had asked him for something that wasn’t about the Force since he’d woken from stasis.

No one had asked him for anything at all.

Ben pushed away from the bulkhead. The cockpit was waiting. The nav-computer needed updating. Whatever was happening in the crawlspace didn’t need him standing in the corridor tallying it like a mission report he’d never file.

He took one step toward the cockpit. Stopped.

Kael’s voice came through the door again, quieter this time, almost casual. “Does it bother you?”

Desh’s answer was muffled by the access panel. “Does what bother me?”

“The Force. The way they talk about it. The way they assume everyone in the room is having the same conversation.”

A pause. The sound of the hydrospanner turning, metal on metal. Then Desh’s voice, flat and dry: “The Force doesn’t fix a bad conduit. I don’t need it to.”

Kael made a sound that wasn’t quite a laugh—more an exhale, short and sharp, the noise someone made when they’d expected a different answer and didn’t get one.

Ben looked back through the doorway. Kael’s face was half-lit in the crawlspace’s dim glow, his mouth still shaped around that sound. Not a smile. Just the faintest lift at one corner, gone almost as soon as it appeared.

Almost.

Ben turned and walked toward the cockpit. The nav-computer was waiting. The next Corridor node was waiting. Whatever Kael and Desh were building in the crawlspace didn’t need him to name it.

The hydrospanner had done that already.
