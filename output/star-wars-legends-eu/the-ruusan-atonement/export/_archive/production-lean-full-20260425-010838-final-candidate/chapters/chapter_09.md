# Chapter 9

## Scene 09.01

Sera had carried the Codex case from the fortress herself. Ben had watched her lift it from the archive chamber, hands steady, face giving away nothing. She’d kept it sealed since then—through the debate in the galley, through the thin place, through every conversation that circled the thing inside without naming it.

Now she set it on the common-area table.

The case was plain enough: dark metal, worn at the edges, no markings Ben recognized. It could have held medical gear or navigation crystals. Around it, the Steady Returns’ common area stayed quiet—four acceleration couches around a table bolted to the deck, the hyperdrive’s low hum threading through the bulkheads until Ben had mostly stopped hearing it.

Desh stood near the corridor to the cockpit, arms crossed, wearing the same easy readiness he used when he’d decided a situation didn’t need his input yet. Kael had folded into the corner couch with his back to the bulkhead, watching Sera’s hands. Torin sat across from Ben, forearms on the table, his Force presence carrying that warm, jagged edge Ben had learned meant anticipation.

Sera didn’t speak. She put her thumb to the seal.

The lock gave with a soft hiss, like a breath released.

The air shifted.

Ben felt it before he understood it—a drop in the room’s balance, the way the air changed just ahead of a storm rolling in off the coast. His ears didn’t pop. Something deeper did. The Force around the table, which a moment before had been four distinct presences held in easy balance, went taut and still.

Sera lifted the fragment from the case.

It was smaller than Ben had expected. A slab of dark stone, about the length of his hand, its surface cut with characters he didn’t know. The lines were dense and precise, deep enough to hold shadow. Inside the case it had glimmered faintly, a residual glow that died the instant Sera’s fingers left it.

She set it on the table between them.

No one spoke. The hyperdrive’s hum seemed to pull back, or maybe the silence around the fragment had simply grown louder. Ben’s attention narrowed until the room’s edges felt far away—the couch under him, the recycled-air tang, the faint vibration in the deck plates all pushed behind something sharper and nearer.

The fragment was not inert. He could feel that now, the way he could feel a live circuit before he touched it. Not a presence. Not even a signature. Just a held breath that made the skin on his arms tighten.

“Ankhural’s Codex,” Sera said at last. Her voice stayed quiet, formal, the same tone she’d used in the fortress archive. “The section on vergence architecture. What he believed the Jedi Lords had done to the local structure around Veranthos.”

Torin leaned forward. His Force presence tightened, that warm hum narrowing to a point. “You’ve read it.”

“I read the sealed summary in Gavran’s records. This is the primary text.” Sera kept her eyes on the fragment. “He argued that what we feel at vergences isn’t inherent. That it was imposed. Built into the site by the practitioners who first shaped it, until the shaping felt natural because no one alive remembered it.”

Kael gave a quiet sound that wasn’t quite a snort and wasn’t quite a laugh. “That would be heresy in any tradition I trained in.”

“It was heresy in ours,” Sera said.

Ben heard the exchange, but it stayed a layer away. His attention had locked on the fragment. The waiting quality had sharpened, or maybe he had just stopped screening it out. The Force around the stone felt off in a way he couldn’t quite name—not damaged, not dark, just missing in one direction. Like a room with one wall taken away and the space beyond not empty, but unmade.

He reached out.

The motion wasn’t entirely conscious. His hand moved toward the fragment the way it might move toward a door he hadn’t decided to open yet, and he was aware of Desh shifting near the corridor, aware of Torin’s eyes following his fingers, aware of Sera going still. None of it stopped him.

His fingertips touched the stone.

The Force was there.

And then it wasn’t.

No—it was there, but stripped bare. The sensation hit him as a sudden emptying, so abrupt his body reacted before his mind caught up. Weight without direction. Sound without body. The Force around him, through him, was simply present with nothing in it—no texture, no pull, no moral weight to tell him which way to lean. The compass he’d trusted since he was old enough to feel the Force at all had stopped pointing.

His hand jerked back.

The recoil slammed his elbow against the couch arm. Pain flashed up his forearm, sharp and welcome and real. He was breathing hard. His pulse hammered. The fragment sat where it had been, unchanged, unlit, and the Force around it was already easing back into the weave he knew.

The whole thing had lasted three seconds.

“Ben.” Desh’s voice came from the corridor, low and level. “What was that?”

Ben shook his head. He didn’t have words yet. His fingertips still tingled, and the place where he’d touched the stone held a ghost-cold that had nothing to do with temperature. The afterimage sat in his chest like a breath he couldn’t quite let go.

“The imposed structure,” Torin said quietly. “Ankhural was right. You felt it. The absence of the shaping—that’s what the Force is without the architecture we’ve put into it.”

Sera’s jaw tightened. “You don’t know what he felt.”

“I know what I felt when the case opened. We all did.” Torin looked around the table. His warmth was still there, still genuine, but something under it had gone sharp. “The Codex lays out a way to read the local weave. To tell what’s inherent from what’s been imposed. If Ankhural was right about vergences—”

“Then the Jedi Lords built a cage and called it sacred ground,” Kael said. His voice stayed dry, nearly lazy, but his eyes were sharp. “Which is exactly what the Sith would have done if they’d thought of it first.”

“That’s not—” Sera began.

“I’m not taking a side. I’m noting a symmetry.” Kael shifted against the bulkhead, settling his shoulders. “Every tradition I’ve been forced into believed its structure was natural. The Brotherhood. The Jedi Lords. Your modern Order, probably. The only difference is whose name is on the architecture.”

Ben heard them. Sera’s clipped precision, Torin’s gathering certainty, Kael’s flat refusal to commit. Four Force-sensitives holding the same thing and landing on four different readings in real time. He should say something. He should push back, ask the right question, keep the conversation from hardening into positions they couldn’t walk back.

He couldn’t. The afterimage was still too loud. His fingertips were still cold. For a handful of seconds the Force had been there and unshaped—no direction, no texture, no moral register—and he had yanked his hand away before his mind arrived, because his body had known faster than thought that whatever he was touching was wrong in a way he didn’t have a name for.

Not dangerous. Not safe either. Safe was the word that had failed. The Force without that familiar register wasn’t hostile. It was just absent in a direction he’d never had to navigate without.

The ship alarm screamed.

The sound cut through the common area like a blade—three sharp tones, then a sustained pulse that shivered through the deck plates. Desh was moving before the second tone ended, pushing off the corridor frame and heading for the cockpit in one smooth motion.

“Breach alarm,” he called over his shoulder. His voice went flat and professional. “The Brotherhood Outpost. Energy readings are spiking hard. Someone’s there.”

Ben was on his feet. The vertigo still turned in his chest, unresolved, but his body had already snapped into tactical focus. Sera was closing the Codex case. Torin’s warmth had gone tight and ready. Kael had unfolded from the corner couch without a sound.

“How recent?” Ben asked.

Desh’s reply came from the cockpit, clipped and certain. “Recent enough the readings haven’t dropped off. Someone is there, or was there very recently.”

Ben braced one hand on the doorframe. The fragment’s afterimage was still there—a cold spot in his memory, a direction he couldn’t feel. He set it aside. Not answered. Just shelved.

The Brotherhood Outpost had just gotten a lot closer.

## Scene 09.02

Torin lifted the fragment without ceremony.

His hand closed on the dark stone and raised it from the case Sera had opened on the table. Ben’s breath tightened. Everyone else had flinched from the thing’s stripped-bare emptiness, from the way the Force went directionless and cold around it. Torin held it like a tool.

His presence shifted with it. The familiar warm, jagged confidence narrowed and smoothed into something lighter, cleaner, more dangerous. The air around the table seemed to thin.

“The records are intact,” Torin said. “Ankhural was documenting what he found at Veranthos.”

Sera stood opposite him, arms folded, jaw hard. “Read it,” she said. “Don’t improve it.”

Torin glanced at her, then back to the fragment. “The wound at Veranthos isn’t damage that heals. It’s absence. The local structure was taken away and never re-formed.”

The understanding landed in Ben’s body first: a drop in his center, throat tight, stomach hollow. Not a scar. A hole.

Torin went on, quieter now. “Gavran’s answer was containment. Twelve Force-sensitives placed around the perimeter, sustaining the seal. Not as guards. As the seal. Living anchors holding the wound closed.”

Ben stared at the stone. Twelve people. Not an outer field, not some machine left humming in a vault. People, year after year, bled into shape and function until there was nothing left but the work they were forced to do.

The ship’s recycled air tasted metallic. Beneath his boots the Steady Returns’ engines kept their steady hum, absurdly ordinary.

Kael said nothing from the couch, but one look at his face was enough: there it was again, all of them building higher walls inside the same trap.

Torin turned the fragment in his hand. “And Ankhural’s heresy is simple. The void wasn’t created by the technique. It was exposed by it. The weave everyone treated as natural at Veranthos wasn’t natural at all. It had been imposed there, shaped and fixed in place. The procedure stripped that architecture away.”

Sera’s mouth tightened. “That’s the part you like.”

“It’s the part the text supports.” Torin set the fragment down with care. “If he was right, then this wasn’t violation in the way we’ve been taught to think of it. It was correction. What Gavran sealed wasn’t a wound trying to heal. It was a state he couldn’t accept. The anchors weren’t punishment. They were a structural answer to the fact that once the site was opened, no one knew how to let it stand.”

Beautiful. That was the danger of it. The pieces fit too well. Nihilus, Malachor, every warning Ben had ever heard about wounds in the Force—they all sat inside the argument instead of breaking it. If the thing at Veranthos would not heal on its own, if the twelve had truly been the only reason it stayed bounded, then horror and necessity locked together until you could barely tell them apart.

And if Ankhural was right—

Ben felt his head start to dip.

Sera moved first.

She stepped to the table and laid her hand flat between Torin and the fragment. Not hard. Not dramatic. A line drawn in plain sight.

“No,” she said.

Torin looked up. “Sera—”

“No.” Her voice stayed low, but the word had edges. “You don’t get to call a man chained into a wall a solution and make it clean.”

For a beat nobody spoke. Ben caught the motion of his own nod and stopped it cold. The shock of that was worse than the argument. He had almost gone with Torin all the way to the end of it.

Sera’s eyes flicked to him, saw that he knew it, and held there for a fraction of a second.

Then she stepped back from the table. “Ben. Corridor.”

It wasn’t a request.

He followed her out. The door slid shut behind them, muting the common area to a dull mechanical hum. The corridor lights were low; the air was cooler here. Sera stopped with her back to the door and faced him.

For a moment she said nothing. Her jaw worked once.

Then, very quietly, “I’ve heard that argument before.”

Ben didn’t answer.

“Same shape,” she said. “Same calm. Same way it takes something monstrous and turns it one degree until it looks necessary.” Her gaze stayed on his. “I followed a man like that into a war.”

The words hit clean and hard.

“He was wrong,” she said. “I knew it too late.”

She didn’t say the name. She didn’t need to. Ben heard it anyway, in the space she left between one sentence and the next.

## Scene 09.03

The alarm slammed through the common area like a blade.

Ben was on his feet before the klaxon finished its first pulse. His body moved through the door and down the corridor, reaching the cockpit doorway before his mind fully caught up. Proximity alert. Desh had set it to trip on energy signatures matching the Corridor’s profile.

Desh was already in the pilot’s seat. Three seconds. Ben had counted them without meaning to. The man’s hands flew across the controls with the same professional calm he’d brought to every sensor sweep since Terminus Station, pulling up data streams and tactical overlays while the klaxon thinned to a steady warning chime.

“We have a problem,” Desh said.

Ben caught the doorframe and braced. The starfield ahead was only the usual smear of hyperspace, but the scanner to Desh’s left was lit with readings that did not belong there. Energy signatures. Clustered. Pointing straight at the next stop on their route.

“The Brotherhood Outpost,” Desh said without looking up. “Someone beat us there.”

Behind Ben, the corridor filled with bodies. Sera first—she’d been closest to the cockpit, and she moved like someone who’d spent a lifetime answering alarms. Her hand closed on the back of the copilot’s chair and stayed there, knuckles white against the worn fabric. Kael came next, silent as ever, folding himself into the corner where the bulkhead met the corridor wall. Torin’s heavier footsteps followed, and Ben felt him settle in behind his right shoulder.

“How bad?” Ben asked.

Desh’s fingers moved over the sensor controls. “Energy spikes. Concentrated. I’m reading at least four distinct signatures—power signatures, not weapons fire. Someone was running equipment down there.” He paused, eyes narrowing at the data. “Days old. Whatever happened, it’s done.”

The words landed in Ben’s chest without bringing relief. Days old. Not an ambush waiting. Veraine had been here, and she’d moved on.

The alarm’s bite shifted in him, from raw startle to something colder. Tactical assessment. The question wasn’t whether they were flying into a fight. The question was what she’d been doing here for days, and what she wanted them to find.

“Show me,” Ben said.

Desh brought up the signatures on the scanner. Four clusters of energy discharge, spread across the outpost’s layout as if someone had worked methodically through the facility. The timestamps told the story: the earliest spike was seventy-two hours old, the most recent almost a day. Veraine hadn’t just passed through. She’d spent time here. Enough time to do something deliberate.

“She knew we were coming,” Sera said quietly. Her voice was flat and controlled. “She left this for us.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was already reaching for the Force, letting his awareness stretch ahead of the ship toward the outpost below. The thin place was waiting.

It was worse than the last sites. Moderate now, not mild—the way a crack widened across a floor, the way oil seeped through and spread. Dark-side residue bled through the weave’s thinning places, thick and cold, pressing at Ben’s awareness like something trying to force its way through a seam that had already started to give. He felt it in his teeth first, then in the back of his throat.

And under it, something else. A pattern. Deliberate. Someone had shaped what was left here, arranged it so anyone who came looking would find exactly what she wanted them to find.

A message.

“How long until we’re in range?”

Torin’s voice cut in from the corridor. Eager. Ready. Ben heard the edge under it, though—the same edge that had been there when Torin had held the Codex fragment and talked about the void as revelation rather than damage.

Desh didn’t answer right away. His hands kept moving across the controls, running a secondary scan, cross-checking the energy signatures against the outpost’s layout. The hesitation was professional, but it opened a space in the cockpit that hadn’t been there a moment earlier. A gap between Torin’s eagerness and Desh’s caution.

“I’d like to finish the scan before we commit to an approach vector,” Desh said. His voice stayed even. “If she left surprises, I want to know about them before we’re in range of whatever she left behind.”

“We’re already behind her by days,” Torin said. “Every minute we spend scanning is another minute she uses to get farther ahead.”

“She’s not running. She’s staging.” Desh turned in his seat and met Torin’s gaze with the flat calm of someone who’d learned patience in rooms where impatience got people killed. “If she wanted to be gone, she wouldn’t have left signatures this clean. She’s inviting us in. I want to know what we’re walking into before we accept.”

The friction was there. Not a fight—not yet—but the shape of one. Torin wanted speed. Desh wanted certainty. And Ben stood in the cockpit doorway with the Codex argument still unresolved behind him, its weight pressing against his shoulders along with the dark-side residue bleeding through the thin place ahead.

He didn’t reach for the argument. The alarm had burned that thought out. What remained was the tactical question: which instinct did he trust?

Ben felt the rest of the group waiting on his answer. Sera’s stillness. Kael’s silence. Torin’s coiled eagerness. Desh’s steady hands on the controls, still running the scan because no one had told him to stop.

“Finish the scan,” Ben said. “I want to know what we’re flying into.”

Torin said nothing. The silence was answer enough.

Desh gave one sharp nod and turned back to his console. The data kept scrolling—energy signatures, layout overlays, the slow build of information that would tell them what Veraine had done here and what she’d left behind. The cockpit settled into the quiet of professionals working under pressure.

Ben stayed braced against the doorframe, eyes on the starfield ahead, and felt the thin place waiting for them. Oil seeping through a cracked floor. Dark-side residue leaking into a space that should have been clean. And beneath it, that deliberate pattern—the shape of a message Veraine had spent days arranging.

His breathing steadied. Uncertainty gave way to focus. Whatever waited at the Brotherhood Outpost, Veraine had left it for them to find.

The Steady Returns carried them toward it.
