Sera was already moving when Ben finished reading the expression on her face.

She crossed the archive bay in three measured strides and laid her hand on the console. Her fingers hovered over the access panel for one thin breath, then snapped into motion. No guesswork. No hesitation. Muscle memory. The sequence she entered looked older than the room itself and moved faster than any modern code Ben had ever seen on a Jedi terminal.

The console answered with a low hum and a bloom of light under her palm.

Ben had the absurd, useless thought that the machine sounded offended to be woken by her.

He took half a step forward, then stopped. The sealed Codex fragment sat in its case on the side table, faintly lit through the transparisteel, and the console’s hum lifted the fine hairs along his forearms. Archive air carried dust, old power cells, and the cold tang of durasteel warmed by too many systems coming online at once.

Desh glanced at him, then at the display. “That good, is it?”

“Apparently,” Ben said.

Sera didn’t look around. “The console is responsive.”

Only Sera could make that sound like a battlefield report.

A holo lattice sprang up over the archive surface, lines resolving into columns of archaic Basic so formal it looked carved instead of displayed. Ben caught maybe half before the text scrolled. Pre-Reformation administrative style, if he was reading the header right. The sort of thing Luke’s Order had kept in the deep stacks and never bothered to make friendly.

Sera traced one column with two fingers, and the archive shifted again.

Something about the sight of her doing it made Ben’s skin prickle, though it took him a second to name why. Not wrong. Wrong the way a locked door was wrong when somebody else opened it from the inside.

Torin came up on Sera’s other side and watched the index unfold with the expression Ben had only seen on people staring at a family heirloom they’d expected to outlive them.

“Still indexed under Veranthos?” Torin said.

Sera’s mouth moved by a fraction. “As it should be.”

Torin gave a soft, warm huff. “Good. For a moment I thought the archivists had finally managed to misplace the important bits.”

He reached in and flicked the side of the display with two fingers. The holo expanded, and a second set of entries bloomed beneath the first. Ben leaned closer before he could stop himself.

Containment terms. Anchor rotations. Seal maintenance. Custodial directives.

His eye snagged on the phrasing. Not because he liked it. Because it felt like standing on a deck that had gone slick under his boots.

Desh exhaled through his nose. “You all keep that in your heads, or is this a hobby?”

Torin glanced at him, and there was that same warmth again, the easy one that made the room feel less like a tomb. “A little of both. Some things are hard to forget. Some are harder to lose.”

Ben watched Torin’s hand hover over the holo, then settle on a line of text as if he were touching a scar through cloth.

“Veranthos containment protocol,” Torin said. “That’s the title.”

Ben looked up from the display. “The seal?”

Sera answered without lifting her gaze. “One of the seals.”

That hit hard enough to make him blink.

One of the seals. Not the whole thing. Not the clean public history he knew, the version Luke’s Order had taught in fragments: Ruusan, the New Sith Wars ending in ruin, the Jedi Lords gone, the Army of Light dissolved, the Order pulled back from military power and folded tighter under Republic and Judicial oversight, training centralized, the whole settlement meant to keep that madness from happening again.

That part had always been true. That was the problem.

The display shifted again, and Ben saw enough to make the back of his neck tighten. This wasn’t the public record. It was older, narrower, meaner. Not triumph. Something built after a wound and meant to stay buried where nobody would ask who paid for it.

He heard himself say, “This is before Ruusan.”

Sera’s hand stayed on the console. “Yes.”

Torin looked at him with a flicker of sympathy, then back at the text. “Before the aftermath, at least. The public part came later.”

Ben’s gaze snapped to him. “You know that?”

Torin gave a small shrug, measured rather than careless. “I know enough to know history likes a tidy shelf label. This wasn’t that.”

Desh made a quiet sound that might have been a laugh if the room had been less grim. “That sounds expensive.”

“It was,” Sera said.

Ben opened his mouth and shut it again. His pulse had found his throat, too fast, too clean. The archive had gone from a room full of records to a thing with teeth. The old index wasn’t just data. It was proof. It was also a door he hadn’t known existed.

Kael had been silent at the edge of the bay, half in shadow, as if someone had set him there and forgotten him. Now he moved.

He didn’t hurry. That would have made it dramatic. He just stepped past Sera, close enough that Ben felt the brush of disturbed air, and raised a hand to the display.

“Not architect,” Kael said.

His voice was flat, almost bored.

The holo flickered where he touched it. One designation changed. Ben leaned in and read the new line.

Keeper. Custodian.

Kael lowered his hand and stepped back as if he’d only corrected a label on a crate.

Ben stared at him. “That was important enough to correct?”

Kael’s eyes stayed on the display. “Yes.”

That was all.

No explanation. No flourish. No interest in whether anyone understood the point. The correction sat there heavier than a paragraph would have. Ben had known Kael was quiet. This was different. This was a choice.

Maybe the first one since they’d woken.

Desh looked from Kael to the console and back. “Well. That saves us an argument later.”

Kael’s mouth moved by the smallest amount. “You were going to have one anyway.”

Ben almost smiled, and the near miss hurt more than if he had managed it.

The holo scrolled again, and Torin’s fingers followed the movement with the ease of someone tracing a path he could have walked blind. He wasn’t reading like a scholar. He was reading like a man remembering where he had once stood while the words were written.

“This line here,” Torin said, and tapped the projection. “That’s the part that matters.”

Ben looked.

Private penance-and-containment protocol.

The words hung in the air between them with all the charm of a blaster bolt.

He felt the connection hit before he had time to name it. Ruusan. The public settlement. The reforms Luke’s Order had inherited as history. Real reforms. Real changes. The end of Jedi Lords. The Army of Light gone. Training centralized. The Order bound closer to the Republic and its oversight after the New Sith Wars. All of that had happened. Ben knew it the way he knew the shape of his own saber hilt.

And under it, apparently, this.

A buried thing.

His stomach dropped so hard he had to brace a hand on the edge of the console. The archive lights sharpened. The holo seemed to pull away from him. For one impossible second he couldn’t tell whether the room had tilted or whether his footing had gone loose.

Torin noticed and shifted half a step, ready to catch him if he went over. Ben hated that he noticed the readiness.

“Sera,” he said, and heard how rough his own voice sounded. “This is not the Reformations.”

“No,” she said.

“Ben,” Desh said carefully, practical as ever, “you’re doing the thing where your face goes blank. I assume that means bad.”

Ben dragged in a breath and let it out slowly. His center of gravity came back by inches. “It means I’m trying not to say something embarrassing.”

Kael gave him a look. “A noble restraint.”

That almost did it. Almost.

Ben stared at the words again. Private penance and containment. Not public settlement. Not the clean, official history the galaxy had kept. The public settlement was still real. That mattered. He could feel his mind trying to fit the new shape around the old one and failing in a dozen small places at once.

“So the records Luke’s Order kept,” he said slowly, “the Ruusan Reformations, the whole public restructuring after the war—”

“—were real,” Desh said. “You look like you think someone just knocked the whole house over.”

Ben shot him a brief look. “I am considering it.”

“Fair.”

Sera finally turned from the console. The holo light made one side of her face pale and severe. Her expression hadn’t softened. If anything, it had gone more exact.

“The public settlement was real,” she said. “The containment was older.”

Ben looked at her, then at Torin, then at Kael, and the room rearranged itself one person at a time.

Sera was the instrument. Exact. Unforgiving. Every line she spoke cut clean.

Torin was the keeper. Not because he owned the truth, but because he carried it like a story told at the right moment, with enough warmth to make it survivable.

And Kael—

Kael was the correction. Small. Dry. The thing that made a false line stop looking like fact.

Ben had the disconcerting sense that the archive was no longer speaking to him as a room. It was speaking through them. Three survivors. Three separate voices. Three different relationships to the same buried scar.

He looked back at Sera just in time to catch the way her hand rested near the console, not on it now, as if she had already used the device and set it aside. Her controlled stillness made the whole bay feel narrower.

Torin had gone quiet, but his face still held that faint storyteller’s warmth, the sort that could make an ugly thing bearable without making it smaller. Kael stood with his hands folded behind his back, unreadable except for the fact that he had chosen to speak at all.

Ben swallowed. The words were there now, assembled in a way he couldn’t unhear.

The Reformations hadn’t erased this.

They had buried it.

He let that settle. It had weight. It had edges. The old history Luke’s Order had passed down wasn’t false. That would have been simpler, and simpler was a trap. It was incomplete in exactly the place that now mattered.

Sera’s gaze dropped once to the display, then back to Ben.

“Containment,” she said. “Penance.”

Ben heard himself answer before he had fully decided to. “The Reformations.”

The word landed and stayed there.

Sera didn’t elaborate. She didn’t need to. She didn’t say what the penance was for, and the absence of that answer was louder than any explanation would have been. Ben could read the rest of it off her face.
