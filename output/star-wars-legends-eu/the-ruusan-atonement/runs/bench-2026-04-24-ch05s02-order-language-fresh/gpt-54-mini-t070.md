Sera crossed to the archive console before Ben could decide whether to stop her.

Her fingers went over the activation studs in a sequence so fast and sure that his own modern training looked clumsy by comparison. The console answered with a low, steady hum. Pale light climbed the surface, then unfolded into a holographic field above the archivist’s rail.

Ben had seen enough ruined systems in his life to know that a machine coming alive ought to look ordinary. This one did not. It looked remembered.

His chest tightened once, hard. The air in the archive had that recycled tang fortress levels always carried, cold durasteel under stale circulation, but the hum under it changed the room. Made the sealed Codex case near the wall seem heavier. Made Sera’s shoulders, squared and narrow in the glow, look carved out of the same stubborn metal as the console.

Desh let out a low breath beside him. “Well,” he said. “That is not a bad start.”

Ben almost laughed, which told him how far gone he already was. “Define bad.”

“Later,” Desh said.

The hologram sharpened. Lines of archaic Basic ran across it in tidy formal blocks, dense with legal markers and ritual phrasing Ben had never seen outside dusty archive scans. Sera did not glance at him. She read, and the set of her mouth went flatter by a fraction.

Torin stepped in on her other side as if he had been waiting for his turn all along. His hand moved over the display with easy familiarity, and a new index expanded from the first. More files. More cross-links. More lines stamped with the same pre-Reformation administrative seal.

“There,” he said, almost lightly. “That is the piece people stop looking for.”

Ben leaned in despite himself. He caught words in the header: Veranthos. Containment. Protocol. Several subclauses referenced chamber allocation, seal integrity, and an accounting term that made the back of his neck go tight because it sounded too much like people reduced to parts of a system.

“This is pre-Ruusan,” he said.

Torin’s mouth twitched. “Yes.”

“That was not a question.”

“No,” Torin said. “It was not.”

Ben kept his eyes on the display. The public history sat in his head like a corridor he had walked a hundred times. The Ruusan Reformations. The end of the Army of Light. The breaking of the Jedi Lords. Centralized training. Republic and Judicial oversight tightened around the Order until the old warlord structure was gone. That was the history his generation learned. The clean version. The version that survived because it deserved to.

This file sat under it like a buried conduit.

Desh’s fingers brushed the edge of the projection, then dropped back to his side. “You seeing the same thing I am?”

Ben tracked the linked headers. One line cross-referenced a seal ledger with a notation on personnel burden. Another linked to a field named under pressure terms he did not like the look of.

“I am seeing enough to dislike the shape of it,” he said.

“That makes two of us,” Desh said.

Kael had been silent near the far side of the console, narrow and still as a cut line in the light. Ben barely noticed him move until his hand came in from the side, past Sera’s shoulder, and tapped one line on the display.

The label changed.

Not architect.

Keeper.

Custodian.

Kael withdrew his hand as if he had never touched the thing. Then he said, “Architect is wrong.”

Sera’s head turned a fraction. Not enough to look at him fully. Enough to acknowledge the correction.

Ben stared at the revised label. The change was small. It landed like a blown hatch.

“Keeper,” he repeated.

Kael gave him a look that had all the friendliness of a closed door. “More accurate.”

Ben might have asked more, but the shape of the room would not let him. Kael did not offer an explanation. That, somehow, made the correction heavier. It was not a performance. It was a cut he had bothered to make and nothing more.

Torin, by contrast, was watching the files unfold with the expression of a man opening a box he had once hated and now could not stop touching.

“Veranthos ran the language different,” he said. “Formal. Too formal, probably. Everyone likes to wrap fear in ceremony when they think it sounds noble.”

Sera’s gaze stayed fixed on the text. “Ceremony does not make a lie less deliberate.”

“No,” Torin said. “It does not.”

Ben looked from one to the other. Sera held the display like a blade she knew how to use. Torin spoke around it as if trying to keep it from cutting anyone open all at once. Kael stood off to the side, all blunt edges and one precise correction. Three survivors. Three different weights in the same room. For the first time since Ben had woken them, he felt the separation between them as clearly as the cold under his boots.

That separation mattered.

Sera touched another column and a subfile expanded. Ben saw a chamber map, then a list of anchors. Twelve chamber-bound entries. One central designation beside them, keyed differently, flagged in a way that made the whole structure read less like a prison and more like a machine built around a living hinge.

Thirteen total, then. Not thirteen chambers. Twelve chambers and Gavran at the center.

Ben’s mouth went dry.

Desh saw the number too. “That is a lot of people to build into a lock.”

Sera did not answer him at once. When she did, her voice came out level and clipped. “It was not a lock. Not as they used the word later.”

Ben glanced at her. “Later?”

She looked at him then, and only for a beat. “The Veranthos protocol was containment. And penance.”

The words landed clean. No flourish. No apology. No attempt to soften them with history or motive.

Ben’s balance went wrong for half a second, not enough to move his feet, enough to make the archive deck feel pitched under him. Containment. Penance. The two words clicked together in his head, and behind them came the rest of the history he knew: Ruusan, the reforms, the public settlement that broke the Jedi Lords and bound the Order to something less dangerous than itself.

Not replaced. Carried.

Buried.

He heard himself say, “Reformations.”

Sera’s face did not change. Which was worse than if it had.

Ben kept looking at the display because looking at her directly felt like stepping into a wind tunnel. “The Ruusan Reformations,” he said, and now his own voice sounded strange to him, like he had dragged it out of some corridor in his memory. “The public settlement after the New Sith Wars. The demilitarization. Central training. Republic oversight. The end of the Jedi Lords.”

Desh glanced at him, then back at the archive. “You are doing the thing where you sound more certain because you are less certain.”

“I know,” Ben said. “I am enjoying it less than usual.”

That got a short snort out of Desh, the first hint of warmth since they had opened the archive. It eased nothing. It just kept the room from freezing.

Torin’s hand rested on the rail now, fingers spread with a familiarity that made Ben’s skin crawl before his mind could explain why. “The public settlement was real,” he said. “No one had to invent that part.”

Ben looked at him.

Torin’s smile was there, but thinner now. More habit than ease. “I know what you are thinking. It is rude to assume the galaxy only ever gets one bad idea at a time.”

Kael’s mouth moved by a fraction. “It often gets several.”

The dry line should have cut the tension. Instead it deepened it, because Kael said it like a man who had seen systems break and had no interest in flattering them.

Ben took a breath and let it out slower. The archive text kept shifting in front of him, links blooming, seals connecting. He could see the public history and this private one fitting together, not as truth and lie, but as two layers built by people who thought one could hide under the other forever.

That was the part that hit hardest. Not that Ruusan had happened. It had. Not that the Jedi had changed afterward. They had. It was that the history he had inherited was missing the thing sitting under his nose right now, the thing that made the later settlement feel less like an ending and more like a lid.

His stomach turned once, hard enough that he had to brace a hand on the console edge.

Sera noticed. Of course she did. Her eyes flicked to his hand, then back to the display.

“The public record was built over it,” she said.

Ben’s throat worked. “Built over what?”

Sera held his gaze for one second, then returned to the file. “A concealed Veranthos protocol. Containment and penance.”

The archive was very quiet except for the console hum and the soft, almost polite chime of the hologram cycling to the next linked page. Ben heard his own breathing in the silence. Heard Desh shift his weight. Heard Torin’s sleeve brush the rail when he folded his arms, like a man waiting for a story to finish going where it had to go.

Ben supplied the word before anyone else could. “Reformations.”

Sera did not answer. She did not need to.

Her face told him the rest.
