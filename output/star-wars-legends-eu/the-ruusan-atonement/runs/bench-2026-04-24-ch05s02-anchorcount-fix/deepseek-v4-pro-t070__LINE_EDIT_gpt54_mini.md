Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Her fingers found the activation sequence before Ben could even tell which panel she’d chosen — three quick touches, a fourth held a beat longer than the pattern required, then a full palm laid flat against what had looked like dead metal. The console answered with a low hum. Light bled through seams Ben hadn’t noticed, picking out lines he’d assumed were decorative scoring. The display surface flickered from dark to amber to pale blue, and the air above it unfolded into a holographic index written in a script Ben had to squint to make sense of.

Pre-Reformation administrative Basic. The characters were square and formal, the syntax padded with legal redundancies that had been stripped out of the language centuries before he was born.

Sera’s shoulders stayed level. Her breathing didn’t change. A thousand years had passed since she’d last touched that panel, and her body remembered faster than Ben’s modern training could have handled on unfamiliar hardware. The unease that had been pressing at the edge of his senses since they’d entered the fortress tightened into something he could finally name.

The archive wasn’t dead storage. It was a room the survivors had built, and it still answered to them.

“How much of the index is intact?” Sera asked.

The question wasn’t aimed at anyone in particular. Her eyes were already moving over the display, tracking lines of text at a speed that made Ben’s translation effort feel painfully slow.

Torin moved to her left shoulder. His hand brushed the console’s edge — not a caress, but the gesture of someone checking furniture in the dark to see if it had shifted. “The public record’s clean. I can see the Reformations index from here.” He pulled a subsection into the display, his fingers dragging through the holographic text with the same thousand-year-old familiarity. “Demilitarization protocols. Army of Light dissolution order. Jedi Lord authority sunset. Centralized training mandate — that one took three years of argument before the Council signed it, and another two before anyone actually moved the younglings to Coruscant.”

He wasn’t lecturing. His voice carried the warmth of someone describing an argument he’d lived through, along with the frustration, the personalities, the compromise that had left nobody happy. Ben had heard that same register from his father when Luke described the early years of the New Jedi Order. The difference was that Luke’s stories were about building something. Torin’s were about dismantling it.

“Republic oversight framework,” Torin went on, scrolling deeper. “That was the last piece. The Senate wanted binding authority over Jedi deployments. The Council wanted operational independence. They settled on a liaison structure that satisfied neither side and functioned adequately for about forty years before everyone forgot why they’d been angry in the first place.”

Desh had positioned himself near the door, arms crossed. His face was professionally neutral, but Ben caught the tension at the corner of his jaw — the look of a man who’d learned to recognize when intelligence was being gathered in a language he couldn’t read. “The public record,” Desh said. “Meaning there’s a private one.”

Sera didn’t answer. Her fingers had gone still on the display.

Kael reached past her.

The motion hit so abruptly that Ben’s hand twitched toward his lightsaber before his brain caught up. Kael had been silent since they’d entered the archive — silent in a way that suggested speech required a deliberate choice to break pattern. Now his arm extended between Sera and Torin, one finger tapping a line near the bottom of the index.

“That word is wrong,” he said.

Sera leaned closer. “Which—”

“There.” He tapped again. “The descriptor. It should be ‘containment,’ not ‘settlement.’ The original draft used ‘containment.’ Someone changed it after we went under.”

He stepped back. His hands returned to his sides. He didn’t explain the correction. He didn’t cite a source or add a qualifier or soften the implication. He simply made the change and withdrew, the way a monk might correct a single note in a chant and then fall silent again.

Ben felt the correction hit him in the chest before his mind caught up to the meaning. After we went under. Kael hadn’t been reading the index as history but as a document he’d seen before — a draft he remembered well enough to notice when someone had altered it. The survivors weren’t a single voice. They were three separate people, and the division between them was the first honest thing Ben had felt since they’d stepped into the archive.

Sera straightened. She turned from the console to face Ben, and her expression held the same composed stillness it had since she’d woken — the discipline of someone who’d learned to keep her face level through conditions that would have shown cracks in most people.

“The Ruusan Reformations were public reform,” she said. “They were also private penance. The settlement the galaxy remembers was real. The containment protocol beneath it was never meant to be read by anyone outside this fortress.”

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t offer context, apology, or the softer frame that might have made the sentence easier to absorb. She stated the fact and left it hanging in the air between them.

Ben felt it land. Not as an idea his mind could neatly sort out — that part was still stumbling over the implications — but as a physical drop in his center of gravity, the sick lurch of stepping onto what looked like solid ground and finding it tilt under him. The history Luke’s Order had inherited was true. The demilitarization, the end of the Jedi Lords, the centralized training, the Republic oversight — all of it had happened. But the records had been edited at the source, and the omission wasn’t accidental. It was structural. The post-Ruusan settlement had been built to carry a concealed wound beneath its legitimate public reforms, and Ben was standing in the room where the concealment had been designed.

Sera hadn’t said what the penance was for. She didn’t need to. Ben could read the rest of it off her face — the century-of-discipline composure carrying something heavier than she was willing to name in front of him.

He looked at Torin. The storyteller’s warmth was still there, but it had settled into something quieter — a man who had kept a secret so long that telling it felt less like relief than reopening an old injury.

He looked at Kael. The monk’s gaze rested on the display, on the single word he’d corrected, and his silence was no longer the silence of someone with nothing to say. It was the silence of someone who’d said the one thing that mattered and was waiting for the room to catch up.

Sera the instrument. Torin the keeper. Kael the correction.

The archive hummed around them. The display flickered with text Ben could barely read — a thousand years of institutional memory written in a script his Order had forgotten how to teach. The Codex fragment sat sealed in its case at the edge of the console, faintly lit, unopened. Ben didn’t need to open it. The testimony was standing in front of him, delivered by three people who’d waited a millennium to say it, and the weight of it was still settling into his shoulders.

He held himself ready for the next blow. The survivors weren’t finished. The penance had a cost, and the cost had a name, and the name was somewhere in the records Sera hadn’t opened yet. But the picture had changed. He was no longer standing in a room with three survivors. He was standing in a room with three witnesses to the part of Jedi history he most needed to trust, and the history they were showing him was incomplete exactly where it mattered most.
