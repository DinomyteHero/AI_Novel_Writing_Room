Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Her fingers found the activation sequence before Ben could identify which panel she was reaching for — three contacts in rapid succession, a fourth held for a beat longer than the rhythm required, then a full palm pressed flat against what had looked like dead metal. The console hummed. Light bled up through seams Ben hadn't noticed, tracing lines he'd assumed were decorative scoring. The display surface flickered from dark to amber to pale blue, and the air above it folded open into a holographic index written in a script Ben had to squint at to parse.

Pre-Reformation administrative Basic. The characters were square and formal, the syntax carrying legal redundancies that had been stripped out of the language centuries before he was born.

Sera's shoulders stayed level. Her breathing hadn't changed. A thousand years since she'd last touched that panel, and her body remembered the sequence faster than Ben's modern training could have executed a cold start on unfamiliar hardware. The wrongness that had been pressing at the edges of his perception since they'd entered the fortress tightened into something he could name.

The archive wasn't a dead repository. It was a room the survivors had built, and it still answered to them.

"How much of the index is intact?" Sera asked. The question wasn't directed at anyone. She was reading the display, her eyes tracking across lines of text at a speed that made Ben's translation effort feel like a child sounding out letters.

Torin moved to her left shoulder. His hand brushed the console's edge — not a caress, exactly. The gesture of someone confirming furniture hadn't shifted in the dark. "The public record's clean. I can see the Reformations index from here." He pulled a subsection into the display, his fingers dragging through the holographic text with the same thousand-year-old fluency. "Demilitarization protocols. Army of Light dissolution order. Jedi Lord authority sunset. Centralized training mandate — that one took three years of argument before the Council signed it, and another two before anyone actually moved the younglings to Coruscant."

He wasn't lecturing. His voice carried the particular warmth of someone describing an argument he'd been in the room for — the remembered frustration, the personalities involved, the compromise that had left nobody happy. Ben had heard the same register from his father describing the early years of the New Jedi Order. The difference was that Luke's stories were about building something. Torin's were about dismantling it.

"Republic oversight framework," Torin continued, scrolling deeper. "That was the last piece. The Senate wanted binding authority over Jedi deployments. The Council wanted to retain operational independence. They settled on a liaison structure that satisfied neither party and functioned adequately for about forty years before everyone forgot why they'd been angry about it in the first place."

Desh had positioned himself near the door, arms crossed. His face was professionally neutral, but Ben caught the slight tension at the corner of his jaw — the look of a man who'd learned to recognize when intelligence was being gathered in a language he couldn't read. "The public record," Desh said. "Meaning there's a private one."

Sera didn't answer. Her fingers had stilled on the display.

Kael reached past her.

The motion was so unexpected that Ben's hand twitched toward his lightsaber before his brain caught up. Kael had been silent since they'd entered the archive — silent in the particular way of someone who'd been silent so long that speech required a conscious decision to break pattern. Now his arm extended between Sera and Torin, one finger tapping a line of text near the bottom of the index.

"That word is wrong," he said.

Sera leaned closer. "Which — "

"There." He tapped again. "The descriptor. It should be 'containment,' not 'settlement.' The original draft used 'containment.' Someone edited it after we went under."

He stepped back. His hands returned to his sides. He didn't explain the correction. He didn't cite a source or provide context or soften the implication with a qualifying phrase. He simply made the change and withdrew, the way a monk might correct a single note in a choir and then resume silence.

Ben felt the correction land somewhere in his chest before his mind caught up to what it meant. *After we went under.* Kael had been reading the index not as history but as a document he'd seen before — a draft he remembered, a wording he'd known well enough to notice when someone had changed it. The survivors weren't unified in their testimony. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth, and the separation itself was the first honest thing Ben had felt since they'd entered the fortress.

Sera straightened. She turned from the console to face Ben, and her expression was the same composed stillness it had been since she'd woken — the discipline of someone who'd learned to hold her face level under conditions that would have broken most people into visible reaction.

"The Ruusan Reformations were public reform," she said. "They were also private penance. The settlement the galaxy remembers was real. The containment protocol beneath it was never meant to be read by anyone outside this fortress."

She didn't elaborate. She didn't offer context or apology or the softening frame that would have made the sentence easier to absorb. She simply stated the fact and let it sit in the air between them.

Ben felt it land. Not as an intellectual proposition — his mind was still struggling to assemble the implications into coherent shape — but as a physical drop in his center of gravity, the vertigo of stepping onto ground that looked solid and feeling it give. The history Luke's Order had inherited was true. The demilitarization, the end of the Jedi Lords, the centralized training, the Republic oversight — all of it had happened. But the records had been edited at the source, and the omission wasn't accidental. It was structural. The post-Ruusan settlement had been built to carry a concealed wound beneath its legitimate public reforms, and Ben was standing in the room where the concealment had been designed.

Sera hadn't said what the penance was for. She didn't need to. Ben could read the rest of it off her face — the century-of-discipline composure that was holding something heavier than she was willing to name in front of him.

He looked at Torin. The storyteller's warmth was still there, but it had settled into something quieter — the register of a man who'd kept a secret long enough that telling it felt less like relief than like reopening a wound.

He looked at Kael. The monk's gaze was on the display, on the single word he'd corrected, and his silence was no longer the silence of someone with nothing to say. It was the silence of someone who'd said the one thing that mattered and was waiting for the room to catch up.

Sera the instrument. Torin the keeper. Kael the correction.

The archive hummed around them. The display flickered with text Ben could barely read — a thousand years of institutional memory written in a script his Order had forgotten how to teach. The Codex fragment sat sealed in its case at the edge of the console, faintly lit, unopened. Ben didn't need to open it. The testimony was standing in front of him, delivered by three people who'd waited a millennium to say it, and the weight of what they'd said was still settling into his shoulders.

He braced for what came next. The survivors hadn't finished. The penance had a cost, and the cost had a name, and the name was somewhere in the records Sera hadn't pulled up yet. But the operating picture had changed. He was no longer standing in a room with three survivors. He was standing in a room with three witnesses to the part of Jedi history he most needed to trust, and the history they were showing him was incomplete exactly where it mattered most.
