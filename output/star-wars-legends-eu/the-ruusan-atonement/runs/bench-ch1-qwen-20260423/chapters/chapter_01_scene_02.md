Luke slid the datapad across the desk. It stopped against the holocomm housing with a soft click.

“Seventeen reports,” Luke said. “Flagged over the last six weeks. All from the same bearing.”

Ben caught the edge of the device before it tipped. The casing still held warmth from Luke’s hand. He kept his palm on the screen for a beat and tracked the low, steady wash of Coruscant traffic bleeding through the transparisteel. The office smelled of recycled air and old paper. A working building on a working planet. Somewhere down the corridor, a turbolift chimed; farther off, boots crossed the floor tiles.

“Survey vessels,” Luke continued. “Standard outer-rim sweeps. They’re logging timing drift in their nav-computers. Sensor ghosts. Routine maintenance flags, mostly.”

“Mostly.” Ben tapped the screen awake. The index loaded. Seventeen entries, clustered in a tight arc past the Darrpa Sector. He scrolled past the first five. The timestamps matched the rotation schedule for the outer terraces.

“Someone noticed a pattern,” Ben said.

“I flagged a pattern,” Luke said. “The analysis droids filed them under background noise until I overrode the filter.”

Ben pulled up the first raw file. Telemetry graphs scrolled past. He ignored the numbers and watched the Force-read column. The graphs dipped. Not a drop. A drag, as if the data had to push through water before it could reach him.

He zoomed the display. Three readings stood out. The timestamps aligned with his own missed footings in the salle. The same sour note under the Temple’s usual rhythm. The same wrongness he had been carrying for weeks, pressed into the back of his thoughts until it had become almost familiar.

He kept his posture neutral. The data was concrete now. Institutional fact. The Order had it on record.

“Sensor ghosts don’t cluster,” Ben said. His voice stayed level. “They scatter. This isn’t scatter.”

“No.” Luke’s hands rested on the desk, calm and steady. “It’s a bearing.”

Ben scrolled to the third file. The graph spiked, then flattened. He knew the shape of that glitch as soon as he saw it: a missed parry, a delayed catch, a breath held too long. The datapad recorded the deviation. It didn’t record the lag in his muscles.

“You’re sending a team to check it,” Ben said.

“I’m sending a Knight.” Luke leaned back in his chair. The leather gave a low creak. “One survey run. Map the drift, verify the sensors, log the findings. Standard protocol.”

Ben’s thumb hovered over the screen. He didn’t ask why the Grand Master was handing him a routine survey instead of routing it through the duty roster. He already knew the answer, and neither of them needed to put it in the air.

“I’ve felt it too,” Luke said.

The words landed cleanly. No flourish. No warning.

Ben looked up. Luke’s face was the same as when he had walked in: open, patient, carrying the gravity Ben had stopped trying to measure against his own.

“How long?” Ben asked.

“Long enough to know it isn’t fading.” Luke held his gaze. “Long enough to stop pretending it’s an echo.”

The gap between them opened, sudden and clear. Luke was describing an anomaly, the way a Grand Master would: careful, restrained, clinical. Ben was looking at a register he had lived inside since he was a boy. The sourness wasn’t an echo. It was a wound. And the wound had a direction.

Something in his chest eased, then tightened again. Luke had seen it. Of course he had. That seeing was a gift. It was also a trap. If Ben named what it was, the mission would change. The roster would open. The distance would close.

Ben set the datapad down on the desk. The blue glow slid off his hands.

“I’ll take it,” Ben said.

Luke gave a single nod. “All right.”

Ben stood and crossed to the window. The transparisteel was cool against his shoulder. Below, the city stretched away in a grid of light, exhaust, and endless motion. Traffic streams merged and split, following lanes that never changed.

“Desh can fly the run,” Ben said. “He’s at Terminus Station. Two days out.”

“I’ll clear the route.” Luke didn’t push. He didn’t ask why Ben hadn’t called for a Jedi partner. He didn’t ask why the wrongness needed to be handled by a Knight and a pilot instead of a full detachment.

Ben watched a transport bank through the upper atmosphere. The bearing on the screen pointed straight into the dark beyond the Rim. The wrongness pulled that way. Active. Not ambient.

“Pack your kit,” Luke said. “File the departure manifest before you leave the building.”

“Yes, sir.”

The title hung between them, formal and necessary. Ben kept his shoulders loose, his breathing even. The mission was his. The distance was necessary. He let the silence settle in place, real and unbroken, and braced for the handoff.
