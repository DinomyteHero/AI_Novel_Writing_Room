The cockpit smelled of recycled air and warm durasteel. Preflight sequences scrolled across the nav display in steady green columns. Every system nominal. Every switch seated. The Jedi Temple hangar bay hummed around him, service droids rolling over the deck plates while two techs argued over a coolant manifold three bays down. Ben kept both hands on the yoke until the last checklist line cleared. Then he pulled his comlink from his belt.

The duty roster lived on the first screen. Three Knight contacts, all cleared for outer-rim survey work. All vetted by Temple protocol. Ben’s thumb hovered over the glass. It did not tremble. It slid past the roster, tapped his personal directory, and stopped on a name he had not dialed in six months.

The motion took less than a second. It felt like sealing a blast door behind him. Efficient. Necessary. He pressed call before his mind could dress the choice in justification.

Static hissed through the speaker. Then a voice cut through, flat and familiar.
“Skywalker. You’re calling from a secure line, which means either you’re buying me dinner or you’re in trouble.”
“Survey run,” Ben said. “Outer Rim vector. I need a second seat.”
A pause. The kind that measured distance, not hesitation. “How bad is the drift?”
“I don’t know yet. That’s the problem.”
“Terminus Station?”
“Two days out. I’ll file the routing paperwork. Just bring your own flight suit.”
Desh’s laugh was short, dry, and completely unconcerned. “I’ll pack the extra ration packs. You always forget to eat when the nav-computer starts arguing with you.”
“See you in forty-eight hours.”
“Copy. End line.”

The comm clicked off. Ben set it on the console and let one shoulder loosen, then the other. The tension of expectation—the weight of a roster he would have had to explain himself to—drained out into the recycled air. He reached up and drew the harness straps snug, the buckles snapping into place with a solid mechanical click. They had worn the same gray uniforms at sixteen. They had learned the same rules in the same narrow corridors. The Galactic Alliance Guard had dissolved into the archives, but the habit of operating without asking permission had survived. It was the only currency they still spent.

Ben keyed the launch sequence. Repulsors engaged with a low resonant thrum that vibrated through the deck plates. The hangar bay’s overhead rigging retracted, clearing the approach vector. He pushed the throttle forward, and the craft lifted from the deck, angling toward the opening in the transparisteel canopy.

Coruscant’s traffic streams parted around him. The city sprawled below in a grid of exhaust, durasteel, and endless motion. Ben kept his touch light on the controls, feeling the craft answer through the feedback levers. Standard ascent. Standard clearance. The Temple receded in the rear sensors, a monument of stone and protocol shrinking into the atmospheric haze.

The wrongness should have faded with the distance. Force disturbances usually thinned as a craft crossed orbital thresholds, losing their grip on the local field. This one did not. It pressed harder. A steady drag against his sternum, like a hand tugging at his tunic. Ben worked his jaw, adjusted the trim, watched the altimeter climb, and tried to breathe through it. The pressure did not scatter. It gathered.

He lined the craft with the Ruusan Corridor jump point. The nav-computer plotted a three-stage hop past the Darrpa Sector, standard routing for uncharted survey vectors. Ben locked the coordinates. He pushed the hyperdrive lever forward.

The engines screamed. The cockpit shuddered as the inertial dampeners fought the transition. Stars stretched into pale, luminous lines. The viewport washed blue, then collapsed into the familiar starless tunnel of hyperspace. Instrument panels flickered, recalibrating to the new spatial geometry.

Ben braced himself against the seat. He expected the usual muffled stillness of hyperspace travel—the way the Force generally receded into a distant static whisper when a craft crossed into subspace. It did not recede.

It sharpened.

The drag against his chest tightened into a clear, unyielding line. It was no longer vague pressure. It had a bearing. It pulled toward the port viewport, steady and absolute, cutting through the artificial stillness of the jump like a tether going taut. Ben’s fingers tightened on the yoke. He did not loosen them. The wrongness in the Force shifted as he left Coruscant—not diminishing but acquiring direction, like a compass needle settling.
