The Steady Returns slid into orbit like a knife through silk, Ruusan's third moon filling the viewport with familiar blues and greens. Ben leaned against the transparisteel, watching continents scroll past. Looked like a thousand settlement worlds he'd visited with his parents—temperate zones, cultivated river valleys, the geometric scars of agriculture healed into patchwork forests.

"Atmosphere reads clean," Desh announced from the sensor console. "Nitrogen-oxygen balance, trace argon. No industrial contaminants, no—" His fingers stilled over the controls. "Huh."

Ben didn't turn. "Problem?"

"Biomass calculations are off. Way off."

Sera stepped closer, her boots ringing against the deck plating. "Define 'off.'"

"Planet's showing forty-three percent of expected vertebrate biomass. Flora reads normal, but fauna..." Desh's voice flattened into the tone Ben recognized from too many battlefield briefings. "It's like something shaved two-thirds of the animals off the surface and forgot to tell the plants."

The viewport showed a pristine wilderness. Birds should have been visible from orbit—flocks darkening the sky, the movement of herds along grasslands. Ben saw nothing. Just green, and blue, and the wrongness of perfect stillness.

They set down in a clearing that had once been a landing field. Native grasses, waist-high, swayed in wind that didn't touch the treeline. Ben's boots sank into soil that felt spongy, resilient. The air tasted clean. Too clean.

"No insects," Kael said quietly. He'd been scanning the ground since the ramp lowered, blaster holstered but hand resting near the grip. "No buzzing. No crawling things under the grass."

Torin knelt, fingers brushing a cluster of white flowers growing in deep shadow beneath a spreading oak. "These shouldn't bloom here. Not enough light." He looked up at Ben. "Wrong species for the region, too. Mountain flowers. Growing at sea level."

Ben reached for the Force.

The technique was automatic—extend perception, feel the living web that connected every organic thing, read the health of the ecosystem through its resonance. He'd done it since childhood, first by instinct, later by training. The Force answered like breath after diving.

Except it didn't.

Energy existed. Potential crackled in the air like static before a storm. But the structure—the lattice that gave direction and meaning to Force perception—had been stripped away. Ben's awareness fired into nothing, questing tendrils grasping smoke. The energy was there, raw and humming, but it had no shape. No texture. No light or dark.

He staggered.

"Ben?" Sera's hand caught his elbow. Her grip felt distant, like touching something through thick gloves. "What—"

"The Force." His voice came out thin. "It's... wrong."

"Wrong how?"

He couldn't answer. How did you explain color to someone who'd only seen grayscale? How did you describe the absence of music to someone born deaf? The Force wasn't gone—it was present in ways that made its absence unbearable. Like trying to read in a library where all the books had been translated into a language that used familiar letters to mean different things.

Desh pulled out his chrono. Old-fashioned mechanical piece, no electronics to scramble. The second hand ticked forward, hesitated, skipped backward two seconds, then resumed its march. He held it up without comment.

"Chronometric drift," Torin breathed. "In an atmosphere?"

"Felt it since we landed." Desh's scarred face could have been carved from stone. "Like walking through a memory of a place. Déjà vu without the original experience."

"Grief," Kael said. He'd moved to the edge of the clearing, one palm flat against an oak trunk. "Not mine. Not anyone's. Just... grief. Without an object."

Ben forced himself to breathe. Jedi techniques—centering exercises, emotional regulation, the meditation practices that had sustained him through his parents' absence, through Jacen's fall, through every mission where he'd walked into darkness expecting to die—they required feedback. The Force provided context for your own thoughts, helped you distinguish between productive caution and paralyzing fear. Between legitimate anger and the kind that led to the dark side.

Without the lattice, he was alone with his own mind.

The thoughts came sharp and unfiltered. This was Veraine's field test—she'd done this deliberately, stripped an entire world down to its component Force-energy to see what would happen. How many people had lived here? How many had died when their connection to the living Force sheared away? Children who'd never learned to shield themselves, elderly Force-sensitives whose life-support systems had depended on their ability to maintain meditation trances?

He could feel the shape of her logic: if the lattice was artificial, removing it revealed truth. If moral intuition was imposed rather than discovered, stripping it away revealed authentic choice. She'd murdered a planet to test a philosophical proposition.

The anger felt different without the Force to contextualize it. Raw. Personal. Not the clean burn that fueled lightsaber forms but something that lived in his bones, that whispered about the satisfaction of wrapping his hands around her throat and squeezing until her superior smile cracked.

Ben closed his eyes. In the darkness behind his eyelids, he could hear himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
