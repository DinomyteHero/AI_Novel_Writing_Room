The *Steady Return* broke orbit, and Ben watched the settlement world fill the forward viewport.

It looked like a gardening holo. Green continents, blue oceans, white cloud swirls over temperate zones. A dozen colony beacons would have dotted that coastline, a million Human lives living quiet, productive lives under a yellow sun. He’d seen a hundred worlds just like it.

“That’s not what the scanners say,” Desh announced from the copilot’s seat, his voice flat.

Ben kept his eyes on the planet. “Run it again.”

“Ran it twice. Atmospheric composition reads nominal. Temperature bands nominal. Spectral reflectance nominal.” Desh tapped a screen. “Biosphere scan shows zero complex life signs. Not low. Zero. No mammals, no reptiles, no avians. Microbial and plant life only, and the plant profiles are…” He trailed off, studying the data. “They’re wrong.”

“Define wrong.”

“Chlorophyll signatures are inverted. Growth patterns in the spectral analysis suggest photosynthesis occurring in near-total darkness. Root structures registering above ground.” Desh swiveled his chair. “The planet is a perfect lie. It looks right. It isn’t.”

Behind them, Sera leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed. “Veraine’s test site.”

“Has to be,” Torin said. He stood beside her, his usual warm demeanor subdued. “A full-scale collapse. She didn’t just damage a node. She removed it.”

Kael, already suited up in his worn flight gear, stared at the planet. He said nothing.

Ben guided the ship into the atmosphere. The ride was smooth, too smooth. No turbulence, no atmospheric shear. The sky bled from black to blue without a flicker of static on the shields. They broke through the cloud layer over what should have been a primary settlement.

The city was there. Buildings stood intact—prefab durasteel and local stone, streets laid out in a sensible grid. Parks dotted the districts. From this altitude, it looked orderly. Abandoned, but orderly.

“No wreckage,” Ben observed. “No signs of panic. They just… stopped.”

He set the *Steady Return* down in a central square large enough to serve as a landing pad. The ship’s landing struts settled on permacrete with a solid thunk. The external atmosphere reading flashed green: breathable, standard pressure, ideal humidity.

The silence after engine shutdown pressed against the hull.

“Full environment suits,” Sera said. It wasn’t a question.

“Readings say it’s safe,” Desh noted.

“The readings said the planet had birds,” she replied.

They suited up. Ben sealed his jacket, checked the breath mask hanging loose around his neck. The ramp descended with a hydraulic sigh.

The air that washed in smelled of damp soil and blooming flowers. Normal. Ben’s skin prickled.

He stepped out first.

The square was clean. No debris, no discarded belongings. Ben’s boots echoed on the permacrete. He turned a slow circle, taking in the buildings. Windows intact. Doors closed. A child’s brightly colored ball rested against a planter box, as if dropped moments before.

Then he saw the vines.

They crawled up the western face of a three-story apartment block, thick and green. Normal for a temperate world. Except they grew in precise, geometric spirals, each tendril mirroring the next in a pattern too exact for nature. And they grew away from the sun. The shadowed eastern wall was bare stone.

“Look at the flowers,” Kael said quietly.

Ben followed his gaze to a decorative bed lining the square. The flowers were a vibrant purple, petals wide open. They bloomed in the deep shadow of a building, while the flowers planted in full sunlight were tightly closed buds, their stems bent away from the light.

A chill settled at the base of Ben’s spine. Not fear—not yet. A professional recognition of a system operating on rules he didn’t know.

He reached for the Force.

The action was instinct, ingrained since childhood: extend awareness, feel the living flow of energy, let it paint the world in textures of intention and emotion. He braced for the expected shockwave of wrongness, for the screaming wound he’d felt at other damaged nodes.

Nothing.

No, not nothing. That was the horror.

The Force was there. He could feel its presence, a potential thrumming beneath his feet, in the air, inside his own blood. But it had no texture. No direction. It was energy without current, light without shadow. He reached for guidance and his perception fired outward into a formless void, finding no purchase, no echo. The handrail he hadn’t even known he leaned on was gone. He was falling inward.

Ben took a sharp breath.

“Ben?” Torin’s voice came from beside him, muffled by the surreal quiet.

“Do you feel it?” Sera asked. Her tone was tight.

“I feel… something.” Ben kept his voice even. “It’s not gone. It’s formless. No light, no dark. No signature at all.”

He tried to feel Sera’s presence beside him, the familiar disciplined focus of her mind. He felt only the same blank potential. He could see her, but the Force reported nothing of her. She was a silhouette.

Desh walked a few paces ahead, then stopped. He pulled a chrono from his belt—a simple, reliable mechanical model, not a digital readout. He stared at it, then held it up. The second hand ticked steadily, then jumped backward two seconds, ticked, jumped forward one, ticked, stuttered in place.

“Chrono drift,” Desh said. “Measurable. Local time isn’t stable.” He put the chrono away and looked around the square, his expression grim. “And there’s a… weight. Not physical. It settles in your chest. Like you’ve lost something vital but you can’t remember what it was. Grief with no object.”

Kael had moved to the edge of the square, peering down a side street. “No birds,” he said. “No insects. Just the plants growing wrong.”

“The lattice is gone here,” Sera said. Her voice held a reverence that bordered on dread. “Completely. This is what she wants. Everywhere.”

Ben closed his eyes, trying to center himself. *There is no emotion, there is peace.* The mantra surfaced from memory. He reached for the peace, for the tranquil pool of the Force that should have welled up in response to the discipline.

The formless potential offered no feedback. No peace, no conflict. No affirmation, no denial. It was a mirror with no reflection. The silence in his head was absolute, and in that silence, his own thoughts rang too loud, too sharp.

He opened his eyes. “We need to see more.”

They moved as a group down a main thoroughfare. The storefronts were intact. A café had tables set, cups still on the saucers. The liquid inside was dried to dust. Ben saw a speeder parked neatly at a charging station, its door ajar.

The wrongness deepened with every step. Trees grew in perfect, alternating spirals of branches, left then right, a mathematical sequence. Moss grew on the sunny sides of rocks. A public fountain stood dry, but the basin was full of lush, green ferns that pulsed gently, though there was no breeze.

Ben kept reaching for the Force, a nervous tic he couldn’t suppress. Each time, the same hollow echo. His moral intuition—the subtle, Force-mediated sense of right-path, wrong-path that had guided him since he was a boy—was silent. The compass needle was gone, leaving him with only the naked map of his own judgment.

“This was a test,” Torin said, his voice heavy. “She picked a settlement world to see if the removal would hold. To see what would happen to a living biosphere when the structure was pulled out.”

“It’s still alive,” Kael observed, touching a leaf that curled away from his finger. “It’s just… following different rules.”

“The rules matter,” Sera shot back. “Structure isn’t prison. It’s what allows complexity. Without it, you get this.” She gestured at the geometrically perfect vines. “Simplification. Pattern without purpose.”

Desh checked his chrono again. “Drift’s increasing the deeper we go. And the weight… it’s getting heavier.”

Ben felt it too, a mounting pressure behind his eyes. Not a Force sensation. A purely physiological response to a universe that had lost its grammar. He tried another Jedi exercise, envisioning a light within, seeking to connect it to the light without.

The light within burned alone. The without was neither light nor dark. It was a void that refused to be empty, a presence that refused to communicate.

He stopped walking. They were in a small residential park. A playground stood to one side, swings motionless. The grass was a uniform, vivid green, each blade the exact same height.

“This is what she felt,” Ben said, the realization arriving cold and clean. “For a thousand years. Not nothing. This. The potential without the path.”

“And she called it liberation,” Sera whispered.

Torin put a hand on Ben’s shoulder. A human gesture of comfort. Ben saw the concern in his eyes, but he couldn’t *feel* the paternal warmth that usually accompanied it. He only saw the gesture, and had to interpret it. The delay was disorienting.

“We have the data point,” Torin said. “We’ve seen it. We should return to the ship. Plan our next move.”

Ben nodded, but he didn’t move. He was listening.

In the silence of the de-structured Force, he heard himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them.

*This is easier than I expected.*

*The silence is clean.*

*Why is that frightening?*

*What if she’s right?*

*What if the handrail was just a crutch?*

*I could do anything here, and no one would know. Not even me.*

The thoughts were his. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
