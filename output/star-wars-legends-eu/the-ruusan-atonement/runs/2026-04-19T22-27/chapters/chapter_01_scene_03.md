Preflight was complete. Ben sat deep in the pilot’s seat, datapad propped against his thigh, and looked at the roster.

Fourteen Jedi Knights, all available, all green on the duty board. Three he’d worked with before. Rendell was probably still in the training salle. He could comm her now, give her an hour to throw a kit together, and depart by oh-six-hundred with a full partner, exactly the arrangement protocol preferred and Luke had very carefully not required.

He scrolled past all of them and opened his personal contacts.

The hangar kept moving around him: an anti-grav dolly whining past his landing strut, two mechanics arguing over a fuel coupling three bays down, the recycled-air tang of a thousand departures worked into the durasteel. Ordinary Temple evening. It already felt distant, as if he had left before the ship did.

His thumb paused over Desh Rolan’s entry.

He tapped it.

The long-range comm took four seconds to connect. Static scratched the line. Then: “Rolan.”

“It’s Ben. I need a second on an Outer Rim survey run. Route’s already plotted. You’d be flying with me.”

A pause. Short. Measuring. “How bad?”

“Don’t know yet. That’s the problem.”

“Duration?”

“Open-ended. Two weeks minimum, probably more.”

“Pay?”

“Standard Jedi consultant rate. I’ll clear the paperwork.”

“When do you need me?”

Ben flicked a glance at the console chrono. “An hour and a half.”

“I’ll be there in forty minutes,” Desh said. “Don’t leave without me.”

The comm clicked dead.

Ben set the datapad on the copilot’s seat and let out a breath that seemed to release somewhere between his ribs and his shoulders. His muscles eased by degrees, and only then did he realize how hard he’d been holding them. Desh hadn’t asked why Ben was calling him instead of pulling a Knight off the duty board. Hadn’t asked what kind of survey wanted an ex-intelligence operative instead of a combat-rated Jedi partner. Hadn’t asked anything that would force Ben to answer with more honesty than he was interested in supplying.

That was Desh. The Guard had trained them both to hear the shape of what wasn’t said, and Desh had always been good at knowing when the missing explanation was the explanation. They’d spent three years in a service where the wrong question could get people killed. The habit had survived the uniform, along with the habit of not reaching for things that might need saying out loud.

Ben checked the fuel readouts. All nominal. He had known they would be. He checked them anyway.

---

Desh arrived in thirty-seven minutes with a kit bag over one shoulder and a thermal caf in each hand. He handed one through the hatch without comment, dropped into the copilot’s seat, and began his own preflight sweep of the secondary systems. He didn’t ask for the mission brief. Ben slid the datapad over with the survey route displayed, and Desh read it in silence. That was the briefing.

“Outer Rim’s a big place,” Desh said at last.

“The route’s specific.”

“Mm.” Desh set the datapad on the console between them. “Seventeen flags on the same bearing. Luke’s notation?”

“Yes.”

“You’ve felt it longer than he has.”

Not a question. Ben turned his head and looked at him.

Desh wore the same expression he’d had in a dozen Guard debriefs: attentive, flat, waiting. He had always been the one in the room who noticed what the Force-sensitives were doing before they noticed it themselves. Not because he could feel what they felt. Because he watched their hands.

“Yeah,” Ben said. “Longer.”

Desh gave a single nod and went back to the secondary board. “Then let’s go find out what it is.”

Ben engaged the repulsorlifts.

---

The Temple hangar dropped away beneath them, then the upper levels, then the whole district—towers, traffic lanes, the amber wash of a city that never paused for anything. Ben held the departure angle and watched Coruscant slide past the viewport. From up here it looked the way it always did: vast, indifferent, utterly certain of its own permanence. He had left it a hundred times. It had never once noticed.

“You’re doing the quiet thing,” Desh said.

“I’m flying.”

“You can fly and talk. I’ve seen you do it.”

“I’m thinking.”

“That’s the bad kind of quiet.” Desh took a pull from his caf. “For the record.”

Ben didn’t answer. Desh let it go. The city dwindled below them, the upper atmosphere thinned to black, and then they were in orbit. Ben keyed in the hyperspace coordinates.

“Jumping in thirty seconds,” he said.

“Ready.”

The countdown ran across the console. Ben settled his hands on the controls and felt the hyperdrive spool up through the deck plates, a rising vibration under his palms and boots. The wrongness sat at the edge of his awareness—patient, as it had always been. He’d learned to carry it the way you carried an old injury: not constant pain, just a flaw in the motion, always there once you knew where to look.

Ten seconds.

For a heartbeat, he thought the jump would strip it away. Hyperspace had smoothed other things, after all: interference, static, the noise of a busy system bleeding across his senses. Maybe this would go the same way. Maybe he’d come out the far side with a cleaner Force-sense, a survey route, and a problem he could treat like any other problem.

The stars stretched.

It didn’t wash out.

The wrongness didn’t fade. It didn’t scatter. It turned inward and sharpened, like a blurred image snapping into focus. Ben’s hands locked on the controls. Blue-white hyperspace flooded the viewport, and beneath that bright tunnel, beneath the engine hum and Desh’s steady presence beside him, the wrongness pulled.

Not ambient. Not leftover. Directional.

It was pointing somewhere. It had been pointing somewhere all along, and he’d lined himself up with it since oh-six-hundred without knowing it, and the bearing on his nav display matched the pull exactly, degree for degree.

Ben sat very still.

“Ben.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’ve got the look.”

“What look.”

“The one where something’s happening in the Force and you’re deciding whether to tell me about it.” Desh set his caf down. “You don’t have to. But the look’s there.”

Ben kept his eyes on the hyperspace tunnel. The pull had settled now, steady and sure. Patient, the way the wrongness had always been patient—except now it wasn’t waiting. It had direction.

“The disturbance,” he said. “The thing I’ve been feeling. It doesn’t go away in hyperspace.”

Desh was quiet a beat. “Should it?”

“Yes.”

“But it didn’t.”

“No.” Ben paused. “It got clearer.”

He didn’t say it’s pointing. He didn’t say I think it has been for years and I’ve only just noticed. Some things had to sit a while before they could be spoken, and this one had barely finished arriving.

Desh picked up his caf again. “Okay,” he said.

Just that. Okay. The Guard had taught them both the value of acknowledgments that stopped there.

Ben turned back to the controls. The wrongness held its bearing as they left Coruscant behind—not diminishing, not easing, but settling into direction, like a compass needle finding north and staying there.
