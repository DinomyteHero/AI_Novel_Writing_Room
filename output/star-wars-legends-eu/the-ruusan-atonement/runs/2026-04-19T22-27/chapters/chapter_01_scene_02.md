The datapad clicked softly onto the desk between them, its blue light washing the grain of the surface.

Ben picked it up.

Seventeen reports. Every one flagged with the same notation—a symbol he'd only seen twice before, buried in a classification tier most Jedi never had reason to open. Survey coordinates. Force-reading anomalies. Six weeks of timestamps. His gaze tracked down the list and snagged on the third entry, then the ninth, then the fourteenth.

He knew those readings. They sat under his sternum like a second pulse, familiar enough that he almost resented the shape of them.

"Same anomaly across all seventeen," Luke said. He stood on the far side of the desk instead of sitting, hands loose at his sides. "Flagged independently. Different observers, different systems, same notation."

Ben scrolled to the three most recent entries and expanded them. The coordinates bunched along a single bearing—a line through the Outer Rim he could have sketched from memory if anyone had bothered to ask. Nobody had.

"Outer Rim survey," he said, keeping his voice even. "Mapping run."

"Mapping run," Luke said.

Ben looked at the bearing again. The route ran straight along it—not near it, not beside it. Along it. His father had read the same data Ben had been feeling in his bones, and the course led toward the source.

He lowered the datapad to the desk and left all of that unsaid.

Luke's office held a working kind of quiet. Through the window, Coruscant came in as a steady wash—speeders threading stacked lanes, a cargo hauler banking around a transit tower, the endless motion of a planet that never stopped to wonder whether something had gone wrong. The holocomm on the desk was dark. Beside it sat a stack of physical reports, the kind that still needed a signature instead of a thumbprint, and Ben noticed, without meaning to, that three remained sealed.

"When did you start flagging these?" he asked.

"Six weeks ago." Luke picked up a stylus, turned it once between his fingers, set it back down. "The notation was new to me. I created it when the first two reports came in and the standard classification didn't fit."

"How long have you been feeling it?"

Luke paused. Not long. A breath, maybe two. "Eleven days," he said. "Directional. Coming from outside the Temple."

Ben looked down at the datapad lying between them. Its glow had dimmed to standby.

Eleven days. Luke had sat with eleven days of a wrongness that came with a direction, a bearing, and seventeen independent confirmations. He'd filed the notation, built the route, and waited. Ben had been carrying a version of it since he was—

He cut the thought off there. His mother's voice, somewhere in the back of his head, offered the word practical, and he let that be enough.

"I've felt it longer," he said.

"I know." Luke's Force presence was warm at close range, familiar as the Temple's usual harmonics. Under it, faint but unmistakable, ran the same sour note Ben had lived with for years—a register he recognized the way you recognized a sound that had been in a room so long you'd stopped hearing it until someone else pointed it out. Luke was naming an anomaly. Ben was hearing proof that the floor had always been a little off-level and his father had only just noticed the tilt.

He picked the datapad up again, mostly to give his hands something to do.

"The route covers the full bearing," he said.

"Yes."

"All seventeen coordinates."

"The last four are projections. Based on the pattern." Luke moved around the corner of the desk—not closer, just shifted position, the way he did when he was about to say something that mattered. "I've felt something else in the last three reports. Not just the anomaly. A quality to it. As if the distance is—"

"Shortening," Ben said.

Luke looked at him.

"It's moving toward us," Ben said. "Or we're being pulled toward it. I haven't been able to tell the difference."

The silence that followed was its own conversation. Ben had learned Luke's silences the same way he'd learned to read the Force—by paying attention to what showed up when he stopped reaching for it. This one said: I suspected. I didn't want to be right.

Luke set the datapad down and said, very evenly, "I've felt something too."

Ben's throat tightened. He kept his eyes on the screen a second longer, on the dim blue reflection under his fingertips. There it was—the difference. In Luke's voice, in the pause before the pad had crossed the desk, in the choice to say it that way. His father was describing an anomaly. Ben was recognizing a register he had lived inside since he was a boy.

"A Knight," Luke said finally. "And a skilled pilot. Not a war." He paused. "Not yet."

Ben set the datapad down and went to the window.

Coruscant spread below in its usual organized chaos—transit lanes layered over one another, scaffolding climbing a tower three blocks east, a delivery speeder slicing across a lane marker and drawing an automated warning ping he saw as a red flash in the distance. Somewhere down there, someone was fighting over permits. Somewhere else, a night-shift crew was eating bad food from foil containers and waiting on a handover already twenty minutes late. The city kept moving.

The sourness was here too, faint and patient, under the room's harmonics like something gone slightly bad in clean recycled air. Ben had stopped noticing it the way he'd stopped noticing the taste of the Temple's ventilation at the back of his throat. It was simply part of the register now.

He heard Luke move behind him—not closer. Still by the desk.

"The anomaly data will be loaded to your ship's nav system before you depart," Luke said. His voice had shifted back into Grand Master register—not cold, but measured. The briefing voice. Ben knew it as well as he knew anything. "Standard survey protocols apply. Check-ins every forty-eight hours."

"Understood."

"The first coordinate is three days out. You'll want to depart by oh-six-hundred if you're using the Hydian transit lane."

"I'll be ready."

Ben widened his stance a fraction and watched the cargo hauler finish its turn and vanish behind the transit tower. The city's light pressed against the transparisteel—amber and gray, same as always at this hour. He thought, before he could stop it: Dad. Then he put the word away, which was also habit.

"Ben."

He turned.

Luke stood with his hands at his sides again, the datapad on the desk between them. His expression was one Ben had seen exactly twice before—once after Centerpoint, once after Jacen. The look that meant: I am sending you somewhere I cannot follow, and I need you to come back.

Ben said nothing. Luke didn't, either.

After a moment, Ben picked up his jacket from the chair by the door.

"Oh-six-hundred," he said.

"Yes," Luke said.

Ben accepted the mission without suggesting a partner. Luke didn't push. Neither of them said anything about the roster, or the silence where that conversation should have gone, or what it meant that they both understood this was already something other than a simple survey run and were choosing not to name it yet.
