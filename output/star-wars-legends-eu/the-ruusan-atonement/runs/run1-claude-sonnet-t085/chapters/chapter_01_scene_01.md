The Force had been playing one note wrong for three months.

Not wrong like a novice losing focus during meditation. Not wrong like the residual static lingering in certain star systems after a battle—the kind Ben had learned to recognize and file away. This was a tuning problem. The whole instrument, the vast resonant hum he had heard since childhood, was off. One string in an enormous chord dragged the rest down with it.

He sat cross-legged on the meditation chamber's stone floor, palms flat against the cold, and tried again.

*Still point.* 

The first exercise. They taught it to initiates before they were old enough to shave. Ben had found his still point at age nine in a cramped berth aboard the *Jade Shadow*, his mother's hand resting between his shoulder blades. He could find it in a firefight. He could find it half-sedated in a medbay. He had found it in the dark of Jacen's ship with blood on his hands.

He could not find it tonight.

The Force was there—he wasn't reaching into a void. It was present the way a room full of people talking was present: layered, warm, dense. To the west, Master Katarn's presence felt like a banked coal, steady and unhurried. Two floors up, a cluster of Padawans in evening drills pulsed with scattered energy. Saba Sebatyne's signature drifted at the edge of his range, solid and cool as basalt.

All of them undisturbed.

Ben pressed his fingertips against the stone. Pressure built behind his eyes—frustration, not pain, though the distinction narrowed. Everyone else in this Temple slept or drilled or meditated into the deep wells of the Force, and none of them heard it. None of them felt it. He had mentioned it twice in the past month—once to Jaina, once to a senior instructor during a training debrief. Both times he watched the same recognition flicker across their faces: *post-crisis stress, give it time, you're reading static as signal.*

He reached past them, seeking the place where the wrongness lived. It was always in the same direction. Not a compass bearing he could name—not north or spinward—but a direction in the Force itself, the way certain wounds pointed back to the moment of injury. He had stopped trying to explain that to anyone who hadn't been in the field.

He found it.

The pressure arrived first, settling behind his back teeth and in the orbital bone above his left eye. It was a vibration at a frequency that had no business existing. Like a mechanical component wearing down in a ship's superstructure—unseen, unlocatable, but felt in the deck plating through the soles of a pilot's boots. He had felt it for the first time during a mission debrief four weeks after the Abeloth crisis closed. He assumed it was his own damage then. Stress. Trauma doing its work. He had good reasons to believe his own perceptions were unreliable.

That was three months ago.

He pushed deeper. The Force responded as it always did—present, dynamic—but something was wrong at the level below. Below the individual presences, below the emotional weather of the Temple, below even the resonant hum that most Jedi described as the "voice" of the Force. The substrate. He lacked a better word. The part that wasn't any individual presence but made all presences possible, the connective medium between every living thing.

That part had a fray in it.

Not darkness. He had been inside darkness—Jacen's, Abeloth's, the darkness of his own worst months. Darkness had texture. It was aggressive, always reaching. This wasn't reaching for anything. It sat there like a gap in a load-bearing wall. The longer Ben pressed against it, the more he understood that what he felt wasn't an intrusion. Nothing had moved in.

Something had come loose.

He pulled back. His hands trembled against the stone—fine motor tremors from sustained concentration. He checked his chronometer out of habit. Twenty-two minutes. The Temple's ambient light had shifted to late-cycle gold, filtering under the chamber door in a thin stripe.

Three months of this. Three months of being the only one who heard it.

He thought about walking across the Temple to his father's quarters. He ran the calculation like a tactical problem: *assets, liabilities, probable outcomes.* Luke would listen. Luke always listened, which was sometimes worse than being dismissed, because Luke's attention had weight. Ben wasn't sure he could hold up under the full measure of it. *Your father has rebuilt an Order from the pieces Caedus left behind. Your father just closed the Abeloth crisis. Your father does not need his son showing up at the twenty-second hour with a meditation headache and a theory about the Force being structurally wrong.*

He was twenty-three years old. He was a Knight. He could carry this until he had something concrete.

Ben pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, then dropped them. The tremor had already faded. Another thing he had trained himself past—the visible evidence of effort.

He reached out one more time, not because he expected to learn anything new, but because he was constitutionally incapable of leaving a problem alone. He found the fray again. He held himself still against it and tried to analyze what he was actually touching.

Not Sith energy. Sith energy had weight, heat, and direction—it was always going somewhere, always consuming. He had felt that in Jacen before Jacen became Caedus, the way the Force around him started to feel like a draft in a pressurized hull: wrong, but localizable. And Abeloth had been something else entirely, vast and chaotic, the Force as flood rather than current.

This was neither.

This was the Force behaving like a structure with a failing joint. Not corrupted. Not weaponized. Something in the architecture was letting go—had been letting go for some time, probably longer than three months if he was honest about when he first felt it versus when he first admitted it. The distinction between those two dates was not something he planned to share.

The thing he kept coming back to, the thing that made the back of his neck go tight, was this: Jacen hadn't felt evil at the beginning either. Abeloth hadn't announced herself. The worst things he had ever encountered in the Force had all started as something he almost couldn't name.

*Structural,* he thought. *Not moral. Mechanical.*

He wasn't sure yet what that distinction meant. He was sure it mattered.

The door slid open.

Luke Skywalker stood in the hallway light. Ben knew from the set of his father's shoulders before he even registered the datapad in his hand. Not a father's posture. Mission-ready—the particular economy of movement that meant decisions had been made.

Ben got to his feet. The tremor in his hands was gone.

"You're awake," his father said.

"Couldn't sleep." The lie was automatic, smooth. Easier than explaining what he had been doing.

Luke held up the datapad. "A survey assignment came through. Outer Rim, three systems along the Darpa Sector corridor. Anomalous Force readings the Council wants catalogued." He paused, his gaze steady. "I thought you might want first look."

Ben's chest tightened. The Darpa Sector. It was the same direction as the fray. He kept his expression neutral, but something must have flickered across his face because Luke's eyes narrowed—not with concern, but with recognition. The look of someone who had just had a suspicion confirmed.

"When do I leave?" Ben asked.

"Tomorrow, 0600." Luke looked at him for a moment longer than the question required. "Get some rest, Ben."

He left without asking why his son sat alone in a meditation chamber at the twenty-second hour with the lights down. Ben did not volunteer it.

He stood in the quiet and felt, from somewhere in the direction of the Darpa Sector, the Force pulling loose from itself, patient as erosion, steady as a fault line settling.

The isolation broke. Not because Luke had named what Ben was feeling, but because Luke had known to look for it. Because Luke had already sent him toward it.

Ben picked up his jacket from the bench by the door and went to pack.
