The wall held the memory of a field.

Ben pressed his palm against the stone. He felt no dark-side malice or the sharp charge of energy deliberately spent. This was worn. It felt like a dry riverbed. The lattice here was thinner than in the other chambers, and that thinness possessed a rhythmic, patient texture, like water finding low ground over centuries. Not violence. Erosion.

He pulled his hand back. "This one didn't break out."

Sera stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, studying the scoring on the walls. These weren't blast marks or the frantic gouging of a prisoner desperate to escape. The damage ran in long, parallel lines at the height of a standing adult's hands. Methodical. The woman had worked the same section of stone, over and over, for a lifetime.

"No," Sera agreed. "She didn't."

Desh had already moved to the terminal. He pulled a forensic interface from his kit—a slim, scratched GAG-issue device capable of handshaking with hardware centuries older than itself. The conduits here were compatible. The facility's designers had built for permanence.

The terminal hummed. A file unspooled.

"Prisoner designation," Desh read, his voice flat. "Darth Veraine. Alchemist. Brotherhood of Darkness."

The title hung in the dusty air.

*Darth.* Ben had grown up with that word as a wound. Vader. Sidious. Caedus—Jacen, who had taken the name, then the title, then everything else. He had heard it applied to historical Sith the way one heard names from ancient wars: terrible in context, but defanged by distance. Here, in a room that still held the impression of her presence, it carried weight. Recent. She had stood exactly where he was standing.

He studied the file image.

The hologram showed a Human woman, perhaps forty, with a stillness the recording had preserved. It wasn't calm. It was the kind of stillness that came from learning to hold oneself with agonizing care. Her eyes were directed off-frame, focused like a person performing mental arithmetic.

Ben glanced at Kael.

The younger man stared at the hologram. He had stopped moving. It wasn't shock, but something controlled—the way a person goes still when deciding what their face is allowed to show. His presence in the Force compressed. Ben had learned that technique years ago when he needed to pass through rooms where he couldn't afford to be read. He had never seen Kael use it.

The recognition of that deliberate erasure registered before Ben could name it. A question formed and stayed unasked.

He returned his attention to the file.

"Gavran flagged her," Desh said, scrolling through the logs. "Three separate times. The first flag was placed before the sealing. 'Subject demonstrates capacity for structural analysis of Force containment. Highest-risk anchor. Recommend additional suppression protocols.' They were approved." He paused. "They weren't enough."

"She didn't try to break the suppression," Sera said. She crouched beside the lowest line of scored wall. "She worked around it. She found the frequency at which her own presence could interact with the containment field without triggering the alarm thresholds. Then she repeated that interaction. For centuries."

"Like tuning an instrument," Torin said from the doorway.

Ben turned. Torin was looking at the wall with an expression closer to recognition than horror. When he caught Ben watching, he looked away and stepped into the chamber as if the movement could erase his previous lapse. "The principle, I mean. Technically."

Sera straightened. "The principle is that she used herself as a conduit. She let the Force leak through her and the seal in controlled amounts. Small enough to avoid detection. Consistent enough to erode the boundary." She gestured to the scored walls. "She didn't rage. She worked it like a problem."

The room felt colder than the ventilation explained.

"Timeline," Ben prompted.

"Working on it." Desh’s fingers danced across the interface. The terminal populated dates, access logs, and power draws until a picture assembled itself. "Containment breach occurred approximately nine weeks ago. She was ambulatory within twelve hours—faster than the others. That tracks with a gradual physiological adjustment. She’d been maintaining partial awareness during the stasis cycle." He scrolled further. "She didn't leave."

Ben waited.

"She stayed for seventy-two hours to access the archive." Desh’s voice lost its flatness. "All of it. Current star maps. Galactic political structure. New Jedi Order records—whatever was in the local cache. Infrastructure assessments for each Corridor node." He looked up. "She knew exactly what she was waking up into before she walked out the door."

Torin turned back to the wall, his hands clenching at his sides. It was a small gesture, easily missed, but Ben saw it. The Battle Master looked like someone who had just placed a familiar figure in a new context and found the fit credible. Not surprised. Confirmed.

"She studied the system's degradation," Sera said. "She would have understood the cascade timeline."

"Better than us," Ben added. "She spent a thousand years as part of the machinery. She’d feel where it was weakest."

Desh pulled up the exit log.

A notification blinked, patient and red. *AUTOMATED RECOVERY PROTOCOL: SUCCESSFUL. DOCKING COMPLETE. VESSEL DEPARTED: 63 STANDARD DAYS AGO.*

Ben read it twice.

The fortress had an emergency recovery protocol—a failsafe from the Lords era designed to summon extraction for any anchor who survived a catastrophic failure. Veraine had found it in the archive and triggered it. A long-range scout craft, registered to a defunct salvage relay in the Outer Rim, had answered the automated signal and docked without knowing what it was retrieving.

Sixty-three days ago.

While they had been waking up and arguing over the moral weight of the Ruusan Reformations, she had been moving.

Ben turned to the star map. The Corridor nodes glowed in sequence, a line of amber and red running along the Mid Rim. It was the failing infrastructure of a seal designed to hold something the galaxy had forgotten existed. Veraine's route was obvious. She wouldn't run from the Corridor. She would follow it, node by node. She understood the architecture better than anyone alive.

She wasn't running. She was working.

"She isn't just out," Ben said. The red light from the terminal reflected across his skin. "She's ahead of us."

Sera’s expression shifted into the carved-stone look she wore during threat assessments. Torin stared at the map, his shoulders tight with a new tension. Kael had moved to stand by the doorway, his back to the room. Ben couldn't see his face.

Sixty-three days. She had the blueprints, the maps, and a thousand years of knowledge the modern galaxy had spent a millennium forgetting. She possessed a patience Ben couldn't quantify.

He could catch her. He had to believe that. But he would need better information, faster movement, and a team that trusted each other enough to act without seeking a consensus for every breath.

He scanned the room: Torin locked in tension, Sera calculating, Kael’s back rigid against the doorframe, and Desh pulling the archive logs into a portable drive. The gap between their knowledge and Veraine’s actions opened like a chasm.

Ben turned back to the terminal and started downloading everything.
