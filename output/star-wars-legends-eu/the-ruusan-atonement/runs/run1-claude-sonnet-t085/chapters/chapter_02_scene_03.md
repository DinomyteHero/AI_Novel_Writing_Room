The second world was frozen.

Not the clean cold of a polar system, but the arrested chill of a place stopped mid-process. Ice sheeted the basalt in formations that should have been snow. Vegetation locked into postures belonging to autumn rather than winter. It looked as if the season had been interrupted, leaving the planet to improvise.

Ben's boots found purchase on a shelf of volcanic rock. He hauled himself up to the ruin's base. The same stone. The same color, the same grain. He hadn't expected that. The first world had been arid and wind-scoured, its ruins the color of old bone. This one sat under iron cloud cover on a shelf of black basalt, and the ruins were—

Identical. Not similar. The same.

He pressed a gloved hand flat against the wall, then pulled it back before the contact could register through the material. He stood with his palm an inch from the stone.

"Same markings," Desh called from below. It wasn't a question.

"Same hand." Ben's voice was clipped. He raised the holorecorder. "Different planet, different geology, different hemisphere. Same hand."

He found the spiral almost immediately. Every ruin on the first world had terminated in that sharp angle—the flow cut off, the groove ending clean. Here the spiral was carved at shoulder height rather than at the base, and the stone was darker, but the termination was precise. The angle matched to the degree. The groove width was exact.

He reached for the Force to gauge the distance to the far wall.

When he stepped toward it, he came up short. Not a stumble—his proprioception simply disagreed with his vision by three centimeters. His boot scraped stone he hadn't calculated was there. He stopped, looked at his feet, then at the wall, and ran the measurement again by counting his stride length against the gap.

The wall sat where his eyes said it was. The Force insisted otherwise.

Ben’s pulse quickened. He tried again, reaching through the Force toward a point he could see clearly. The sensory report was wrong. It felt as if space itself had developed a stutter.

"You're doing that thing," Desh said.

Ben lowered the holorecorder. "What thing?"

"The thing where you go still and your face turns to stone." Desh climbed up to join him, his movements economical. "Which means something is wrong."

"The Force is giving me bad distances. Like a comms array with lag—I reach for a position and land behind it. Depth perception for the vacuum." Ben kept his eyes on the spiral. "It's localized to the anomaly zone."

Desh moved to the ruin's eastern corner and tilted his head, a listening posture Ben knew well. "You hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"Water. Running water, somewhere underneath us." Desh tapped his heel against the basalt. "Solid rock. Survey data says the subsurface hydrology here is glacial melt, dormant for six centuries." He tapped again. "But I hear water."

Ben listened. He heard wind off the ice shelf and the faint creak of frozen vegetation contracting. No water. He wanted to dismiss it as Desh's imagination or instrument noise, but the words caught in his throat. Desh didn't imagine things. Desh noticed them.

"I don't hear anything," Ben said.

"I know." Desh stepped back. When he looked at Ben, his gaze held a careful assessment. "That's the problem."

Ben finished the holorecord sweep in eleven minutes. He’d refined the protocol since the first world. They were back on the shuttle before the cloud cover broke. Neither spoke during the ascent. The frozen world dropped away, its ruined shelf shrinking to a dark stroke against white ice. Ben watched until the atmosphere thinned and the stars returned, the wrongness fading to a pressure at the back of his skull.

The *Steady Returns* smelled of recycled air and Desh's caf. Ben took the co-pilot's seat and pulled up the navigational archive while Desh settled into the pilot's chair to plot the survey coordinates. The normalcy of the routine should have steadied him.

"Give me both sites," Ben said.

Desh entered the coordinates. The holomap rendered them as two blue points against the dark, separated by forty-three parsecs of empty space.

"Mark the anomaly bearing from my original readings. The directional vector."

Desh overlaid the vector from the Darpa Sector observation. The bearing line extended from the first site, passed through the second with a deviation of less than half a degree, and continued outward.

Ben stared. His fingers tightened on the armrest.

Half a degree was instrument error. It was the margin of error expected when working with two data points across forty-three parsecs of the Outer Rim.

Except it wasn't. Two worlds, separated by a gulf of space, both bearing the same markings, both sitting on the same line. That was a hand reaching across the stars.

"Extend the bearing," he said.

The line pushed across the chart. It crossed mapped space—sparse systems, survey tags, a handful of hyperspace waypoints—and then hit the edge of the charted region. It kept going into the blank space beyond.

Ben leaned forward. "How far does the chart go?"

"Standard Imperial survey data, updated through 45 ABY. Past that—" Desh gestured at the void. "Old Republic data. Jedi era. Pre-Clone Wars."

"Pull the older data."

The old Republican charts were fragmentary, rendered in a pale hue. They extended the map further into the void. The bearing line crossed them too. It passed through two survey tags—designation numbers without names—and continued into the blankness beyond even the oldest records.

The void was not simply unmapped. Ben had worked Outer Rim routes before; he knew what unmapped space looked like. It looked like absence—white space or null-return markers.

This void had no notation at all. It sat in the chart like a hole in a document, a section removed rather than never written.

Ben's hand moved, tracing the bearing line from the two survey sites into that void. His father had given him eight coordinates. Not two. Eight.

"That shouldn't be blank," he said.

"No." Desh studied the void. "Those designation numbers on the old survey tags—" He pulled up the records. The data was sparse: two survey passes, one in 412 BBY and one in 289 BBY, both marked *SURVEY INCOMPLETE, EQUIPMENT MALFUNCTION*. No follow-up expeditions. No explanation of why two separate teams had turned back from the same region a century apart.

Ben pulled up the full route list. All eight survey sites, in the order Luke had given them. His hands moved with precision. He overlaid them on the map.

They formed a line.

Not eight random points. Eight points in sequence, each one closer to that void than the last. The survey route was walking him toward whatever sat at the bearing's terminus. Eight breadcrumbs. Eight checkpoints on a path laid out before he'd ever left Coruscant.

The holomap glowed blue in the dim cockpit. The bearing line ended in darkness.

Desh watched him with the patience of a man who had learned not to rush a Jedi. But his fingers drummed once against the console. Desh saw the pattern.

"My father didn't send me to survey the Rim," Ben said. His voice was steady, though a cold realization had finally found its shape. "He sent me to find whatever is at the end of this line."

The caf was still warm. Outside, the stars held their positions with perfect indifference. Ben's hands stopped shaking. His jaw unclenched. The confusion in his chest shifted into focus.

Luke had sent him here deliberately. Luke had known what he would find.

The question was no longer why. The question was what waited in the dark.
