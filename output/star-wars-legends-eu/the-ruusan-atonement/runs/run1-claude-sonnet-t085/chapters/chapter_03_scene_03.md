The third draft repeated the first two word for word.

Ben stared at the screen. The holocomm terminal cast a blue wash over his hands and the nav-display, where coordinates pulsed in amber. Around him, the *Steady Returns* groaned—the low thrum of the sublight drive punctuated by the irregular tick of a heat-exchange coupling. Desh had promised to replace that part three systems ago. The cursor blinked, a steady, rhythmic taunt.

*Master Skywalker. Anomalous Force readings in the Darpa Sector have escalated to critical. Survey data attached. The anchor network appears to be a pre-Reform containment system. Recommend immediate Council review and—*

He hit Delete.

The screen wiped. The cursor resumed its vigil.

He already knew the shape of the next draft. He had composed it twice during the walk from the cargo hold, stacking sentences the way his mother had taught him to build reports: lead with facts, follow with assessment, close with recommendation. He was proficient at the craft. The Jedi Council would receive his field reports, file them, and perhaps dispatch a research team weeks later. By then, the situation on the ground usually shifted in ways the bureaucracy couldn't track.

The seal sat at fourteen percent.

He pulled up the sensor data. The degradation curve had lost its linearity, accelerating three times in the past standard year. Each spike climbed steeper than the last. Ben wasn't a structural engineer or a Force historian, but he could read a graph. Whatever remained of the containment system wouldn't survive a Council review cycle.

He told himself that clearly. The logic felt sound. Necessary. It seemed the only choice a competent Jedi could make.

His father's face didn't appear in his mind—Ben didn't summon it—but the image lingered regardless. He saw the specific set of Luke’s mouth when he received reports lacking prior notification. Ben’s chest tightened. He shoved the thought aside. He refused to consider how this appeared to an observer: a Jedi Knight in an unmapped sector, hovering over a failing seal, scrubbing his own dispatches. He didn't think about Jacen, who had also believed the galaxy couldn't afford to wait.

He focused on the coordinates.

They lived in the nav-computer's memory now, calculated and locked. The route threaded through a dead zone—no hyperspace lanes, no survey markers, nothing in the modern charts except a notation from 289 BBY: *equipment malfunction, survey incomplete.* Someone had filed that report a millennium ago. They had gone home, written their excuses, and never returned. Now Ben sat in the cockpit, staring at the numbers.

He opened a fourth draft.

*Master Skywalker. I've located the source of the Darpa Sector anomaly. I'm proceeding to investigate before—*

The hatch hissed open. Ben didn't close the draft. He recognized Desh's footsteps—the measured cadence of a man who knew how to move silently but chose to announce his presence.

Desh dropped into the co-pilot's chair. He smelled of engine grease and recycled air. Ignoring the nav-display, he fixed his gaze on the holocomm screen where the fourth draft sat half-finished.

He remained silent for a long beat.

"What's the word from Coruscant?" Desh asked.

The cockpit felt cramped. Ben kept his eyes on the screen, fingers hovering over the console. "Nothing yet."

"Nothing yet." Desh repeated the words, stripping them of inflection until they sounded like a challenge. "That packet you were drafting when I came up for fuel cells—did it go out?"

Ben’s thumb moved. He pressed the key.

The screen went dark.

"No," he said.

Desh turned to the nav-display. He studied the coordinates and the dead zone notation the computer flagged as a routing error. He scanned the degradation data in the secondary window, processing the information with a flat, clinical focus.

"You're not reporting this," Desh said.

The statement wasn't an accusation. Desh had served in the GAG long enough to recognize a fixed decision. He didn't waste energy on outrage, but a certain weight entered his voice—the sound of a man bracing for a load.

"Not yet," Ben replied.

Desh rested his hands on his knees. "Not yet," he echoed. "You know what 'not yet' sounded like where we used to work."

"This is different."

"It always is." Desh turned fully in his chair, his expression shifting to the one he used when calculating odds. "The seal is at fourteen percent. If your father sent a research team tomorrow, when would they arrive?"

Ben didn't answer. Desh already knew.

"Three weeks minimum," Desh said. "Five, more likely. Council review, mission authorization, ship assignment." He paused. "How long before fourteen becomes zero?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I. But you think it's less than five weeks." Desh leaned back, repositioning himself. "You think waiting for the chain of command is the same as failing."

Ben looked at the display. The route sat ready. He had manually overridden the safety parameters, and the computer had finally stopped flagging the dead zone. The course was set. He could engage the drive in minutes, and there would be no retreat. No report. No Council. Just the coordinates burning in the dark.

"Someone built that system a thousand years ago," Ben said. "It’s been running without maintenance ever since, and it’s still holding. Whatever is inside..." He stopped, then tried again. "If I wait, I'm not waiting for help. I'm waiting for a committee to decide if help is warranted. I can be there while they're still debating."

Desh didn't argue. He could follow a line of logic to its end without being convinced by the destination. He saw the shape of the argument and the cracks in the frame.

"I want it on record," Desh said, "that I recommended the report."

"Noted."

"And that 'not yet' usually becomes 'not at all.'" Desh’s gaze dropped to Ben’s hands. "You know that."

Ben turned to the nav-computer. He keyed the jump sequence. Coordinates locked. Route confirmed. The sublight drive shifted pitch as the *Steady Returns* oriented toward the jump point. The controls felt smooth, worn down by years of use. Others had sat in this seat and made choices. A long chain of people had chosen to move forward rather than look back.

"I know what it looks like," Ben said.

"I know you do." Desh reached across and tightened his restraints with practiced efficiency. "That's what worries me."

Ben engaged the hyperdrive.

The stars stretched into white lines. The blue-white tunnel of hyperspace enveloped the ship, and the *Steady Returns* slid into the void with a low, harmonic groan.

The wrongness remained.

He had expected the transition to ease the pressure. Hyperspace was a realm apart, and he had hoped the tension in his skull was tied to a physical location in realspace. Instead, the sensation intensified. It wasn't louder, but it felt closer—a frequency that had been humming at the edge of his perception now occupied the center of the room.

His reflection appeared in the viewport glass, made alien by the flickering blue light. For a second, he didn't recognize the man staring back. Then he did.

Desh had already pulled out his datapad to study the sensor logs. He wasn't checking them for errors; he was reading them like a ledger of things already lost.

Neither of them spoke for a long time.
