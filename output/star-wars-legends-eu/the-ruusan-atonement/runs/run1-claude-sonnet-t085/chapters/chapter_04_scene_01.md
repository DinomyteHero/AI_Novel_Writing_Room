The plateau caught the wind from three directions and held it like a weapon.

Ben leaned into the gust. Each breath came shallow and cold, thin enough that his lungs demanded two where they would normally take one. He had been regulating since the ship—drawing warmth inward, slowing his metabolic demand—but the mountain air had its own opinion. It sliced through his discipline like a blade through gauze. Functional, but not painless.

Beside him, Desh walked with his chin tucked, pack adjusted for the grade. He hadn't complained about the atmosphere. He simply pulled his collar up and started calculating.

The fortress didn't announce itself.

That was the first thing Ben registered. No gate, no wall, no deliberate threshold. One moment the mountainside was a series of natural ridgelines and windblown scree; the next, the geometry changed. The angles were too consistent. The stone faces too flat. A horizontal line ran for forty meters where no geological process would cut one, and the pale alloy buried within the rock caught the pitiless light differently than the surrounding stone.

Not a building. A scar.

"No perimeter emplacements." Desh stopped. His eyes moved in a pattern Ben recognized—sector, threat vector, fallback route, sector again. "No automated defenses. No sensor clusters on the high points."

"They weren't worried about what might come in," Ben said.

"No." Desh studied the line of alloy where it vanished into the rock. "They were worried about what might come out."

Ben felt the pressure more strongly here than he had from orbit. The wrongness wasn't a Dark Side taint—he knew that sensation from Korriban and Centerpoint, the oily residue of terrible choices. This was different. Where the dark side felt like a wound that kept bleeding, this felt like tension building behind metal walls. Containing. The containment was old, the metal was tired, and the pressure remained.

He walked toward the entrance.

The door was a single slab, four meters tall and twice as wide, set into the mountain face with a precision that made Ben’s eyes want to skip over it. Alloy and stone had fused over a millennium—mineral deposits threading through the seams, the surface patinated to a color that bridged the two materials. Whoever built this understood that the best way to hide something permanent was to let it become the ground.

No handle. No visible mechanism. No script or sigil carved into the surface. Just the door and the weight of the mountain.

Ben raised one hand and reached through the Force.

He started with a standard telekinetic probe—feeling for the mechanism, searching for a latch, applying graduated pressure. He had opened sealed Jedi caches before, ancient ones that required more intention than technique. The Force moved with him, responsive and precise. He found the door's surface and pushed.

Nothing.

He adjusted his approach. Not a push, but an imprint. He projected his identification in the Force as he had been trained, broadcasted his standing as a Jedi Knight, his alignment, his intent. The mechanism should recognize him as a peer of its architect.

The door ignored him.

He reached deeper, past the surface and into the architecture behind the alloy. Old Jedi caches often had mechanical hearts—counterweights and pressure plates that responded to specific meditative frequencies. He found a structure. It felt his presence the way a locked gate feels a hand without a key. Registered, yet insufficient.

"Progress?" Desh asked.

Ben’s teeth ground together. "Working on it."

He tried the Unifying Force perspective—a broader, diffuse attention. He let the Force move through him rather than directing it. The mechanism remained unmoved. He felt like a man shouting in a language where only the accent was wrong, certain the words existed but unable to find the register.

Ten minutes passed. Fifteen.

The wind cut across the plateau and found the gap at his neck. Ben’s temperature regulation dropped two degrees before he caught it. He was burning more energy than he should. The door was drawing from him—not with hostility, but requiring the effort any immovable object demands. The compound interest of failure was adding up.

His hands dropped to his sides.

"Ben." Desh’s voice carried no inflection, but the use of the name meant his tactical assessment had shifted. "We have maybe six hours of light left. I’m reading no shelter within twenty klicks."

Ben pressed both hands flat against the alloy. It was cold enough to sting.

He stopped trying.

It wasn't a conscious choice, but a system reaching a stopping point. His shoulders dropped. His breathing evened out. He stood with his palms against the door and thought of nothing, and that was when the door decided to pay attention.

The vibration started in his palms and climbed his arms. A frequency from the bedrock—deep, resonant, and old enough to feel geological. The stone beneath his boots hummed. The wrongness he had carried since orbit suddenly organized itself, pulling toward the door like iron filings to a magnet.

The door wasn't reading his technique. It wasn't reading his rank or his intent. It was reading something beneath the training and the years. Something that had been there before any of it.

The alloy warmed.

Slowly, the metal remembered its heat. The mineral deposits in the seams caught the light. The patina shifted. Ben understood, in the way understanding arrives when the search for it ends, that this door had been waiting for a specific signature for a thousand years. The signature wasn't *Jedi Knight*.

It was his.

Not Ben Skywalker, the twenty-three-year-old Knight of the new Order. The signature was older. It recognized the bloodline the way a loyalty-keyed weapon recognizes a grip—through resonance, through a specific quality of Force-presence catalogued a millennium before his grandfather was born.

He pulled his hands back. The door kept moving.

It opened with the certainty of tectonic plates. The seam appeared first, a line of darkness splitting the slab, and then the halves drew into the rock with a hydraulic slowness.

The air that spilled out was dead. It was the cold of a space sealed from weather and time, carrying the weight of compressed stillness.

Ben stood at the threshold and looked into the dark.

The wrongness was structured here, channeled through the mountain like a pipe carrying pressure. Somewhere in the gloom, something moved in the Force. Not a person.

A mechanism that had just noticed it was being watched.

Behind him, Desh had gone still, calculating new contingencies. No extraction. No way out but through.

Ben stepped inside.
