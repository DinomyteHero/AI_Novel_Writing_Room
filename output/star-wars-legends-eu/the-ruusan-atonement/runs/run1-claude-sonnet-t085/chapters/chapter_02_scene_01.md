The *Steady Returns* handled like a ship repaired more times than it had been built. It flew the way most veterans walked—functional, compensating, and loudly disapproving of the weather.

Desh wedged his forearm under the co-pilot’s console, a diagnostic pad balanced on his knee. He wore the expression of a man who had long ago accepted that machines were personally difficult. The stabilizer threw a rhythmic *thrum-clunk* on every third rotation—three seconds, four seconds, clunk—like a metronome that had abandoned precision.

"Left coupling," Ben said, eyes fixed on the nav readout.

"Already replaced it."

"When?"

"Kalandis."

Ben turned. "That was eight months ago."

"Couplings are getting worse." Desh extracted his arm and inspected a smear of dark grease on his sleeve. He examined the stain with the detached air of a man looking at someone else's problem. "You want to file a complaint with the manufacturer, or you want me to shim it?"

"Shim it."

"Thought so." Desh produced a strip of heat-resistant composite from his jacket pocket. He had already cut it to size. He’d known before Ben spoke.

That was Desh. He came prepared for every argument he’d already won.

Ben watched him work. The hyperspace tunnel scrolled past the viewport in long blue ribbons. The cockpit smelled of scorched insulation and the dregs of caf. Recycled air tasted of metal, the sharp tang of atmospheric processors running warm. None of it was unpleasant. It was the specific texture of a ship lived in long enough to develop opinions.

Desh was a warm absence in Ben's peripheral awareness. Not a gap, not a wound—just a space where the Force didn't reach, the way a room could be quiet without being empty. After months of feeling the lattice fray at the edges, that absence felt restful. Desh didn't register in the Force. He didn't generate the low-frequency signal of a Force-sensitive processing their environment. He was simply there, solid and unaware that the galaxy was structurally compromised.

Ben's chest tightened.

"You still carrying that old field kit?" Ben asked. "The one from the—"

"Yes."

"I wasn't finished."

"You were going to say the service." Desh didn't look up. "The answer is yes. Standard kit, updated charge packs." He paused. "It's a good kit."

The cockpit air grew heavy in the silence following that sentence. 

Ben turned back to the nav readout. "Yeah," he said. "It is."

That was the whole conversation. It was always the whole conversation. They had spent three years in the GAG together—three years of work that didn't survive the transition to official memory—and what they had preserved of it fit into two sentences and a mutual decision to move on. Some histories were better carried in the weight of a field kit than spoken aloud.

The stabilizer went *thrum-clunk*.

"Better," Desh said, settling into his seat. He reached for the caf carafe and poured the remains into a ceramic mug that had lost its handle somewhere near the Outer Rim. He wrapped both hands around it, seeking the heat. "Nav says forty minutes to reversion."

"I know." Ben had been watching the counter. He’d also been trying to feel the way forward—the lattice-sense that usually oriented him in transit, a low current of structural information that signaled what waited at the coordinates ahead.

It felt like plunging his hand into static.

The sensation wasn't dark or hostile. It was frayed. The lattice wasn't broken like a bone; it was abraded, like rope under tension for too long. He could feel the underlying architecture, but the signal arrived in fragments. Distance didn't explain it. He'd traveled farther in hyperspace with better reception.

He stopped trying.

Desh hummed something low and tuneless, turning the mug in his hands. His eyes were half-closed. He looked like a man waiting for a transport, not one flying toward an anomaly that had given Ben headaches for months. 

Ben's fingers drummed once against the armrest.

Desh tapped the sensor panel twice—not with urgency, but thoughtfully, the way one knocks on a door of uncertain occupancy. He leaned forward, read the display, and then read it again.

"Ben."

"I see it."

"You're not looking at it."

"I see it anyway." Ben pulled up the secondary display. The readout confirmed what the lattice-static had suggested for the last hour. The vacuum density sensor was spiking at irregular intervals, returning values the system flagged as instrument error. Impossible readings. Space didn't behave the way the sensor claimed.

"Says the vacuum density is fluctuating." Desh set down the mug with deliberate care. "That's not a thing. Vacuum density doesn't fluctuate. You'd need—" He stopped. "I don't know what you'd need. Something's wrong with the sensor."

"Maybe," Ben said.

Desh looked at him with the patience of a man who had worked with Force-sensitives long enough to know what *maybe* meant. "Or," he said, "the stars are forgetting how to be stars."

He said it the way he said everything—flat, dry, without emphasis. He wasn't being dramatic; he was describing the data. He was also acknowledging the thing neither of them had named since Coruscant: something was wrong in a way instruments couldn't quite catch.

Ben gripped the armrests.

The proximity alarm chirped. The counter ticked toward zero. Ben silenced it, each motion precise and controlled.

The pressure behind his eyes had been building since the sensor spike. It wasn't pain. It felt like standing in a room where the air pressure was wrong—the inner ear registering a shift the body couldn't account for. He'd been cataloguing it as hyperspace nausea since leaving Coruscant.

He stopped cataloguing.

"Finish your caf," he said.

"Already cold." Desh drank it anyway, eyes locked on the sensor readout.

The stabilizer went *thrum-clunk*, *thrum-clunk*, steady as a fault line. Ben watched the hyperspace tunnel and thought about the lattice—the fraying, the static, the degrading signal—and kept his hands on the armrests instead of reaching for the controls.

The tunnel collapsed.

Realspace arrived.

The local sun was there, burning at the correct spectral classification. Everything appeared in order. The planet hung at the expected distance, survey-class and unremarkable. The star field was normal.

Except the light looked wrong. Not dim, but thin. It looked like winter light through a dirty window—present, but losing something in transit.

Ben's knuckles were white.

Desh leaned forward, studying the readouts. His expression remained unchanged. "Well," he said quietly. "That's new."

Ben didn't respond. He didn't reach for the Force to confirm what he was seeing. He didn't need to. The pressure behind his eyes was answer enough.

*It's worse than I thought.* He had used that phrase before, in the GAG and the years after. He had a practiced relationship with the thought.

He had never meant it this much.
