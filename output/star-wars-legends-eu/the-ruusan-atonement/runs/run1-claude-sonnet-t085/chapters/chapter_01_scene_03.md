The hangar smelled of machine oil and recycled air, a sharp contrast to the incense and stone of the Temple above. Ben stood at the edge of bay seven, scrolling through his comlink.

Seff Hellin. Capable in a fight, more proficient with the Force than he admitted. He would ask questions Ben couldn't answer.

Jaina Solo Fel. She would dissect his motives in seconds, then spend an hour demanding to know why he hadn't cleared this through the Council. Family was a liability in this.

Radd Minker. Fresh from the Agricultural Corps. Ben lingered on the name, then swiped past.

The bay remained quiet. At the far end, two mechanics in orange jumpsuits ran diagnostics on a transport. Their voices drifted across the durasteel floor in low, indecipherable fragments. Behind Ben, a maintenance droid chirped twice and fell silent. The heavy hum of the Temple’s power grid pressed down from the ceiling.

Ben kept scrolling.

He needed a pilot who thought in three dimensions. He needed someone who wouldn't sense the rot and waste hours theorizing about the will of the Force. A tactical mind, not a metaphysical one. Someone who understood vectors, hard points, and the cold geometry of survival on an uncharted world.

His thumb stopped on Desh Rolan.

Ben stared at the contact. He waited long enough to feel the gravity of the choice, then initiated the call before he could talk himself out of it. The comm chirped. While he waited, he walked toward his ship—not the *Jade Shadow*, which drew too much scrutiny, but the *Steady Returns*. It was a Skipray blastboat he’d requisitioned through the survey division. Compact. Unassuming. The kind of vessel that discouraged conversation.

"Rolan." The voice arrived thin, compressed by a long-range bounce. Desh sounded like a man who had been awake for hours and found nothing surprising about it.

"It's Ben."

"I know." A pause followed. "What time is it on Coruscant?"

"Late."

"Right." Ben heard the scrape of a chair—the sound of a man rising from a dark room. "Where am I going?"

Desh didn't ask for a briefing or a justification. He asked for a heading. The question landed with the weight of recognition; Desh never required a preamble. It was safer that way.

"Darpa Sector. I'll transmit coordinates once you're aboard."

"Aboard what?"

"Bay seven, Jedi Temple. Skipray blastboat. Registered for survey."

"Survey," Desh repeated, his tone flat. "Who else?"

Ben climbed the boarding ramp and keyed the hatch shut. "Just us."

The ensuing silence was heavy. It wasn't shock—Ben had never known Desh to be surprised—but a specific, calculated quiet. It was the silence of two men refusing to discuss a shared understanding. Their time in the GAG had been full of such moments. You learned to embrace the void when the chain of command rotted and the only way to function was to stop asking questions.

Ben waited for the push. He expected Desh to demand the full picture, as any other Jedi would.

"How long?" Desh asked instead.

The tension in Ben’s shoulders gave way. "Unknown. Pack for a week, assume two."

"Gear?"

"Dealer's choice."

"I always choose armor and a repeating blaster."

"Good instinct." Ben moved through the *Steady Returns* without activating the overheads, guided by the amber glow of standby lights. He knew the layout by heart—twelve steps to the cockpit, ducking under the low conduit at the galley. The familiar proportions steadied him. Desh wouldn't pry. Desh never asked the questions that mattered.

"You're calling me," Desh said, "because I can't feel it."

Ben stopped at the cockpit threshold. He nearly caught his breath. The statement hung in the air, unadorned and accurate.

He prepared a professional response: *I need your tactical read. I need someone who processes threats without interference. I need a partner who won't alert the Council.*

Instead, he said, "I need your tactical read."

"Right." Desh didn't press. "I'll be there in forty."

The connection cut.

Ben settled into the pilot's seat and began the pre-flight sequence manually. He could have delegated the task to the R4 unit bolted behind the co-pilot's chair, but he preferred the tactile routine. Fuel cells. Atmospheric processors. Nav computer. Each toggle was a small confirmation that the ship would hold together. His hands moved with practiced autonomy.

He was halfway through the third checklist when Desh ascended the ramp, a pack over one shoulder and a locked weapon case in his hand. Desh glanced at the console, then at Ben.

"You look tired."

"I'm fine."

"That's the tired man's mantra." Desh stowed his gear in the weapons locker with the familiarity of someone who had been aboard before. "You eating?"

"In hyperspace."

Desh offered no reply, which was his version of a rebuke. He dropped into the co-pilot’s seat and began his own inspection: weapons systems, escape pod seals, life support redundancy. He checked the things that kept people alive when the official systems failed.

They cleared Coruscant’s traffic grid forty minutes later. Ben handled the comms himself, navigating the departure vectors east of the commercial lanes. The city-planet receded in the viewport—a trillion lights stacked in shimmering layers, shrinking to a point of glare before vanishing as Ben pitched the nose up.

The stars froze. He pulled the hyperdrive lever.

The tunnel of hyperspace erupted around them, filling the viewport with blue-white static. Ben had made this jump a thousand times. He knew the sensation: a blurring of the Force, a softening of signals until the immediate moment was all that remained. Perception in transit was notoriously unreliable. Whatever a Jedi felt usually dissolved into the hum of the engines.

The wrongness did not dissolve. It sharpened.

For three months, the sensation had lingered at the base of his skull like a low-frequency tone—pervasive and sourceless. In hyperspace, it should have flattened into white noise. Instead, it resolved into a jagged edge. It had direction now. It wasn't louder, but it was clearer, emanating from a point ahead of them and below the galactic disk. It was a vector. A heading his instincts recognized before his mind could name it.

His grip tightened on the yoke. The dread wasn't fading; it was pulling, a compass needle swinging toward true north. Three months of ambient anxiety had crystallized into a purpose. Something out there wanted to be found.

Beside him, Desh was already asleep, his breathing rhythmic and deep. No intuition. No metaphysical interference. Just a soldier resting before the work began.

Ben watched the swirling light of the tunnel. He had recruited Desh because the man was blind to this. Because Desh would follow a heading without needing to know what waited at the coordinates.

But the wrongness knew. It was drawing him toward a specific destination, and Ben could no longer tell if he was the hunter or the prey.

He wondered if the distinction even mattered.
