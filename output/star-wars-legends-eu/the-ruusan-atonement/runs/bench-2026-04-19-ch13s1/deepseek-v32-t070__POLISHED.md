Kolven filled the viewport, green and blue and temperate, a perfect settlement world painted across the stars.

"Atmospheric composition nominal," Desh said from the sensor console. His voice was flat, professional. "Surface temperature readings… inconsistent."

Ben kept his hands loose on the co-pilot yoke. "Define inconsistent."

"The eastern hemisphere shows a gradient of twenty-three degrees across five hundred kilometers. The western hemisphere reads uniform eighteen." Desh tapped the display. "That's not meteorology. That's a system error."

"Or a system," Ben said.

The *Steady Returns* broke orbit. The planet's details resolved—forests, rivers, the geometric grids of settlement towns. Everything looked intact. Everything looked normal.

"Vegetation signatures," Desh continued. "Primary scan says deciduous broadleaf. Secondary says coniferous needle. Tertiary says… fungal mat."

Sera stood behind them, her hands resting on the backs of their chairs. "Three contradictory bioscans from the same patch of forest."

"The sensors aren't malfunctioning," Desh said. "They're receiving three different truths."

Ben adjusted the approach vector. "Veraine's field test."

"Yes." Sera's tone was clipped. "She collapsed a settlement world. Not destroyed. Collapsed."

The landing pads were cracked but intact, duracrete slabs laid out in orderly rows beside low, functional buildings. Ben set the ship down with a gentle thump of landing struts. The external sensors registered ambient temperature twenty-one degrees. Perfect.

He powered down the engines. "Everyone suited?"

Torin checked his belt. "Armor, comms, basic scanpack."

Kael leaned against the bulkhead, watching the hatch. "No birds."

Ben glanced at him. "Scan shows avian populations."

"Scan shows three different forests." Kael's gaze didn't move from the hatch. "I'm looking at the trees through the viewport. No movement. No birds."

Ben stood. "Then we look closer."

He led them down the ramp. The air was clean, slightly cool, carrying the scent of vegetation and damp soil. Normal. Too normal.

His boots touched duracrete. The surface was solid, textured, exactly as landing pad duracrete should feel. His balance tilted anyway, a subtle unmoored sensation like standing on a deck that wasn't quite level.

He reached for the Force to steady himself.

His perception fired. Energy answered. Presence answered. Potential answered.

No structure answered.

He took a sharp breath, held it, let it out slow. The ground of sense had dropped out. He stood on solid duracrete but felt like he was floating.

"Streets are traceable," Torin said, moving ahead. He pointed between buildings. "Grid pattern. No structural damage."

Sera walked to the nearest wall. "Flowers."

They bloomed in shadow, vibrant purple clusters clinging to the shaded northern face of a warehouse. Flowers didn't bloom in shadow. They needed light.

Ben joined her. The petals were open, healthy, glistening with moisture. "Wrong side of the wall."

"Not just wrong side." Sera touched a vine. "Watch."

The vine coiled around a support beam. As Ben watched, the tip uncurled, reversed direction, began climbing downward toward the ground. It moved slowly, deliberately, like a creature reconsidering its path.

"Growth reversal," Ben said.

"Visible reversal." Sera's voice tightened. "While we watch."

Kael stood in the middle of the street, his head tilted. "Silence isn't right."

Desh unclipped his chrono from his belt. He held it up, watching the second hand.

It ticked forward. Tick. Tick. Tick.

It skipped back two seconds.

Tick. Tick.

Skipped forward four.

Desh's face went still. "Time's drifting."

"How much?" Ben asked.

"Measurable." Desh kept his eyes on the chrono. "The second hand isn't malfunctioning. It's receiving… inconsistent data."

A pressure settled under Ben's ribs. It wasn't pain. It was a hollow, aching weight, a grief without an object. He recognized it as Desh's—the man's quiet, practical distress bleeding into the de-structured field, becoming a sensation Ben could feel but not name. He should offer reassurance. He should reach out, find the right words, ease the burden.

The moral shape that would let him respond was absent. The lattice that gave intuition, that whispered *here, this way, comfort him*, was gone. The grief pressed against Ben's sternum and he could do nothing with it.

"Split up?" Torin suggested. "Cover more ground?"

"No." Ben's voice came out harder than he intended. "We stay together. We don't know what this environment does to perception."

He walked to the center of the street, away from the buildings, away from the wrong-growing vines. He widened his stance. Planted his feet. Closed his eyes.

He reached for the Force on purpose.

Energy answered. Presence answered. Potential answered.

No lattice answered. No axis. No light, no dark, no framework to hold the response. His perception fired into formless nothing, every trained instinct echoing in a room with no walls. He tried to sense direction, tried to feel the usual currents that told him which way was forward, which choice was balanced. There was only raw potential, a field of energy without landmarks.

He opened his eyes.

Sera watched him, her expression guarded. "What do you feel?"

"The Force is present," Ben said. "It's not absent. It's… de-structured."

Torin frowned. "Explain."

Ben shook his head. "I can't. It's like hearing sound without pitch. Feeling heat without temperature. The properties are gone."

Kael's dry voice cut through. "So our compasses are broken."

"Our compasses never existed," Ben said. "We just had a map we thought was the territory."

Desh's chrono skipped again. He didn't look at it. He stared at the buildings, the streets, the wrong-side flowers. "This is what she did. Veraine. She didn't destroy the world. She removed its… structural integrity."

"Not just the world," Sera said. She touched her own chest, a brief, unconscious gesture. "The Force here has no lattice. No light/dark distinction. No moral axis."

Ben felt the hollow grief under his ribs sharpen. Desh's distress was bleeding into the de-structured field, becoming a physical fact Ben could sense but not interpret. He wanted to say something. He wanted to find the right Jedi words, the calm assurance, the reminder that the Force was with them even when it felt strange.

The words didn't exist. The assurance didn't exist. The Force was with them, but it had no opinion.

"We should move," Torin said. "Find the center of the effect. See if there's a source."

Ben nodded. He led them down the street, boots on duracrete, eyes scanning the intact buildings. Everything stood. Everything looked functional. Everything was wrong.

A vine on a lamppost reversed direction while they passed it. Flowers bloomed in a doorway that had been sealed shut. The air temperature shifted—Ben felt a chill, then warmth, then chill again, no pattern to it.

Kael walked beside him, quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "The birds aren't missing. They're elsewhere."

"Elsewhere?"

"The scan said avian populations. They're here. But they're not in the trees." Kael pointed at a rooftop. "They're on the roofs. On the streets. In the windows. They've redistributed."

Ben looked. He saw a small brown bird perched on a duracrete curb, another sitting inside an open warehouse window, a third standing on a rooftop vent. No trees. They had moved to artificial structures.

"Why?" Torin asked.

"The trees are wrong," Kael said. "The birds know."

They reached a central plaza, an open space with a dry fountain and benches. The fountain's basin was empty, cracked, but the benches looked recently used. The air here held a different quality—not colder or warmer, but thinner, like the atmosphere had been stretched.

Ben stopped. He closed his eyes again.

He reached.

Energy answered. Presence answered. Potential answered.

No lattice answered.

He tried to feel for Sera, for Torin, for Kael, for Desh. He could sense their presence—five living beings in the plaza. He could not sense their emotions, their intentions, their moral alignment. They were points of life, undefined.

He tried to feel for the planet itself, for Kolven's spirit, for the usual whisper of a world's character. There was only presence, vast and formless, like an ocean without currents.

He opened his eyes. "It's uniform. The whole zone is de-structured. No gradient."

Sera's posture was rigid, her hands clasped in front of her. "Then her test was successful. She can create a field this large. Stable. Uniform."

"Stable isn't the word," Torin said. He gestured at a vine reversing on a bench. "It's actively drifting."

"Stably drifting," Sera corrected. "The drift is consistent. Measurable. She's not destroying the lattice. She's removing it. The properties that remain… drift."

Desh sat on one of the benches. He placed his chrono on the seat beside him. The second hand ticked, skipped, ticked, skipped. He didn't watch it. He watched the dry fountain.

"I feel," Desh said slowly, "like I've lost something. Not a person. Not a memory. A… category."

Ben stood beside him. The grief under his ribs was Desh's, but now it had blended with his own, a hollow ache that had no name. "The lattice gives moral shape to the Force. It's the framework that lets us feel right and wrong as tangible things. When it's gone, the feeling of wrongness… loses its object."

Desh looked at him. "So my grief has no target."

"Yes."

"But I'm still grieving."

"Yes."

Desh picked up his chrono. The second hand had skipped so far it was now counting backward for three ticks, then forward for two. "Time is grieving too."

They stood in the plaza for a long moment. The wrong-side flowers bloomed. The vines reversed. The birds sat on benches and rooftops, silent.

Torin finally said, "We need to decide. Do we search for a control point? Do we try to measure the field's boundaries? Do we leave?"

Ben felt the question hang in the air. He felt his own mind working, assessing options, weighing risks. He felt the absence of the Force's intuition, the silent safety net that usually whispered *this way, not that way*. He heard his thoughts clearly, uncushioned.

*Leave. Get the team out. Report. Let the Council handle it.*

*Stay. Find the source. Understand. Stop Veraine.*

*The Council will debate. They'll send scouts. They'll lose time.*

*We could lose more than time.*

He heard the thoughts, sharp and distinct, his own voice in his own head. No softening. No gentle guidance. Just options, consequences, fears.

In the silence of the de-structured Force, Ben heard himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
