Kolven looked like a postcard.

Ben held that thought while the *Steady Returns* dropped through the cloud layer — green-blue, temperate, the kind of world that showed up in emigration holos to make farmers consider their options. The viewport framed it perfectly: the northern continent's agricultural belt, the settlement clusters, the clean geometry of irrigation channels still visible from orbit.

"Temperature gradient, northern sector," Desh said from the sensor station, his voice carrying the flat tone he used when data was actively annoying him. "Reading a forty-degree differential between the settlement perimeter and the center, and I cannot find a terrain feature that explains it."

"Atmospheric phenomenon?" Torin offered.

"Vegetation signatures are wrong too." Desh scrolled through the readout. "The canopy coverage reads as mature forest, but the spectral return is off. Like the leaves are facing the wrong direction."

Ben kept his hands loose on the co-pilot yoke. The instruments and the viewport were having two separate conversations and he'd learned, in twelve years of Jedi field work, that when that happened you trusted the instruments. He reached for the Force — a habit, like breathing, a quick ambient check of what lay below — and got back the usual answer: life, presence, the low pulse of a living world.

Except it didn't feel like a living world. It felt like a signal with the carrier wave removed.

He filed that and didn't say it.

"No birds," Kael said.

Everyone looked at him.

He was leaning against the forward bulkhead, arms crossed, watching the viewport with the expression he used when he'd already decided something and was waiting for everyone else to arrive. "Settlement world, temperate, agricultural. Should have birds. Flocks of them, this time of day, this latitude. Nothing."

Ben looked at the viewport again. Kael was right. The sky was perfectly, completely empty.

"Well," Ben said. "That's not unsettling at all."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't expected them to.

---

The landing pad cracked under the *Steady Returns* but held. The cracks were old — stress fractures in the duracrete, not fresh damage — and the pad's surface lights still blinked their patient amber sequence, running on whatever automated system had kept this place cycling through its routines for however long it had been empty. Months, maybe. The Ankhural files hadn't given them a date for when Kolven's population had stopped returning comms.

Ben was the third one down the ramp. Sera had her hand on her lightsaber hilt. Torin had his eyes up, scanning the rooflines. Kael came last, moving slow, the way he did when he was paying close attention to something he hadn't named yet.

The air tasted flat. Not stale — there was wind, and it carried the green smell of vegetation — but flat in a way Ben couldn't locate in any single sense. He put his boot on the pad surface, felt the solid resistance of duracrete, and reached for the Force.

The Force was there.

That was the wrong part. It was there — he could feel the presence of it, energy and potential, the raw material of everything he'd ever done with it — but the reach produced nothing to grab. No direction. No weight. No sense of where his next step should fall or whether the figure moving in the shadow of the nearest building was threat or debris. His perception fired and kept firing and the responses came back with nothing to compare themselves to.

He stood on solid ground and his balance said otherwise.

Torin had stopped walking. He was standing in the middle of the street, very still, looking at his hands.

"It's not gone," Sera said. She had moved to the wall of the nearest building, pressing her palm flat against the stone. Her voice was controlled in the way that took effort. "I can feel it. But there's no—" She stopped.

"No axis," Ben said.

She looked at him. "Yes."

Torin lowered his hands. "I keep reaching for orientation and finding none. It's like trying to read a compass that shows magnetic north everywhere simultaneously."

"That's a cheerful image," Kael said.

"Desh." Ben turned. "Chrono."

Desh had already pulled it out. He held it up so Ben could see the face — a good Corellian mechanical, the kind that kept time on atmospheric worlds without needing a sync signal. The second hand moved, stuttered, moved again, stuttered, then ran three seconds in a single lurch before settling back into its stutter.

"Consistent with the sector readings," Desh said. He put the chrono away with the deliberate care of a man who didn't want to look at it anymore. "Been doing that since we broke atmosphere."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. The way he stood, slightly apart from the group, told Ben enough. Something was pressing on Desh that had no name attached to it. A weight that had arrived with the atmosphere and hadn't lifted. Ben recognized the shape of it — a grief without an object, a loss that couldn't find what it had lost because the thing's moral shape had been removed from the Force itself. He knew what it was and knew he couldn't fix it, and that knowledge sat under his ribs like a stone he hadn't put there.

Normally he'd have something to offer. A sense of the other person's state, a read on what they needed, the quiet guidance of intuition that told him when to speak and when to stay back.

He had nothing. The stone sat there and he had no idea what to do with it.

---

The vegetation was the worst part.

Sera and Torin had moved to examine a storefront three buildings down, where flowering vines had climbed the wall facing away from the sun. The flowers were open on the shaded side, closed on the lit side, which was wrong, and Ben knew it was wrong before he could articulate the specific wrongness. He stood at the mouth of the street and watched a section of vine — a meter of green stem, half a dozen leaves — slowly reverse its growth direction. Not fast. Not dramatically. Just: a curl that had been going one way was now going another way, the way a conversation might drift if you looked away for a moment.

"It's doing it while I watch," Sera said. She didn't sound frightened. She sounded like someone who had just had a hypothesis confirmed in a way she hadn't wanted confirmed.

"The whole settlement?" Torin asked.

Ben turned a slow circle. The buildings were intact. Streets were traceable. Someone had built here, lived here, planted gardens and set up irrigation and put amber lights on the landing pads. All of that remained. But the vines had grown over the wrong sides of walls, and the flowers had opened facing shadow, and there were no birds, and the chrono stuttered, and the Force was everywhere and nowhere and he couldn't tell if the person standing twenty meters away from him was afraid.

He widened his stance. Planted his feet on the cracked duracrete.

And reached.

Not the ambient check he'd done from orbit — this was deliberate, a full reach, the way he'd been trained to extend perception across a space and read it. The Force answered. It answered immediately and completely, a flood of presence and energy and raw potential, and there was absolutely nothing to do with any of it. No direction. No light, no dark, no sense of shape or weight or moral texture. His perception extended and kept extending and found no edge, no wall, no point at which the information resolved into something actionable.

He pulled back.

The ground under his feet was solid. He checked. It was solid.

"Confirmed," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him.

"Confirmed," Sera agreed.

Kael was looking at the storefront with the closed flowers. "She used an entire settlement world for a field test."

Nobody answered that. The math was obvious: if Veraine could do this to Kolven as a proof of concept, then whatever she was building toward at Veranthos was not a test.

Ben stood in the street and breathed the flat green air and reached again — not for the Force this time, just checking, making sure his own thoughts were still his — and they were. That was the thing. They were entirely, completely his. Every instinct, every preference, every flicker of judgment that he'd spent his whole life assuming was the Force's gentle guidance or the lattice's quiet correction.

All his.

Including the ones that were afraid.

Including the ones that had looked at Torin's stillness in the middle of the street and wondered, for the first time, whether the man's certainty was something to trust.

Including the one that had been there for years, quiet and cushioned, that said: *you don't actually know if your choices are yours.*

The Force gave him nothing to push that thought back with. No comfort, no reframe, no sense that the question was smaller than it felt.

He stood on Kolven's cracked landing pad in the silence where his intuition used to be, and the thought sat there, and it was his, and there was nothing in the universe right now to tell him it was wrong.
