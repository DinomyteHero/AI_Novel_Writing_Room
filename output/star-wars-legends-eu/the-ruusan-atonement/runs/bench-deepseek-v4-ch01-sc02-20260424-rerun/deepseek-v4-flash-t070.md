The datapad slid across the desk and stopped at the edge, close enough for Ben to read the header without reaching. Seventeen flagged survey reports. Same format, same analyst's code, same stamped anomaly marker in the corner of each.

He reached anyway. His hand was on the pad before his mind caught up with what the gesture meant.

"Outer Rim," Luke said. His voice carried the particular calm of someone who had already decided what he was about to say. "Clustered along a bearing from Exodo II through the Veranthos corridor. The analysts flagged them as sensor artifacts."

Ben scrolled. Columns of coordinates, spectral readings, Force-register notes from the survey teams. Standard format. Standard everything except the anomaly marker and the pattern his eyes found before his brain finished processing: three readings, spaced across four weeks and three different survey ships, that matched the wrongness he had been carrying since before he could name it.

The same sourness under the Temple's usual harmonics. The same gap where something should have been.

"Not artifacts," he said.

"No."

Ben zoomed on the first of the three. Exodo II approach vector, deep-range scan. The report's language was careful — *unusual Force-register variance, recommend secondary investigation* — but the numbers underneath told a different story. A dead spot. A place where the Force had gone quiet in a way that wasn't silence.

His father's office hummed around him. The holocomm on the desk, the stack of flimsies by the lamp, the low wash of Coruscant traffic filtering through the transparisteel behind Luke's chair. A working room in a working building, and Ben sat in it holding evidence that the galaxy was coming apart somewhere most people couldn't see.

"I flagged them six weeks ago," Luke said. "Sat on them. Wanted to be sure before I moved them up the chain."

"And you're sure now."

"I'm sure something is there. I'm not sure what." Luke leaned back. His hands stayed on the desk, still and easy, the way they always did when he was about to let someone else carry the weight. "I need a Knight who can fly his own ship, read a survey report, and tell me whether this is a geological anomaly or something the Order needs to know about. You're the best pilot I have who also happens to be a Jedi."

Ben kept scrolling. The second reading matched the first. The third matched the second. Same signature, same wrongness, same thing he had felt in his quarters at night when he couldn't sleep and the Force went thin around him like a room with one wall missing.

His father was describing an anomaly.

Ben was recognizing a frequency he had lived inside since he was a boy.

"Survey route runs straight along the bearing," he said. "If I take a direct line from Exodo through Veranthos, I cover all three readings in two standard weeks."

"That was my thinking."

"And if I find something?"

Luke held his gaze for a moment. The silence between them was warm and familiar and carried everything neither of them would say out loud. *I trust you. I am giving you this alone because I trust you. I am not asking why you need it alone.*

"Then you come back and tell me," Luke said. "And we decide together what comes next."

Ben set the datapad down.

The wrongness sat in the room with them, faint as a note played slightly flat, present in the space between his father's words. Ben could feel it even here, in the heart of the Temple, surrounded by Jedi and light and the steady pulse of a thousand years of institutional certainty. The wrongness didn't care about any of that. It was patient. It had been patient for a very long time.

He stood and moved to the window.

Coruscant spread below him in bands of light and shadow, speeder lanes threading between towers, the whole machine of the Republic turning beneath a sky that never got dark enough to see stars. He had grown up in this city. He had learned to read a lightsaber here, learned to feel the Force here, learned what it meant to be a Skywalker in a galaxy that never stopped needing one.

His shoulders settled. The readiness that came before a mission, the shift from waiting to moving, the moment when the problem became a route and a ship and a departure time.

"I'll file the flight plan today," he said. "Leave in the morning."

"Take whatever resources you need."

"I won't need much."

Luke didn't push. The silence stretched, and Ben felt it as a question his father was choosing not to ask. *Do you want a partner? Do you want someone to watch your back? Do you want to tell me what you're really feeling?*

None of those questions landed.

"I'll brief the Council when you're en route," Luke said. "They'll have the survey reports by then. I'd rather they hear about the mission after you've already left."

"Sounds like a plan."

Ben turned from the window. His father was still sitting at the desk, hands still resting on the surface, watching him with the particular expression of a man who had learned to read his son's silences the way other people read words.

"I've felt it too," Luke said. Evenly. Quietly. The way he landed hard things, without performance or preamble. "Not as clearly as you. But I've felt it."

The room went still.

Ben felt the words land in his chest like a hand on his shoulder, brief and warm and gone before he could decide what to do with them. His father saw the anomaly. Ben saw the wound. The distinction mattered and he couldn't name it, not here, not now, not when the mission was sitting on the desk between them waiting to be picked up.

"Then you know why I need to go alone," he said.

Luke held his gaze. The silence was careful, love-laden, full of things neither of them would say.

"I know," Luke said. "Be careful."

"I'm always careful."

"You're always capable. Those are different things."

Ben picked up the datapad. The weight of it was familiar, the same standard-issue pad every Knight carried, but the information on it felt heavier than durasteel. He tucked it under his arm and moved toward the door.

"Dad," he said, and stopped.

Luke waited.

The word hung between them, warm and rare, carrying more than Ben had meant to put into it. He didn't turn around.

"I'll report when I reach Exodo."

"I'll be here."

Ben stepped through the door and let it close behind him. The corridor stretched ahead, empty and quiet, the Temple's usual hum settling around him like water closing over a stone. He didn't look back.

The datapad felt light in his hand. The wrongness felt heavier than anything he had ever carried.

He walked.
