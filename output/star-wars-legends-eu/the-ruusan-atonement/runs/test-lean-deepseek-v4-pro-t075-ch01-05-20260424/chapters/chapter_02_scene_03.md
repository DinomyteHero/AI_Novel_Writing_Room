The second world was drier than the first. The air had a baked, alkaline taste, and the vegetation — what little of it had survived the long slide into desert — grew in tight, defensive clusters that looked more like geological formations than plants. The ruins sat on a low plateau above a dry riverbed, their stone a pale grey that caught the afternoon light and threw it back hard enough to make Ben squint.

He didn't need to touch the stone to know. The wrongness was waiting for him the moment the *Steady Returns* had broken atmosphere — stronger here than at the first site, a pressure that sat behind his eyes and made the horizon feel fractionally too close, as if the world had been built to a scale that was almost right.

"Same hand," he said.

Desh stood a few meters back, datapad in one hand, the other shading his eyes against the glare. "You haven't looked at the markings yet."

"I don't need to."

He crouched beside the nearest exposed wall anyway. The stone was a dense grey basalt, nothing like the reddish mineral of the first world. Different geology. Different climate. Different star in the sky. And carved into the face of the block at the same precise angle, with the same tool-marks that didn't match any tradition Ben knew, were the markings.

He reached out. Stopped his hand a centimeter short of the surface.

The wrongness pushed back against his palm before he made contact — a fractional resistance, the way two magnets repelled each other when you brought the wrong poles together. His body registered the discrepancy as a warning his conscious mind hadn't asked for. The distance between his skin and the stone felt wrong in a way that had nothing to do with measurement.

He touched the groove.

The vibration hit harder this time. Not pain — a low note played through his bones, a wrongness that traveled up his arm and settled in his chest like a second heartbeat running slightly out of phase. The Force said his hand was touching the stone. The Force also said his hand was somewhere else, six inches to the left, or maybe six inches behind him, or maybe not touching anything at all. The three impressions arrived simultaneously and none of them cancelled the others out.

He pulled his hand back.

The vibration stopped. The stone was just stone again. The pressure behind his eyes remained.

"You're making the face," Desh said.

Ben stood. His knees cracked — he'd been crouching too long, his body tensed against a threat it couldn't locate. "What face?"

"The one from the first world. The one that says you felt something you're not putting in the report."

Ben didn't answer. He pulled out his datapad and started cataloging — the markings, the tool-work, the erosion patterns, the geological context. His hands moved with the same professional precision they'd had on the first world, and the omission was already hardening into habit before he'd finished the first scan.

The report would include everything the first report had included. It would not include what had happened when he'd touched the stone.

"Ben."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He finished the last scan and pocketed the datapad. The wind had picked up, carrying grit that stung his face and made the ruins look even older than they were, the grey stone blurring into the grey sky the way old things blurred into the landscape when no one was looking. A contrail arced overhead — high atmosphere, probably a freighter on the Coreward run, too far up to be anything but a white scratch against the blue. People moved through this space without stopping. Without knowing what was sitting half-buried in the dust beneath them.

"Two worlds," Ben said. "Different stone. Different climate. Same hand."

"That's not a coincidence."

"No."

He turned back toward the landing pad. Desh fell into step beside him, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The silence had a different quality than it had on the first world — less careful, more focused. The silence of two people who had worked together long enough to know when talking would slow things down.

---

The *Steady Returns* smelled like recycled air and the faint ozone tang of a nav computer that had been running too long. Ben dropped into the copilot's seat while Desh pulled up the chart on the main display — a holographic projection of the sector, the two survey worlds marked in green, the space between them a web of established lanes and charted systems that stopped abruptly at a boundary Ben had never had reason to notice before.

Desh overlaid the two sites. A line snapped into place between them, clean and deliberate, cutting through the charted sector and extending past the boundary into a region the display rendered as empty black.

"That's the bearing," Desh said.

Ben stared at the line. It ran directly along the route Luke had laid out for the survey — the sequence of worlds Ben was supposed to visit, the order he was supposed to visit them in, the corridor through the Outer Rim that his father had marked on the mission briefing like it was any other assignment.

Coincidence or design. Ben didn't know. He didn't want to know yet.

"How far does it go?"

Desh adjusted the display. The line extended past the chart boundary, past the last registered system in the sector, past anything the nav computer had a name for. It terminated in a cluster of coordinates the display couldn't resolve — systems that didn't appear on any chart, stars that weren't supposed to be there, space that wasn't supposed to exist.

"Far as I can push it," Desh said. "After that it's just math. The bearing keeps going."

Ben leaned forward and punched the coordinates into the nav computer. The system hummed, processing, its status light flickering amber as it tried to reconcile the input against a database that had nothing to offer. The display held steady — the line, the empty sector, the cluster of unresolved points at the edge of the chart.

The hum settled. The status light went green. The nav computer had accepted the coordinates as valid input, even if it couldn't tell him what was waiting at the other end.

"Something's out there," Ben said.

"Yeah." Desh's voice was flat, the operational deadpan he defaulted to when the situation had stopped being routine and he didn't want to make a thing of it. "Something that someone went to a lot of trouble to hide from modern records."

Ben didn't answer. He was watching the line on the display — the bearing that ran exactly along his father's survey route, the empty sector that stared back at him like a question he hadn't asked yet. The wrongness pressed at the edge of his awareness, stronger now than it had been on the ground, as if the nav computer had given it a shape. A direction. A place to go.

Desh shifted in his seat. Ben caught the movement in his peripheral vision — the set of his shoulders, the way he was reading Ben's tension and choosing not to press. The friendship held. The scope had outgrown the job, and Desh knew it, and he was waiting for Ben to say what came next.

Ben didn't say anything. He reached out and killed the display, and the line vanished, and the empty sector went dark, and the coordinates sat in the nav computer's memory like a promise he hadn't decided whether to keep.

The bearing pointed to a cluster of systems that did not appear on any navigation chart Ben had ever seen. Something was out there.
