Ben typed the report for the third time.

His fingers moved across the console with the particular rhythm of someone who had drafted enough mission summaries to do it in his sleep — survey designation, system coordinates, anomalous readings, recommendation. The words assembled themselves into the shape of compliance. *Perimeter facility, active power, unknown origin. Message content: seal failure warning. Recommend immediate follow-up investigation.*

He read it.

He deleted it.

The buffer showed three drafts in the queue, each one a slightly different version of the same honest report. The first had been terse, operational, the kind of thing he'd have filed from a GAG deployment without thinking twice. The second had included the Force wrongness — a paragraph he'd cut before finishing, because describing the pressure in his chest in a mission report to the Grand Master of the Jedi Order was the kind of thing that triggered questions he wasn't ready to answer. The third had been the most complete, the most professional, the version that would have made it to the transmission queue if he'd let his fingers finish the last line.

He hadn't.

The *Steady Returns* sat quiet around him, the cockpit lit only by the console displays and the pale scatter of stars through the viewport. The perimeter facility was somewhere behind them now, receding into the distance at sublight while Ben sat in the pilot's seat and wrote reports he kept not sending. The wrongness in the Force pressed against his awareness — not the directional pull he'd felt in the data core, not yet, but something closer to a weight that had settled into the air and refused to dissipate. It sat at the edge of his perception the way a pending decision sat at the edge of a conversation, waiting for someone to name it.

He started the fourth draft.

The first line appeared on the display. *To: Grand Master Skywalker. Subject: Survey Anomaly —*

Footsteps in the corridor. The particular cadence of someone who'd spent years moving through ships quietly and didn't bother announcing himself.

Desh leaned over the copilot's shoulder and read the first line.

"You're not sending that."

Not a question. Ben didn't turn.

"Not yet."

The word hung in the cockpit air — not a denial, not a defense. Just the first time the withholding had been spoken aloud, and spoken by Desh, who had served in the same machine and knew the signature of institutional avoidance when he saw it.

Ben felt the recognition land. It didn't feel like accusation. It felt like a door closing — the private hesitation becoming a shared choice, the abstract delay becoming something operational and irreversible. The wrongness in the Force pulled harder, directional now, orienting toward the coordinates they'd copied from the data core.

Desh didn't push. He moved around the copilot's station and dropped into the seat, his hands finding the nav computer's interface with the economy of someone who'd made the same motion a thousand times. The fortress coordinates were still in the buffer — the string of numbers and reference points that pointed to a region of space that didn't exist on any modern chart.

"You want me to plot it."

Ben looked at the display. The fourth draft sat unfinished, the cursor blinking at the end of the subject line. The comm system was ready. The transmission queue was empty. All he had to do was finish the report and press send, and the choice would belong to someone else.

The wrongness pressed against his chest. The seal was failing. The survivors — if there were survivors — were waiting on a clock that had been running for a thousand years and was now counting down in hours.

"Yeah," Ben said. "Plot it."

Desh's hands moved across the nav computer. The fortress coordinates locked into the system, and the display shifted — the clean lines of known hyperspace routes replaced by the jagged, probabilistic geometry of uncharted navigation. The computer began calculating, cycling through course corrections and probability matrices, building a route through a sector that had been deliberately hidden from every astrographic survey in modern history.

Ben watched it work. The rationalized certainty was hardening in his chest, settling into something that felt almost like inevitability. The seal's degradation. The urgency. The committee that would take two weeks to decide what he already knew. The justifications lined up neatly, each one a reason not to press send, and beneath them the thing he wasn't examining — the particular relief of action over explanation, the pull toward the coordinates that felt more like instinct than analysis.

"Desh."

"Mm."

"You don't have to —"

"I know." Desh didn't look up from the nav computer. "I'm not doing it because I have to."

The display cycled through another probability matrix. The route was resolving — a series of micro-jumps and course corrections that would carry them deeper into uncharted space, toward a fortress that had been built to contain something someone had considered worth hiding from the rest of the galaxy.

Ben didn't say thank you. The words felt wrong for what Desh was doing — not a favor, not loyalty in the clean sense, but something closer to complicity. Desh had read the pattern and chosen to follow it. He hadn't challenged the withholding. He hadn't asked Ben to explain why a Jedi Knight was failing to report an active crisis to his own father. He'd just reached for the nav computer and waited for the answer.

The cost of that sat at the edge of Ben's awareness, unexamined. Someone who could have stopped him had chosen to enable him instead. The isolation was deepening, and the person who might have named it had just become part of it.

The nav computer chimed. Route calculated.

"Got it," Desh said. "Three micro-jumps to avoid the worst of the uncharted mass shadows. After that, it's a straight burn to the coordinates. Assuming the fortress is where the data says it is."

"And if it's not?"

"Then we're in uncharted space with a nav computer that just built a route to nowhere." Desh's voice was flat, operational, the particular deadpan he used when a situation had stopped being routine and he'd decided not to make it a discussion. "Same as every other survey run you've dragged me on."

Ben reached for the hyperdrive controls. His hand was steady — the steadiness of a decision that had already been made, that had been made the moment he'd deleted the first draft and known he was going to delete the second. The comm system was still ready. The transmission queue was still empty. The report was still unfinished.

He didn't look at it.

"Jumping."

The *Steady Returns* stretched into hyperspace.

The transition should have been smooth — the familiar lurch of realspace folding into the blue tunnel of a calculated jump, the wrongness in the Force fading with distance the way it always faded when you put enough light-years between yourself and the source. That was how distance worked. That was how the Force worked. You moved away from a disturbance, and the disturbance diminished.

It didn't.

The wrongness intensified.

It came through the transition as pressure — not the ambient weight Ben had felt in the corridor, not the directional pull of the data core, but something that pressed against the bulkheads from the outside, as if the ship were descending through a medium that didn't want to let it pass. The deck plates hummed at a frequency that didn't match the engines. The stars in the viewport were the familiar blue streaks of hyperspace, but they looked wrong — stretched, compressed, the distance not behaving the way distance should.

Ben's fingers tightened on the armrest.

"What?" Desh was watching him, not the displays. "What is it?"

"The wrongness." Ben's voice came out flat, controlled, the register he used when he was bracing against something and didn't want it to show. "It's not fading."

"It's hyperspace. Everything fades in hyperspace."

"Not this."

Desh looked at the viewport. At the nav computer. At the displays that showed nothing wrong — engines nominal, course steady, all systems green. A non-Force-sensitive reading instruments that told him everything was fine while Ben sat beside him and felt the pressure of a failing seal pressing through the hull.

"How bad?"

Ben didn't answer. The wrongness was intensifying — not sharply, not in a way that suggested immediate danger, but in a way that suggested they were moving toward something rather than away from it. The seal's failure was no longer abstract. It was the medium they were traveling through, the pressure against the ship, the spatial distortion that made hyperspace feel like a tunnel that was slowly collapsing behind them.

They were heading toward the source.

The decision locked in. There was no return to the comm system now, no option to send the delayed report, no way to reverse the jump until they reached the coordinates or found a reversion point. Ben and Desh were committed — to the route, to the fortress, to whatever was waiting at the center of a containment network that had been failing for a thousand years.

Ben was alone with the knowledge. Desh had chosen to be alone with him.

The cost of that choice sat unexamined at the edge of his awareness — the loyalty that enabled avoidance, the friend who had read the pattern and followed it instead of challenging it. Ben didn't name it. The pressure of the seal's degradation, the survivors waiting, the urgency that had become justification — these were enough to keep the doubt suppressed, to keep the rationalized certainty intact, to keep him from looking too closely at what he was asking Desh to carry.

The *Steady Returns* plunged deeper into uncharted space. The wrongness pressed against the hull. The blue of hyperspace stretched wrong around them, and Ben Skywalker sat in the pilot's seat and did not reach for the comm system, and the silence between him and Desh was the silence of two people who had both decided not to stop what was happening.
