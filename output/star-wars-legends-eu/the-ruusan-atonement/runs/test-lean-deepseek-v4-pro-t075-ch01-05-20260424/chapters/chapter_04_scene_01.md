The plateau was a lie told in stone.

From the ridge where the *Steady Returns* had set down, the mountainside read as unbroken geology — a sweep of grey rock and wind-scoured faces climbing toward a sky too pale to hold warmth. The wrongness that had pressed against the hull during the descent was everywhere now, a weight that sat behind Ben's sternum and pushed outward, as if the thin air had found something inside him to occupy. His breath steamed in the cold and his lungs burned slightly with each inhalation, the altitude registering as a low physical pressure that his body kept trying to compensate for and couldn't.

They'd been walking for twenty minutes before Desh stopped.

"That's not a mountain."

Ben followed his gaze. The formation ahead was weathered stone and metal alloy, its lines so precisely integrated into the rock face that even now, with the angle resolving into geometry, his eyes kept trying to read it as natural. No seam where the construction met the mountain. No symbols. No ornament. Just a vertical face of durasteel-grey surface that had been wearing the same patina as the surrounding cliffs for a thousand years, and angles that were wrong the way a door in a dream is wrong — you couldn't say what was off about it, only that it had been built by someone who didn't think the way you did.

Desh pulled his jacket tighter. "Defensible."

"Mm."

"No extraction plan. The walls are thicker than anything we brought."

Ben didn't answer. The wrongness was directional now — not the ambient pressure of the plateau but something focused, shaped, moving through the rock like a held breath waiting to be released. It concentrated at the fortress face, at the seam his eyes still couldn't find, at the absence of an entrance that was itself the entrance.

"It's keyed," he said.

"Keyed to what?"

The answer was under his palm before he'd finished forming the question. The mechanism was set into the stone at chest height — a smooth panel of metal alloy that didn't match the surrounding patina, its surface clean and dark and slightly warm despite the cold. No visible controls. No interface. Just a surface that had been waiting for a hand for a thousand years.

Ben pressed his palm against it.

The resistance was immediate. Not physical — his hand met stone, solid and unyielding — but something else, a subtle repulsion that pushed back against his presence in the Force the way a held breath pushes against a closed door. The panel pulsed once, amber light bleeding through the metal in a slow wave that started at the edges and gathered toward the center, scanning.

The amber deepened.

Held.

Ben reached for the technique he'd been trained in — the clean, disciplined signature of a modern Jedi Knight, the particular way the Order taught its students to present themselves to ancient mechanisms and locked doors and the thousand small security systems that still recognized the Order's authority. He'd done this a dozen times on a dozen worlds, and the doors had always opened.

The amber didn't shift.

He tried again, pushing harder, the technique sharpening into something more deliberate. The light flickered — a moment of hesitation that pulled his attention tight — and then steadied back into amber, deeper now, the color of a wound that had closed wrong.

"The hell," Desh said quietly.

Ben's jaw tightened. His hand stayed pressed against the stone; he could feel the mechanism's pulse under his palm, a slow heartbeat that wasn't hostile but wasn't welcoming either. The wrongness here was discriminating. It knew what it was looking for, and Ben's modern training — the thing he'd spent his life building, the identity he'd earned through discipline and failure and the long slow work of becoming a Jedi Knight — wasn't it.

Alternatives cycled through his mind. Force the lock — possible, maybe, if he was willing to risk whatever the fortress had been designed to do to intruders. Find another entry — the mountain face was seamless, no secondary hatches, no ventilation shafts, nothing that read as a weak point. Retreat — report the coordinates, wait for a team, let someone else make the decision.

None of them sat right.

The wrongness pressed against his sternum. The seal was failing. The clock was running.

Ben stopped trying to impose his technique.

He opened his palm flat against the mechanism and reached deeper — not into the training, not into the discipline, not into the identity he'd built as a Jedi of the modern Order. He reached into the older thing. The thing that lived in his bloodline and didn't have a name he'd ever been taught, the signature that had opened doors before he knew how to name them and would open doors after he was gone. The thing the fortress had been built to recognize.

The amber light flickered.

Hesitated.

Shifted to green.

The moment the color changed, Ben felt something move in the Force — not a surge, not a revelation, but a recognition so specific it felt like a door opening inside him. The wrongness acknowledged him. The fortress knew what he was, and what he was wasn't the Jedi he'd trained to be. It was something older. Something the builders had expected. Something that had been waiting.

His hand dropped from the mechanism. The green light held.

"Ben." Desh's voice was flat, operational, the register he used when a situation had stopped being routine and he'd decided not to make it a discussion. "What just happened."

The door answered before Ben could.

The grinding started low in the stone — a sustained note that vibrated through the plateau and up through Ben's boots, the sound of surfaces that hadn't moved in a thousand years remembering how to move. The fortress face split along a seam that had been invisible until the moment it wasn't, stone and metal alloy separating with the slow inevitability of geology, and the air that spilled out carried a weight that Ben's lungs registered before his mind named it.

Staleness. Isolation. The particular deadness of atmosphere that had been sealed away before the Ruusan Reformations and never circulated since.

The wrongness shifted.

It had been external pressure on the plateau — directional, focused, the held-breath sensation of something waiting behind the stone. Now it was internal, concentrated, shaped. The corridor beyond the door was dark and the darkness had architecture — the wrongness moved through it like pressure through a pipe, channeled by walls that had been built to direct it, to contain it, to keep whatever was at the center from spilling outward before its time.

Desh pulled a glow rod from his belt and swept the entry corridor. The beam caught walls of the same weathered alloy as the exterior, floor plates that had been laid with the particular precision of builders who knew no one was coming to fix their mistakes, a ceiling that vanished into shadow. No symbols. No markings. No indication of who had built this place or why.

"Thousand years," Desh said. "The air hasn't moved in a thousand years."

Ben reached into the Force ahead of them.

The sense that came back wasn't blank. It was shaped — directed, almost architectural, the wrongness organized into channels and conduits and holding patterns that spoke of deliberate design rather than natural accumulation. Something had been maintained here. Something had been kept alive, or kept contained, or kept in a state that was neither living nor dead but suspended between them, waiting.

The wrongness moved through the rock like a held breath.

Something was alive in here.
