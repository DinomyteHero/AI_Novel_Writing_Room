Sera didn’t ask permission. She crossed to the archive console with the stride of someone who had walked this room a thousand times, and her hand found the activation plate before Ben finished the sentence he’d opened his mouth to offer.

The console hummed. Not the hesitant flicker Ben had gotten from the stasis-ring displays — a full, immediate response, the system recognizing a user it had been waiting for. Holographic light spilled across her face, and Ben saw her expression catch on something before it smoothed again. A thousand years, and the machine still knew her hand.

Desh shifted his weight behind Ben’s left shoulder. “That was fast.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was watching Sera’s fingers move across the interface — not the exploratory tapping of someone learning an unfamiliar system, but the precise, economical motion of a user who had built her muscle memory before the language the display was written in had evolved into the one Ben spoke. She wasn’t reading the archive. She was retrieving it.

His chest tightened. The wrongness he’d been feeling since they entered the fortress was still there — the missing-handrail sensation, the bad footing — but it had changed register. Before, it had been environmental. Now it was personal. The room was about to tell him something, and his body knew it before his mind had caught up.

Torin moved to Sera’s right. He didn’t crowd her — there was a practiced distance between them, the kind that came from working together in spaces where a wrong step could kill you. His hand brushed the console’s edge with the same familiarity Sera had shown, and Ben watched his fingers settle against the old metal the way a pilot’s hand settled on a throttle. Not thinking about it. Just home.

“Index, please,” Torin said, and his voice had changed. Still warm, but the warmth was layered over something older — the particular register of a man who had been telling stories in briefing rooms before the briefing rooms had a name. “Reformations-era institutional record. Unredacted.”

The holographic display shifted. A timeline bloomed above the console — dates, names, structural reorganizations, the kind of institutional mapping Ben had studied in his Knight trials. He recognized the shape of it. The Ruusan Reformations as every modern Jedi learned them: the Army of Light dissolved, the Order decentralized, the Jedi Lords’ era closed. A thousand years of peace built on the lessons of a war that had nearly destroyed the galaxy.

Except the timeline kept scrolling.

Ben’s focus tightened. The names were the same — Gavran, the council signatories, the charter revisions — but the causal chain was wrong. Decisions that his history texts had presented as philosophical corrections were listed here as *containment measures*. Doctrinal reforms that had been taught to him as ethical refinements showed up under a header he didn’t recognize, a word in Old Galactic Standard that the console’s translation matrix rendered as *penance-structure*.

Torin gestured at the display, and a secondary index pulled forward — personal logs, council minutes, facility-status reports. “You know what I love about modern Jedi?” he said, and the wryness in his voice was so casual that Ben almost missed the weight underneath it. “You still believe the Reformations were about philosophy.”

“They were about philosophy,” Ben said. The words came out before he’d decided to speak.

Torin looked at him. Not unkindly. “They were about what Gavran couldn’t bring himself to write down in the public charter.” He tapped the display. “This is the private version. The one the Order decided not to pass on.”

Ben’s breathing steadied. His mind was doing the thing it did under pressure — cataloguing, comparing, searching for the discrepancy he could fix. But the discrepancy wasn’t a broken console or a mistranslated glyph. It was the entire foundation of the institution he’d been raised inside, and it was sitting in front of him in a holographic index he couldn’t read because the language was a thousand years dead.

Kael moved.

Ben caught it from the corner of his eye — the man who had been standing against the wall with his back to the stone, silent since he’d spoken his name in the stasis ring. He stepped forward now, not fast, not hesitant, just there. His shoulder passed within a hand’s breadth of Sera’s, and she didn’t flinch. Didn’t acknowledge him either. The kind of not-acknowledging that came from long familiarity with someone whose presence you’d learned to accept without comment.

Kael reached past her and touched the display.

One word. He highlighted a single entry in the index — a date marker, Old Galactic Standard — and replaced it with another. The correction was so small that Ben almost missed it. A digit. The difference between the year the public Reformations charter was signed and the year the private penance protocols went into effect.

Kael didn’t explain. He stepped back, his hand dropping to his side, his eyes already returning to their default position — cataloguing exits, mapping threats, refusing to commit.

Ben stared at the corrected date. His vertigo sharpened. The survivors weren’t unified. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth, and the fact that Kael had chosen this moment — this detail — to break his silence meant something Ben couldn’t yet parse.

Sera didn’t look at Kael. She didn’t thank him. She accepted the correction the way she’d accepted the console’s activation — as a thing that had happened, and that she had already factored into her next move.

And then she spoke.

“The Ruusan Reformations were dual-purpose.” Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw from a millennium of disuse, but the syntax was precise — complete sentence, formal address, the particular cadence of someone delivering a finding she had waited a very long time to state aloud. “The public restructuring the Order remembers. And a private penance for what the Jedi Lords did at Veranthos.”

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t soften the sentence with context or apology. She delivered it the way she’d delivered her name in the stasis ring — complete, composed, and finished.

Ben felt the words land in his body before his intellect caught up.

It was a vertigo-like drop in his center of gravity — the sudden, sickening clarity of a door opening onto a room you hadn’t known was there. The Reformations weren’t a philosophical correction. They were damage control. The Order he’d been raised inside, the institution whose identity he’d never questioned, had been built on what Gavran chose not to say.

He didn’t ask what the penance was for. He didn’t need to. Sera’s face told him the answer wasn’t in this room — and that she was carrying it anyway, had been carrying it for a thousand years, would carry it until the moment she decided he was ready to hear it.

The archive hummed around them. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case, unopened, its faint light steady against the dark stone. Desh hadn’t moved from Ben’s shoulder, and Ben could feel the question he wasn’t asking — the operational assessment, the threat calculus, the particular silence of a man who had learned to wait for the Force-users to finish their revelation before he asked what it meant for the mission.

Ben looked at Sera — the instrument of precision, the edge that had been whetted against something harder than stone. At Torin — the keeper of narrative warmth, the foundation that had been holding a weight Ben was only beginning to understand. At Kael — the quiet correction, the monk’s brevity that had just spoken for the first time since waking, and had chosen to speak about a date.

Three witnesses to the Order’s hidden history. Three people who had waited a thousand years to say what Sera had just said in one composed sentence.

The weight of it settled into Ben’s shoulders — not as despair, but as a new clarity about what he was responsible for. He braced for what came next.
