The *Steady Returns* had set down on a pad that hadn't seen maintenance in thirty years.

Ben dropped the last meter from the boarding ramp onto packed earth, his boots kicking up a puff of reddish dust that the wind snatched away before it could settle. The air tasted dry and mineral, the way air tasted on worlds that had never supported anything more ambitious than scrub-grass and the occasional survey team that didn't stay long enough to name the place.

Desh came down behind him, a datapad already in his hand. "Landing beacon's civilian. Pre-Galactic Alliance, maybe older. Someone's been through here since the last official survey, but not recently enough to upgrade the hardware."

"How recently?"

Desh tilted the datapad so Ben could see the signal log. "Beacon's been pinging for at least twelve standard years. No registry in the local traffic net. Could be a smuggler waystation, could be a supply dump for the mining ops three systems over." He pocketed the datapad and scanned the horizon with the same flat, unhurried expression he'd worn through every mission briefing Ben had ever sat through with him. "Could be nothing."

"It's not nothing," Ben said.

The wrongness was stronger planetside. He'd felt it the moment the *Steady Returns* had dipped below the atmosphere — not a spike, not a warning, just a pressure that hadn't been there in orbit. The way a room felt different when someone was standing behind you, even before you turned around.

He didn't say that to Desh. Desh didn't need the Force to read a situation, and Ben didn't need to make his own unease someone else's problem.

The ruins were visible from the pad — a low sprawl of stone half a klick east, where the terrain rose into a ridge of wind-scoured rock. The structures were half-buried in the same reddish dust that coated Ben's boots, their outlines blurred by centuries of erosion. From this distance they looked almost natural, the way old ruins sometimes did, as if the stone had been trying to forget what it had been shaped into and was slowly succeeding.

"Sixty-year survey," Desh said, falling into step beside him. "Recorded as uninhabited. No prior settlement, no indigenous structures, no archaeological significance."

"That's what the report says."

"You don't sound convinced."

Ben didn't answer. He was watching the ruins as they walked, the way the light fell across the exposed stone, the shadows pooling in the cuts where wind and time had worn the edges soft. Something about the proportions was wrong. Not in a way he could name yet — just a shift in the relationship between the structures and the ground, the kind of discrepancy his body registered before his conscious mind could frame the question.

The hike took twenty minutes. The terrain was uneven, studded with loose rock and the occasional thorny bush that looked like it had evolved specifically to make survey work unpleasant. A contrail arced across the sky to the north — high atmosphere, probably a freighter on the Coreward run, too far up to be anything but a white scratch against the blue. The sector was flown through. People moved through this space without stopping, without looking down, without knowing what was sitting half-buried in the dust beneath them.

The first structure was a wall. Or what was left of one — a section of fitted stone rising maybe three meters out of the earth, its surface worn smooth on one side where the prevailing wind had been sanding it for centuries. The blocks were cut with a precision that Ben's training wanted to date to the Old Republic, but the tool-marks didn't match any tradition he knew. Too clean for pre-Republic masonry. Wrong angle on the chisel work. The stone itself was local — the same reddish mineral that made up the ridge — but the cutting had been done with tools that left a signature Ben had never seen in any archive.

He crouched beside the wall and ran his fingers over the surface without quite touching it, the way he'd been taught to examine artifacts that might be unstable. The stone was cool despite the sun. Dry. Unremarkable.

"You're making the face," Desh said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to tell me the report was wrong and we're standing on something that's going to take three weeks to catalog."

Ben didn't look up. "The report was wrong. We're standing on something older than the sixty-year survey."

"How much older?"

"I don't know yet."

He moved along the wall toward a section where the stone had fractured, exposing a cut that ran deeper than the surface erosion. The marking was deliberate — a groove carved into the block at an angle that wasn't structural, wasn't decorative, wasn't anything Ben recognized from the architectural traditions he'd studied. It was precise. Intentional. The kind of mark someone made when they wanted something to last.

Ben reached out.

His fingers brushed the edge of the cut.

The wrongness hit him like a low note played through the stone itself — a vibration that had no physical source, traveling up through his hand and into his arm before his mind had time to register the sensation. It wasn't pain. It wasn't cold. It was a fractional error in the way the Force felt at this location, the way distance warped slightly when you were looking through heat rising off a landing pad. His hand was touching the stone. The Force said his hand was touching the stone. But the relationship between his skin and the surface was six inches further away than it should have been, and the discrepancy sat in his bones like a wrongness his body understood before his mind could name it.

He pulled his hand back.

The vibration stopped. The stone was just stone again — cool, dry, unremarkable. The wind carried grit across the ridge. Desh's boots crunched on gravel somewhere behind him, a careful distance away, the way Desh always kept a careful distance when Ben was doing something that required the Force and didn't require an audience.

Ben stayed crouched for a moment longer, breathing through the aftershock. His heart was beating faster than the exertion justified. His hand felt normal. The wrongness was still there, pressing at the edge of his awareness, but it had settled back into the background pressure he'd been feeling since Coruscant. The spike was over.

He stood.

"Ben?"

"Markings," Ben said. His voice came out steady. "Tool-work I don't recognize. I'm going to scan them."

He pulled out his datapad and started cataloging. The shapes. The angles. The depth of the cuts and the erosion patterns that would give him an age estimate, give or take a few centuries. His hands moved with the professional precision of someone who'd done this a hundred times on a hundred different worlds, and the omission was already settling into place before he consciously decided to make it.

The report would include the markings. The estimated age. The geological context. The recommendation for follow-up survey.

It would not include what had happened when he'd touched the stone.

He didn't examine the decision. He didn't theorize about why he was making it, or what it meant, or what his father would say when he read a report that was technically accurate and structurally incomplete. He just worked, the way he'd been trained to work, and let the quiet alarm in his chest harden into something he could carry without naming.

"You're quiet," Desh said.

"Cataloging."

"That's the bad kind of quiet."

Ben finished the last scan and attached the data to a transmission header. Luke's contact code was already loaded — the survey protocol routed everything through the Grand Master's office before it hit the Temple archives. Standard procedure. Nothing unusual about a Knight filing a report directly.

He keyed the transmission.

The datapad chimed. Data sent. The markings, the estimates, the geological notes — all of it compressed into a burst that would reach Coruscant in a few hours, routed through the relay network that connected the Outer Rim to the Core. Luke would read it. Luke would see the shapes Ben had recorded, the tool-marks he couldn't identify, the age estimate that didn't match the official record.

Luke would not see the wrongness.

Ben pocketed the datapad and turned back toward the landing pad. The contrail had faded from the sky. The wind was picking up, carrying grit that stung his face and made the ruins look even older than they were, the stone blurring into the dust the way old things blurred into the landscape when no one was looking.

"Anything I need to know?" Desh asked.

Ben kept walking. "The report's filed. We'll see what my father says."

It wasn't a lie. It wasn't the truth either. It was the space between them, the gap where the Force impression should have been, and Ben carried it back to the ship without looking at the ruins again.
