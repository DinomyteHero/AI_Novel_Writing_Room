The third world was stone and silence, and the structure at its center was not a ruin.

Ben felt the difference before he saw it. The wrongness had been waiting for them the moment the *Steady Returns* broke atmosphere — heavier than it had been on the first two worlds, a dense pressure that settled low in his chest and stayed there, not the ambient unease he'd grown accustomed to but something specific and proximate, the way a door left open in a house you thought was empty registered before you could name what was wrong. He stood at the base of the facility's outer wall and let the weight of it press against his sternum while Desh ran his scanner over the stone.

"It has power," Desh said.

Ben didn't answer. He was reading the structure — the angles of its construction, the way the stone had been cut and fitted with a precision that had nothing to do with the ruins they'd catalogued on the first two worlds. This was military architecture. Hard lines, defensive sightlines, an entry corridor set at an angle that would force an approaching force to turn twice before reaching the interior. Someone had built this to hold.

"Power cells," Desh continued, tapping the scanner. "Still cycling. Not a lot of draw, but they're live. This thing has been running since before the Ruusan Reformations and it is still running."

"How long?"

"I don't know. My instruments say it shouldn't be. That's all I've got." Desh holstered the scanner and let his hand rest near his blaster. "You're making the face."

Ben started walking toward the entry corridor. "What face?"

"The one from the first two worlds. The one that says you felt something you're not putting in the report."

The entry corridor was dark and smelled of dust and cold durasteel, the recycled air of a sealed environment that had been cycling the same atmosphere for centuries. Ben's boots left prints in the dust on the floor — the first prints, he realized, in a very long time. The wrongness thickened as he moved deeper, pressing against his chest with every step until he had to make himself breathe around it, the way you breathed around a weight you couldn't shift.

Desh fell into step behind him, blaster drawn now, his chrono flickering on his wrist — Ben caught the motion in his peripheral vision, the way Desh glanced at it and frowned.

"Two seconds fast," Desh said. "Same as the last site."

Ben stopped at the first intersection. The corridor split left and right, both branches dark, both carrying the same wrongness in the same measure. He reached for the Force — not to push, just to read, the way he'd been trained to read a room before entering it. The wrongness pushed back.

Not hostile. Not dark-side, not exactly. Something else. Something that had been waiting.

A blue pulse of light crawled out of the wall beside him.

Ben's hand was on his blaster before he registered the movement, but the pulse wasn't a weapon. It was a scan — a sheet of pale blue light that moved across his body from head to foot, unhurried, methodical, the way a security system might check a visitor's credentials if the system had been designed for visitors whose credentials mattered more than their weapons. The light lingered at his chest, at his hands, at the place where his lightsaber hung beneath his jacket. It was reading him.

Desh had his blaster up. "Ben."

"Wait."

The pulse hesitated. Ben felt it — not through the Force, not exactly, but through something adjacent, the way you felt a door's lock cycling when you'd inserted the right key but the mechanism was old and had to think about it. The light crawled back across his body, slower this time, checking something it had already checked. As if it had found what it was looking for and wasn't sure it believed it.

Something older in him. Something the scan recognized.

The light went out. A deep hum started somewhere beneath the floor — power cells cycling up, drawing more current than they'd drawn in centuries. Displays flickered to life along the walls, pale amber text scrolling across screens that had been dark for a thousand years. The dust on the consoles jumped with the vibration of systems waking.

"It let you in," Desh said. His voice was flat, operational. The voice he used when the situation had stopped being routine and he didn't want to make a thing of it.

"It checked something first."

"Did it like what it found?"

Ben didn't answer. He was watching the displays, trying to parse the text scrolling across them — archaic Basic, the characters slightly wrong, the syntax older than any protocol he'd been trained on. The message was cycling. The same sequence, over and over, broadcast on frequencies that shouldn't have been able to carry anything. Dead frequencies. Frequencies no modern receiver would have been listening for.

Desh moved past him, sweeping the corridor with his blaster, reading the architecture the way Ben had read it outside — military command-and-control, a facility built to coordinate something larger than itself. His chrono was still flickering, two seconds fast, the wrongness pressing against his instruments the way it pressed against Ben's chest.

"This is a command post," Desh said. "Someone built this to run an operation. Not a temple. Not a monastery. A military installation."

Ben respected the reading. Desh had spent years in Galactic Alliance intelligence before he'd transitioned to private security; he knew what a command center looked like, and this was a command center. The displays were arranged for tactical oversight. The corridors were built for personnel who needed to move fast. The whole facility had the feel of a place where decisions had been made under pressure.

The message cycled again. The amber text flickered, stuttered, resolved — the archaic characters shifting into something Ben could read.

Two words.

SEAL FAILING.

Ben's breath caught. The weight on his chest became a pressure that pinned him to the moment, a tightness that had nothing to do with the recycled air or the dust or the dark. He read the words. He read them again. The message cycled and the words were still there, clear and unmistakable, a warning that had been broadcasting on dead frequencies for centuries while the Jedi Order went about its business and no one heard.

SEAL FAILING.

He didn't know what the seal was. He didn't know what it protected against. The meaning hadn't reached him yet — only the weight of the words, the pressure of standing in front of something the modern Order did not know existed and being told, in archaic Basic on a console that had been dark for a thousand years, that it was breaking.

"Ben." Desh's voice was still flat, but there was an edge under it now. "What does that mean?"

Ben shook his head. He couldn't answer. The wrongness was not ambient anymore. It was the sound of something failing, and he was standing in front of the proof, and the message kept cycling — SEAL FAILING, SEAL FAILING — while the power cells hummed beneath his feet and the displays flickered amber and the weight on his chest became a thing he could not breathe around.

The seal was real. The seal was failing. And Ben Skywalker was standing in a command post that had been waiting for someone to hear it.
