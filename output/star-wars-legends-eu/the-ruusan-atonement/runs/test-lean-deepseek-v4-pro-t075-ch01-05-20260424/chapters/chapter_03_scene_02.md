The data core was a small room behind a heavier door — blast-rated, Ben noted, the kind of door you installed when whatever was inside mattered more than whoever was trying to get in. Emergency lighting ran in pale strips along the ceiling, casting everything in amber and shadow. The air was cold enough that his breath caught visible, each exhalation a ghost of itself that hung for a moment before dissipating. The hum of ancient power cells came up through the floor, a vibration he felt in his teeth more than he heard.

Desh was already at the main console, running his hands over the casing. "This is older than anything I've cracked." He found the access panel's seam and worked his fingers into it. "The coupler's going to hate this."

"Can you get a read?"

"The storage media's intact. Whether it'll talk to modern equipment is a different question." Desh pried the panel free — corroded metal scraping against corroded metal, a sound that belonged in a salvage yard — and exposed the interior. The components were dense, layered, built in an era when miniaturization hadn't been the priority. "Hold the light."

Ben angled his glowrod into the cavity. The beam caught dust motes suspended in the cold air, illuminated circuitry that looked more like carved crystal than printed board. The Force here was different from the corridor — still heavy with the wrongness, still pressing against his chest like a recording that had been playing for a thousand years, but layered now with something else. A quality of waiting. As if the room had been holding its breath since the last person walked out and had never quite exhaled.

Desh pulled the coupler from his kit — a compact modern unit with a dozen adapter leads dangling from its housing — and began sorting through the connections. "This is going to be ugly. The interface protocols won't match. I'm going to have to bridge the power draw manually or the coupler will trip its safety cutoff."

"How long?"

"Depends on how many times it rejects me." Desh found a contact point, fitted an adapter, shook his head, and tried another. "The last time I did this was on a smugglers' relay station in the Corporate Sector. That equipment was only forty years out of date. This is a thousand."

Ben watched him work. Desh's hands moved with the particular economy of someone who'd done field improvisation under pressure — no wasted motion, no hesitation between attempts. He tried three contact points, rejected two, and on the fourth the coupler's indicator flickered amber.

"There," Desh said. "Maybe."

He touched the activator.

The coupler sparked. Blue-white, sharp, the smell of ozone cutting through the dust. The indicator died. Desh didn't curse — he just reset the connection and tried again, his expression flat in the way Ben recognized from operations that had gone sideways and needed to be un-sidewayed without drama.

The second spark held.

The coupler's indicator steadied to green. A low hum built in the console — ancient power cells recognizing a draw they hadn't felt in centuries. The displays along the walls flickered, stuttered, and then resolved into pale amber text scrolling across screens in archaic Basic.

"Got it," Desh said. He was already reading the data, his eyes moving across the displays in the quick, patterned way of someone trained to extract operational intelligence from raw feeds. "Navigation logs. System-status reports. This is a command-and-control architecture — the consoles are arranged for tactical oversight, the data hierarchy is military, not civilian. Someone ran an operation from this room."

Ben moved to the console beside him. The text was fragmentary — centuries of storage degradation had left gaps in the records, whole sectors of data corrupted beyond retrieval — but the structure was legible. Monitoring logs. Power-distribution reports. A network-status display that showed multiple nodes reporting to a central hub.

One of the monitoring logs carried an authorization line. The signature was archaic Basic, the characters formal in a way that suggested rank: *Gavran, Jedi Lord*.

Ben didn't know the name. He filed it and kept reading.

"Here," Desh said. He'd found the navigation data. "Let me push this to the holo."

The holographic projector woke slowly — ancient crystal matrices warming after a millennium of darkness — and then the star map flickered into the air above the console.

It didn't match any modern chart Ben had ever seen.

The projection showed a sector of the Outer Rim rendered in pale blue light, the stars positioned slightly wrong, the coordinate grid using a reference system that had been obsolete for centuries. But the wrongness wasn't in the astrography. It was in what someone had built across it.

Installations. Multiple facilities, arranged in a deliberate pattern, connected by a route that curved through the sector like a perimeter fence drawn around something at the center. Ben counted them — eight, ten, twelve nodes — each one positioned at a distance from the next that suggested careful calculation rather than convenience. The route didn't follow natural hyperspace lanes. It had been carved into the sector's geography with purpose.

At the center of the ring, the route terminated at a single point. A fortress marker, larger than the perimeter nodes, its coordinates locked behind a navigation cipher that the data core was still chewing on.

Ben felt the pressure shift.

It had been ambient before — a wrongness that pressed against his chest from all directions, the way deep water pressed without telling you which way was up. Now it had a shape. The perimeter installations formed a ring, and the ring had a center, and the pressure in his chest was pulling toward that center the way a wound pulled toward its source.

He didn't construct a theory. He let the geometry sit — twelve nodes, one fortress, a route that had been deliberately hidden in uncharted space — and felt the weight of it settle into his breathing.

"This isn't one facility," he said.

"No," Desh agreed. "It's a network."

"A containment network."

Desh looked at him. "You're sure."

Ben wasn't sure. The data didn't say what was contained — only that the perimeter existed, that the nodes had been reporting to the fortress for a thousand years, and that the message cycling on the corridor displays had been broadcasting on dead frequencies for as long as the power cells had held. *SEAL FAILING.* The seal was real. The seal was a network. And the network was arranged around something someone had considered worth hiding from the rest of the galaxy.

"The geometry," Ben said. "Twelve installations in a perimeter pattern. One central hub. The route doesn't follow trade lanes — it was built to enclose. This is military containment architecture."

Desh studied the holo. His expression was the one he wore when a situation had stopped being routine and he was deciding how much of his professional opinion to offer. "I've seen this layout before. Not this specific network — the design logic. GAG intel used to brief us on Imperial-era containment grids. The pattern's the same. Perimeter nodes for monitoring and early warning. Central command for response coordination. The difference is scale." He gestured at the projection. "This isn't a prison for one target. This is strategic infrastructure. Someone built this to hold something they couldn't kill."

The nav computer chimed. The cipher had resolved. The fortress coordinates locked into the display — a string of numbers and reference points that pointed to a region of space Ben had to cross-reference twice to confirm.

Uncharted. No hyperspace lane. No Republic survey. No record of any traffic, registered or otherwise, in the entire history of the modern astrographic database.

Something had been deliberately hidden there.

Ben reached for the data-transfer interface. His hand was steady — the steadiness of a decision he hadn't consciously made yet but his body had already executed. He copied the fortress coordinates. He copied the route data. He fed both into the *Steady Returns* nav computer through the coupler's link, watching the ship's system accept information that didn't belong on any modern chart.

Desh watched him do it. "That's a choice."

"I'm copying data. That's not a choice."

"It's the thing you do right before the choice." Desh's voice was still flat, operational, but there was something under it now — not quite a warning, not quite a question. "We're supposed to report this. Standard protocol. Anomalous installation, active power, unknown message content — this is exactly the kind of thing Luke's survey was designed to flag. We call it in, we wait for backup, we let the Council decide what to do about a thousand-year-old containment network that's been broadcasting a failure warning on dead frequencies."

"And if the backup takes two weeks?"

"Then it takes two weeks. The seal's been failing for a thousand years. It'll hold for two more."

Ben looked at the holo. The perimeter nodes glowed pale blue in the cold air, arranged around a center that was still dark — the fortress marker waiting for the nav computer to finish rendering its position. The wrongness pressed against his chest, directional now, pulling toward that center the way a recording pulled toward its source. The walls remembered. The data core remembered. The whole facility had been waiting for someone to hear it, and Ben was the one who had walked in.

"You don't believe that," he said.

Desh was quiet for a moment. "No. I don't."

The nav computer chimed again. The fortress coordinates resolved. The display painted a destination deep in uncharted space, and Ben Skywalker stood in the cold amber light of a room that had been waiting a thousand years and felt the weight of the choice settle into his chest beside the wrongness.

He carried the coordinates now.

He carried the choice.
