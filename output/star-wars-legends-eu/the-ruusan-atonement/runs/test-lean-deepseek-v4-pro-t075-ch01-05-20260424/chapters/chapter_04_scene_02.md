The corridor from the entry chamber opened into a space that had been built to run a war.

Level 1 was a command post. The ceiling rose two stories, lost in shadow above the glow rod's reach, and the floor stretched away in a grid of consoles and monitoring stations arranged with the particular precision of people who had expected to use them for years. Dust coated every surface — a grey-white layer that had settled in the centuries since the air last moved and held the stillness like a held breath. Ben's boots left prints in it as he stepped forward, the sound swallowed by the room's size.

Desh swept his glow rod across the nearest bank of consoles. "Communications gear is dead."

Ben didn't need the glow rod to confirm it. The equipment nearest the door — transmitters, relay stations, the heavy durasteel housings of military-grade comms — sat cold and dark, their indicator panels blank. Whatever had killed the long-range systems had done it centuries ago, and the dust had been settling ever since.

But the monitoring stations further in were different.

Ben felt it before he saw it — a low thrum in the Force, not active but *available*, the way a powered-down system held charge in its capacitors. The consoles were dark but not dead. Residual power moved through them, a slow circulation that had been cycling for a thousand years while the dust accumulated and the air stilled and no one came.

He crossed to the nearest station and laid his palm flat against the console's surface.

The metal was cool. The dust was undisturbed. The pulse beneath it was faint but steady — a heartbeat at the threshold of perception, waiting.

"What are you doing?" Desh asked.

Ben didn't answer. He reached into the Force and pushed.

Not hard. Not the sharp, directed technique he'd used on the door. This was gentler — a nudge rather than a demand, the Force equivalent of tapping a hibernating system and waiting to see if it remembered how to wake. The pulse beneath his palm stuttered. Hesitated. Then caught, and spread, and the console's surface flickered from dark to amber as the monitoring station drew its first real power in a thousand years.

The hum rose through the floor.

It wasn't loud — a subsonic vibration that Ben felt in his boots and his teeth and the base of his skull, the sound of systems that had been designed to persist waking up one by one. Indicator lights blinked amber across the room. Dust shivered on console surfaces. The air shifted, stirring for the first time since the Ruusan Reformations, and the wrongness that had been pressing against Ben's sternum since the plateau shifted with it — not easing, but *focusing*, as if the fortress had been waiting for someone to ask the right question.

Desh's glow rod swept the room again, slower this time. "That should not have worked."

"Mm."

"Ben. That should not have worked. The power plants in this place are a thousand years old."

"They built for persistence." Ben's hand stayed on the console. The pulse beneath his palm was steady now, a slow rhythm that matched the hum in the walls. "They knew no one was coming to fix it."

The amber lights steadied. The monitoring stations resolved into function — not full function, not the bright active displays of a live command post, but a reduced state that was deliberate rather than damaged. The fortress had been designed to sleep. It had been designed to wake. And it had been designed to track something while it did.

The display at the center of the room flickered alive.

It was a network map — a holographic projection that rose from the central console in pale blue light, its edges flickering with the instability of ancient emitters. Nodes scattered across a star chart that Ben recognized as the Outer Rim, systems and sectors marked in a notation he didn't know. Most of the nodes were red.

Dead.

A handful burned amber — degraded, their connections to the central hub thinned by time and distance and whatever had happened to the network in the centuries since its builders sealed themselves away. The amber nodes clustered in a loose chain that stretched from the fortress's position toward the galactic southeast, following a path that Ben's eyes traced without understanding.

And one node was flashing.

Not red. Not amber. White — urgent, strobing, the particular frequency that military systems used for a condition that had no precedent. The label beneath it was in the same unknown notation, but the word that accompanied it was clear enough.

BREACH.

Ben's breath caught.

The Force around the display was wrong in a way he hadn't felt before. Not deadened — the upper levels had been deadened, the pressure of something sealed away and pressing outward. This was different. This was *strained*, the way a cable strains when the load exceeds its rating, the way a hull strains when the pressure differential is too great to hold. The system was screaming. It had been screaming for a long time, and no one had heard it.

His hand moved toward the display before he'd decided to move it. The node flashed. The amber chain flickered. The red nodes — dozens of them, scattered across the Outer Rim like dead stars — sat dark and silent, and Ben understood without understanding that each one represented something that had failed.

Something that had been maintained.

Something that had been allowed to die.

"What am I looking at?" Desh's voice was flat, operational. He'd moved to the central console while Ben was reading the display, his datapad out, the glow rod clamped between his teeth while he worked. The camera on the datapad clicked as he photographed each console in sequence — the indicator panels, the power distribution readouts, the unknown notation on the network labels.

"I don't know."

"You don't know, or you don't want to say?"

Ben's jaw tightened. The node kept flashing. The wrongness kept straining. He didn't have the vocabulary for what he was seeing — didn't have the context, the history, the institutional knowledge that would let him read the network as anything other than a disaster in progress. The fortress had been built to run a war. The war had ended. And the network had kept running, kept monitoring, kept screaming into a silence that had lasted a thousand years.

"I don't know," he said again. "But it's losing."

Desh lowered the datapad. His jaw was tight — the particular tension Ben had learned to read in the months since he'd hired him, the sign that Desh had stopped treating a situation as routine and started calculating how to get out of it.

"There's a stairwell at the back of the room. Descending. I've got three exits mapped between here and the entry, plus the route we came in." He didn't ask what the network was for. He didn't ask what BREACH meant. He was already doing the math on how fast they could clear the corridor if whatever was failing decided to fail while they were inside it.

Ben registered the shift in his posture — the way Desh had angled himself toward the exit, the way his weight had settled onto his back foot. It landed as a warning. Not a warning Ben could name, not yet, but a pressure in the room that hadn't been there before the display lit up. Desh's fear was a practical thing — it lived in exit routes and escape vectors and the particular way he held his shoulders when he'd decided a situation was wrong. And it was contagious.

Ben's confidence in *we can handle this* wavered.

He didn't slow down. But he noticed that Desh wanted him to.

"The stairwell," he said.

"Ben."

"I saw it."

"That's not what I'm asking."

Ben turned from the display. The node kept flashing behind him — he could feel it in the Force, the strobing urgency of a system that had been waiting for someone to notice it. The red nodes sat dead. The amber chain flickered. The fortress hummed around them, the residual power cycling through systems that had been designed to persist, and the wrongness pressed against his sternum with a weight that was becoming harder to ignore.

"I know," he said. "I know what you're asking."

"And?"

"I don't have an answer yet."

Desh held his gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded — not agreement, but acknowledgment, the particular nod of a man who had decided to follow someone into a bad situation and was making his peace with it.

"The stairwell," he said. "After you."

---

The stairwell descended from the rear of the command post in a tight spiral, the steps cut from the same weathered alloy as the walls. Emergency lighting ran along the base of each riser — not the amber glow of the monitoring stations but a pale blue that intensified the lower they went, casting the stairs in an otherworldly glow that made Desh's face look hollow.

The air changed as they descended.

It had been cool and still on Level 1 — the particular deadness of atmosphere that had been sealed away for centuries and never circulated. Now it was warming, stirring, picking up a charge that Ben felt on his skin and in his lungs and in the Force. The hum in the walls was louder here. The wrongness was sharper, more focused, less like ambient pressure and more like a held breath that was about to be released.

Level 2 opened off the stairwell at the halfway point — an archive, its stacks visible through an open doorway as they passed. Ben caught a glimpse of holocrons arranged in meticulous rows on the nearer shelves, data tablets stacked with the precision of a mind that had organized everything it could reach. Further back, the stacks grew frantic — tablets piled on top of each other, vacuum-sealed documents shoved into gaps, the careful order of the front shelves collapsing into the desperate accumulation of someone who had run out of time.

Gavran's voice, written in what he'd chosen to keep and how he'd chosen to keep it. Meticulous where time allowed. Frantic where it did not.

Ben didn't stop. The archive could wait. The stairwell kept descending, and the blue light kept deepening, and the air kept warming, and the Force down there was different in a way that pulled his attention past the doorway and down, past the stacks and the holocrons and the thousand years of records, toward something that was not wrong.

Something that was alive.

He stopped at the top of the final flight.

The stairwell opened below him into a space he couldn't see — the blue emergency lighting threw the walls into sharp relief but the floor was lost in shadow, and the air that rose from it carried a warmth that had nothing to do with environmental systems. The wrongness was still there, still pressing, still straining against whatever containment the fortress had been built to maintain. But beneath it, moving through it, pulsing with a rhythm that Ben felt in his chest before his mind caught up to what he was perceiving—

Three distinct signatures.

Living. Separate. Each one shaped differently in the Force — one disciplined, its rhythm steady and controlled in a way that spoke of training held through long isolation. One heavy, its pulse slower and deeper, carrying a weight that Ben's senses registered without being able to name. One clipped, abbreviated, its cadence cut short in a pattern that suggested restraint rather than damage.

People.

Living people, sealed below for a thousand years, their presence in the Force as clear and immediate as a heartbeat.

Ben's hand found the railing. The metal was warm.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet behind him. "What is it?"

The three rhythms pulsed in the blue-tinged dark. The disciplined one held steady. The heavy one pressed outward. The clipped one flickered, quick and sharp, like someone who had been waiting for a long time and had learned not to expect an answer.

Something was waiting. Something had been waiting since before the Ruusan Reformations, sealed in stasis while the network screamed and the nodes died and the one flashing BREACH counted down to a failure no one had been alive to see.

Until now.

"People," Ben said. His voice sounded strange to his own ears — not afraid, not yet, but shocked in a way that hadn't finished registering. "There are people down there."
