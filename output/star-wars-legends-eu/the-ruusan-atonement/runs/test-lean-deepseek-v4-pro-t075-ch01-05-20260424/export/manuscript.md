# Chapter 1

## Scene 01.01

The training saber hummed in Ben’s grip, a familiar weight he’d stopped thinking about years ago. Rendell circled to his left, blade held in a conservative middle guard, footwork clean and predictable. Ben read the feint before Rendell’s shoulder twitched—a high-line probe that would drop into a low sweep, the same sequence Rendell had been favoring all session.

Ben’s guard should have shifted to meet it. His body knew the answer. The rhythm lived in his spine, in the reflex that had carried him through GAG operations and Abeloth and a dozen missions since.

The Force arrived a half-beat late.

Rendell’s blade caught him across the right shoulder—a sharp crack of plasma-on-contact that sent a jolt down his arm and left the smell of scorched tunic in his nose. The training saber’s power cell cycled down on impact, safety protocol, but the sting lingered.

Ben stepped back, blade lowering.

“Point.” Rendell deactivated his saber and clipped it to his belt in one motion. “You all right? That was—”

“Sloppy.” Ben killed his own blade. The salle’s ambient hum dropped to silence, and in the quiet the wrongness pressed against his attention again—not a sound, not a color, just the field arriving behind the beat, like a note played slightly flat. “Yeah. I know.”

The third time this week. The first time it had happened with a witness.

Rendell was watching him, head tilted a few degrees. A Knight Ben had sparred with often enough to know his habits—professional, solid, the kind of Jedi who showed up on time and didn’t ask questions he didn’t need the answers to. His expression was neutral, but the pause before he spoke said he’d noticed the lag.

“Want to run it again?”

Ben’s shoulder throbbed. The wrongness pulled at his attention like a half-heard echo, and his body was still waiting for the Force to catch up to where it should have been three seconds ago. What he wanted was to go again, push harder, prove the fluke out of existence with speed and precision until his body remembered what it was supposed to do.

What he said was: “Think I’m done for the night.” He forced his shoulders into something that looked relaxed. “Week’s been catching up with me. Shouldn’t have skipped dinner.”

It wasn’t quite a lie. He had skipped dinner.

Rendell held the look for a beat longer than comfortable, then nodded. “Get some rest.”

Ben waited until the salle door hissed shut behind him before he let his posture drop. The training saber went onto the rack. His hand lingered on the grip a moment too long—the impulse to ignite it again, to run the form until the wrongness bent to his will or broke him trying.

He let go.

The salle stretched empty around him, a long rectangle of polished floor and padded walls, lit by the dimmed glow panels that kicked in after the evening session block. Someone’s boots had left scuff marks near the far wall. A cleaning droid hummed past the door, its motor a low drone that faded toward the turbolift. The Temple at this hour was quiet but not dead—late-shift foot traffic in the corridor beyond, the occasional chime of a turbolift arriving, the distant murmur of voices that belonged to people who weren’t standing alone in a training salle trying to convince themselves they weren’t the problem.

Ben walked to the center of the floor.

He didn’t ignite the blade.

Instead he raised his empty hand and found the first position of the form—the one his father had drilled into him at fourteen, the one that had become as automatic as breathing. Feet set. Weight balanced. Arm extended along the line his blade would follow.

He moved.

The sequence unfolded in muscle memory: step, pivot, cut. Step, pivot, guard. His body knew the shape of it, and in the first three movements the rhythm held. His breathing steadied. The salle’s recycled air cooled the sweat on his neck.

On the fourth movement, the Force lagged.

Not in him. Not a failure of his concentration or a crack in his focus. The ambient field—the living presence that should have met his motion, the current every Jedi learned to move inside—arrived a half-second behind the rhythm his body had known by reflex for years. His empty hand cut through air that felt a beat out of step with where it should have been.

He stopped.

His chest had tightened. He hadn’t noticed it happening.

The wrongness was not fatigue. It was not the residue of a long week or the ghost of a skipped meal. It was in the field itself—a structural lag, a note played slightly flat, something arriving late to a rhythm that had held for as long as Ben had been able to feel the Force at all.

He didn’t have words for it. He didn’t try to find them.

What he had was the specific, physical certainty of a musician who’d heard the wrong note and couldn’t unhear it. The wrongness was external. It was not him.

The relief that came with that thought was sharp and brief, because the thought that followed it was worse: if it wasn’t him, what was it? And why was he the only one who felt it?

He lowered his arm. The salle’s silence settled back around him, broken only by the distant chime of a turbolift and the soft hum of the cleaning droid making its second pass. Coruscant glittered beyond the high windows, a sprawl of light and traffic that didn’t care about a half-beat lag in a training salle.

Ben stood in the center of the floor and let the wrongness press against his senses without fighting it. Not naming it. Not diagnosing it. Just feeling the shape of the thing—the lag, the flat note, the field arriving late.

The door hissed open behind him.

Ben turned. His hand dropped to his side, the form abandoned.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway. He had a datapad in one hand and the expression Ben had learned to read at sixteen—the one that meant a mission was coming, and that whatever Ben had been planning to do with the next week was about to become irrelevant. His father’s face was calm, the Grand Master’s composure settled over it like a familiar coat, but his eyes flicked to the unlit training saber on the rack and then back to Ben.

He didn’t ask about the sparring session. He didn’t have to.

Ben felt the reluctant hope before he could stop it—the relief that he might not be alone with this, tangled up with the cost of admitting it aloud. Whatever Luke was about to hand him, it meant the private problem was about to become a shared one.

He braced for the handoff and waited.

## Scene 01.02

Luke’s office smelled of caf and old flimsiplast, the same as it had since Ben was old enough to be summoned here for briefings instead of lectures. The desk was cluttered in the way that meant his father had been working through the night again — three empty mugs pushed to one side, a stack of datacards that needed sorting, the holocomm’s indicator light blinking a message someone had left at an hour no one should have been sending messages.

Ben stood just inside the door, hands at his sides. The wrongness pushed at the edge of his awareness even here, a faint sour note under the Temple’s usual harmonics. It had been stronger since the salle. Or he had been less able to ignore it.

Luke didn’t look up from the datapad in his hand. “Sit.”

Ben sat. The chair was the same one that had been across from Luke’s desk for as long as he could remember — worn at the armrests, the cushion shaped to the backs of a thousand Jedi who’d sat in it before him. He didn’t lean back.

Luke set the datapad on the desk and slid it across. The screen glowed blue, a string of survey reports already queued. Seventeen of them, Ben saw, each flagged with the same anomaly marker — a small amber triangle that meant the reporting officer had found something they couldn’t classify.

“Outer Rim,” Luke said. “All along one bearing. You can see the cluster.”

Ben pulled the datapad toward him. His body had already tightened, the shift from waiting to attention happening before his mind caught up. The reports were standard template — sensor logs, local Force-readings, the kind of routine sweep a Knight ran when passing through a sector on other business. Nothing that should have merited a Grand Master’s attention.

Except the amber flags ran in a line. Seventeen points, each one a few hundred parsecs from the last, stretching from the Mid Rim out toward the edge of known space.

“When did these come in?”

“The first one about six weeks ago.” Luke’s voice was even. Grand Master register — the tone he used in Council sessions, measured and calm. “The last one yesterday. I’ve been sitting on them.”

Ben looked up. His father’s face gave away nothing, but the fact that he’d been sitting on them meant he’d been waiting for something. A pattern. A confirmation. A reason to bring them to Ben instead of to the Council.

Ben scrolled. The sensor data was thin — most of the reporting officers had noted the anomaly as a curiosity, not a threat. Ambient Force-readings slightly elevated. Local practitioners reporting unease without a clear source. One Knight had written *feels like a note played off-key* and then crossed it out and replaced it with *unexplained variance in the local field*.

Ben’s thumb stopped on the third report. Then the ninth. Then the fourteenth.

The readings matched. Not the same anomaly — the same *shape* of anomaly, the same sourness under the data, the same wrongness he’d been carrying in his spine since the first time the Force lagged behind his motion. Someone else had felt it. Several someone elses, scattered across a dozen sectors, each one writing it up as a curiosity and moving on.

He zoomed in on the three that matched most closely. The coordinates pulled into focus, and Ben felt his stomach drop before he understood why. The bearing ran along a line he could trace in his sleep — the route he’d flown a dozen times as a teenager, the corridor his father had first shown him on a starchart when he was fourteen and learning to navigate by feel.

“That’s the old hyperspace lane,” he said. “The one that runs past—”

“I know which one it is.” Luke’s voice was still even, but something in it had shifted. Not the words. The pause before them.

Ben set the datapad down. The sourness pressed against his senses, and for a moment he let himself feel it without pushing back — the lag, the flat note, the field arriving a half-beat late. It was stronger now than it had been in the salle. Or he was closer to the bearing.

“You’ve felt it too,” Ben said. Not a question.

Luke didn’t answer immediately. He looked at the datapad, then at the window behind Ben’s shoulder, where the Coruscant traffic washed past in a continuous low drone. His Force presence was warm and steady in the room, the same as it had always been — vast, contained, the weight of it familiar enough that Ben didn’t notice it unless he reached for it.

When Luke spoke again, the Grand Master register was gone.

“I’ve felt something. For a few weeks now. I couldn’t localize it.” He nodded at the datapad. “The reports helped.”

Ben heard the difference. His father was describing an anomaly — a disturbance in the field, a variance worth investigating, the kind of thing a Jedi Master might note in a report and file for later review. Luke had felt something. Ben had been living inside it since the wrongness first surfaced, carrying it in his body like a second heartbeat, unable to name it and unable to put it down.

The gap between them opened in the space of a breath. Luke saw the anomaly. Ben saw the wound.

He didn’t say that. He didn’t have words for it yet, and even if he had, the words would have been wrong for this room, for the datapad on the desk and the caf mugs and the blinking message light. His father had given him seventeen reports and a bearing. That was the mission. The rest was Ben’s to carry.

“You want me to run the survey.” Ben’s voice came out steady. Knight-to-Grand-Master, the register he’d learned to use in this office when he was sixteen and trying to prove he belonged here.

“I want someone who can read what the reports are pointing at.” Luke’s gaze was steady. “You felt it before I showed you the data. That’s not a coincidence.”

No. It wasn’t. Ben had felt it in the salle, in his quarters, in the corridor outside the refectory when he’d stopped mid-step because the wrongness had spiked and then faded before he could track it. He’d been feeling it for weeks, alone with it, and now his father was handing him a mission that made the isolation official.

The relief hit first — sharp and brief, the pressure of being truly seen. Then the terror, because being seen meant being sent. Alone. On a bearing that matched the wrongness exactly.

Ben stood. The chair scraped against the floor, a sound too loud for the quiet office. He moved to the window, needing the space, the physical separation, the excuse to look at something other than his father’s face.

Outside, Coruscant stretched to the horizon in a sprawl of light and traffic. Speeders cut between the towers in their designated lanes, thousands of them, millions, the city going about its business without any sense that the Force had developed a sour note. Somewhere below, a landing platform was cycling its guidance lights. A cleaning droid hummed past the office door, the same model that had been in the corridor outside the salle.

Ben’s shoulders settled. He felt them do it — the deliberate drop, the posture of someone accepting a solo assignment. His reflection in the transparisteel looked calm. Competent. Ready.

“When do I leave?”

Luke didn’t answer right away. Ben heard him rise from the desk, heard the soft sound of the datapad being picked up, and then the silence stretched long enough that Ben almost turned around.

“There’s a ship in bay twelve,” Luke said. “Survey-class. Prepped this morning.”

This morning. Before the sparring session. Before Ben had stood in an empty salle and proven to himself that the wrongness was external. His father had known he was going to ask before Ben knew he was going to say yes.

“You’ll need a pilot,” Luke added. The words were careful. An opening, not a suggestion.

Ben didn’t take it. He could have named someone — a Knight he trusted, a pilot who knew the Outer Rim, anyone who would make the mission less solitary than it already was. The roster was open. His father was waiting.

He said nothing. The silence was a choice, and they both knew it.

Luke didn’t push. He set the datapad on the edge of the desk, closer to Ben’s side than his own, and let the gesture speak for him. The mission was Ben’s. The pilot was Ben’s to choose. The distance between them was still there, intact, the careful love-laden space that had defined their relationship since Ben was old enough to understand that his father trusted him enough to let him go alone.

“I’ll handle the pilot,” Ben said.

He didn’t turn around. The window showed him his father’s reflection — Luke standing beside the desk, one hand resting on the stack of datacards, his expression unreadable. The wrongness pulled at Ben’s attention like a direction now, not just a note. The bearing was there, waiting, and the survey route ran straight along it.

Neither of them acknowledged what the choice meant.

## Scene 01.03

The cockpit smelled of recycled air and the faint ozone tang of a ship that had been sitting on standby since morning. Ben's thumb moved across the datapad screen before his mind caught up — past the Jedi partner roster, two taps to the personal contacts list, scrolling down to the name he'd already decided on before he'd left his father's office.

The preflight indicators glowed green across the console. Fuel at capacity. Nav computer holding the survey route Luke had uploaded. Hyperspace motivator warm and ready. Everything in order except the crew manifest, which still read *one*.

Ben's thumb stopped on Desh's frequency. The contact entry was three years old, last used six months ago for a drink on Coruscant that had turned into two drinks and a long silence that neither of them had needed to fill.

He opened the channel.

The hangar bay stretched out beyond the cockpit canopy — a cavern of durasteel and guidance lights, service techs moving between the other ships in their designated lanes. A fueling droid hummed past his starboard viewport. Somewhere behind him, the Temple's upper levels were cycling through the evening shift change, the same as they had every night since he'd been old enough to notice schedules.

The comm crackled. Desh's voice came through tinny and warm under the static, the way long-range always sounded from the surface.

"Ben."

"Desh. I need you for a survey run."

The pause was short — shorter than it should have been, given the hour and the lack of preamble. Ben heard the shift in the background noise, the sound of a chair adjusting, a datapad being set down.

"How bad?"

"I don't know yet. That's the problem."

Another pause. Ben watched the fueling droid detach from a shuttle two bays over and trundle toward its charging station. The wrongness pressed at the edge of his awareness, a sour note that had followed him from the salle to the office to the cockpit and showed no sign of fading.

"Outer Rim," Ben said. "Survey-class. Could be a week, could be longer. I need someone who doesn't need to know why I'm not bringing a Jedi."

The words landed. Ben felt them land — the way Desh's silence shifted from consideration to recognition, the weight of years neither of them discussed settling into the space between the words. The Galactic Alliance Guard. The dissolution. The thing they'd both survived at an age when they should have been learning to fly without watching for threats.

Desh's voice came back dry. "You're asking me to leave a paying contract for a survey run with no end date and no explanation."

"Yes."

"And you called me instead of opening the roster."

Ben's thumb was still resting on the datapad screen, the Jedi partner contacts three names he hadn't looked at. "Yes."

"All right." Desh's tone didn't change — still dry, still practical, the same register he'd used when they were seventeen and running operations that didn't go into any official record. "Where do I meet you?"

The relief hit before Ben could brace for it. Sharp and brief, the tension of justification evaporating. His shoulders dropped slightly — a physical release he felt in the cockpit seat, the harness suddenly looser than it had been a moment ago.

"Hangar bay twelve. Temple landing platform. I'm lifting in twenty."

"I'll be there in fifteen."

The comm clicked off. Ben sat in the silence, his hand still on the datapad, the Jedi roster still open on the screen behind his personal contacts. He closed it without looking at the names.

---

The ship lifted cleanly from the Temple hangar bay, repulsors humming as Ben angled toward the orbital approach vector. Below, the Temple receded into the Coruscant cityscape — a spire among spires, its guidance lights blinking in the evening haze. The traffic control channel chattered in Ben's earpiece, automated instructions routing him through the departure lanes.

Desh was in the copilot seat, strapping in with the practiced efficiency of someone who'd done it a thousand times. He hadn't asked any questions in the hangar. He'd walked up the ramp with a duffel bag and a raised eyebrow at the ship's survey-class designation, then settled into the cockpit like he'd been flying with Ben last week instead of six months ago.

"Survey route," Desh said, scanning the nav computer readout. "That's a lot of stops for a routine sweep."

"It's a long bearing."

"Mm." Desh tapped the display. "And the part where you called me instead of a Knight?"

Ben's hands were steady on the controls. The ship cleared the approach vector and the nav computer chimed, ready for the first jump calculation. "The part where I called you is the part you already know."

Desh didn't answer. He didn't need to. The silence between them was the same silence from three years ago, from six months ago, from every conversation they'd had since the GAG was dissolved and they'd both walked away from the thing they'd been made into. It wasn't uncomfortable. It was familiar — the quiet of two people who'd learned early that some things didn't need to be said because saying them didn't change what they were.

Ben punched the first set of coordinates. The nav computer ran the calculation and the hyperdrive began its spin-up cycle, a low hum building through the deck plates.

The wrongness was still there.

It should have been fading. They were climbing out of Coruscant's gravity well, putting distance between the ship and the Temple, the surface, the place where the disturbance had first registered as a sour note in the salle. Distance should have thinned it. That was how disturbances worked — localized, tied to a place or a person or an event, diminishing as you left them behind.

This one wasn't diminishing. It was sharpening.

Ben's jaw tightened. He felt the muscles along his spine contract, the body registering what the mind hadn't quite caught up to — the wrongness wasn't just persisting. It was acquiring weight. Direction. Like a compass needle that had been spinning aimlessly and was now settling toward a bearing he could trace in his sleep.

The hyperdrive reached cycle. Ben pushed the lever forward.

The stars stretched into lines and the ship punched into hyperspace, the familiar lurch of the transition pressing him back against the seat. The instruments flickered — a half-second of static across the nav display, the sensor board spiking and then settling. Normal for the jump. Nothing that should have registered as anything other than routine.

Except the wrongness followed.

Ben's hands tightened on the controls. The sour note was still there, sharper now than it had been in the hangar, sharper than it had been in his father's office. It should have vanished into the background hum of hyperspace travel the way all Force-sense did during a jump — muffled, distant, the galaxy's living presence reduced to a whisper while the ship moved outside normal space.

It didn't. It pulled instead — a pressure against his senses that had nothing to do with the ship's acceleration and everything to do with the bearing they were flying. The survey route. The line of amber flags stretching from the Mid Rim to the edge of known space. The direction his father had handed him along with seventeen reports and a silence that meant *I couldn't localize it but you can*.

Ben's breath caught. He felt it catch, a hitch in his chest that had no business being there during a routine jump.

The Force did not work this way. Disturbances dissipated. They did not follow. They did not pull. They were symptoms of something wrong in a place, not guidance toward a destination.

But this one was pulling. Not ambient anymore. Not sour background noise. A compass needle settling on a bearing that matched the route he'd already committed to, the survey his father had prepped this morning, the mission Ben had accepted without hesitation because accepting it meant not having to explain why he'd been feeling it for weeks.

Desh glanced over from the copilot seat. "You all right?"

Ben didn't answer immediately. He was watching the nav display, the hyperspace tunnel streaming past the viewport, the wrongness sitting in his chest like a second heartbeat. The pressure had stopped being a problem. It had become a destination.

"Ben."

"Yeah." His voice came out steady. Knight's register, the one he'd learned to use when he was sixteen and trying to prove he belonged in the cockpit. "I'm fine."

Desh didn't push. He turned back to the nav readout, and the silence between them stretched out into the jump, comfortable and familiar and built on years of not asking the questions they both already knew the answers to.

Ben let his hands rest on the controls. The wrongness pulled toward the Outer Rim, toward the bearing, toward something waiting at the end of a line of amber flags that his father had been collecting for six weeks. He didn't know what it was. He didn't know why it followed into hyperspace when nothing should.

He knew he was going to find out.

# Chapter 2

## Scene 02.01

The *Steady Returns* shuddered out of hyperspace like a ship that had forgotten how.

The reversion hit harder than any jump Ben had ridden in years — a bone-deep rattle that traveled up through the deck plates and into his teeth before the instruments had time to catch up. The starfield snapped back into focus through the viewport, normal space reasserting itself with a violence that sent his caf cup skidding across the copilot console.

Hot liquid sloshed over the rim. Ben's hand shot out — too late to catch the cup, fast enough to keep it from hitting the deck. The caf spread across the sensor readouts in a brown wash, pooling around the nav display's bezel before dripping down toward the auxiliary panel.

"Kriff," Ben said, and meant it.

Desh was already working the controls, his hands moving across the primary console with the unhurried efficiency of someone who'd been flying junk freighters since before he could legally drink. The ship's shuddering eased as he brought the inertial compensators back into alignment, but the cockpit still hummed at a frequency that felt wrong — a vibration in the deck plates that didn't match any engine harmonic Ben knew.

"That wasn't the motivator," Desh said. His voice was calm. It was always calm. Even during the GAG years, when things had gone sideways in ways that didn't make it into any official record, Desh had sounded exactly like this — like the situation was a problem to be solved rather than a crisis to survive.

Ben grabbed a rag from the storage pocket beside his seat and started mopping caf off the instruments. The liquid was still warm, soaking through the fabric and into his sleeve. "Nav computer hiccup?"

"Nav computer doesn't hiccup hard enough to rattle the caf out of your hand." Desh tapped the sensor panel, his finger leaving a smudge through the caf residue Ben hadn't reached yet. "Chrono's off by four-point-two seconds. Gravitic sensors are reading a mass shadow that isn't there. And the reversion point was eighteen thousand klicks short of where the computer said it would be."

Ben wiped down the nav display, his sleeve darkening with caf. The ritual was familiar — clean the mess, check the systems, move on. The kind of small repair that gave his hands something to do while his mind caught up to what his body already knew.

The wrongness was sharper now.

It had been a background pressure since Coruscant — a sour note at the edge of his awareness, the way a door left slightly ajar registered as wrong before you consciously noticed the gap. During the jump it had pulled at him, directional, insistent in a way that disturbances weren't supposed to be. Now, with the ship shuddering back into realspace and the instruments showing readings that didn't match the charts, it sat in his chest like a second heartbeat.

He didn't reach for the Force to examine it. The Force was the problem.

"Diagnostic's running," Desh said. He leaned back in the pilot's seat, one hand still resting on the control yoke, and watched the sensor readouts cycle through their self-check. "You want to tell me why we're really out here?"

Ben finished wiping the last of the caf off the auxiliary panel and dropped the rag into the storage pocket. "Survey run. My father—"

"Your father sent you on a survey run and you called me instead of a Jedi." Desh's tone didn't change. Still dry, still patient, still the same register he'd used when they were seventeen and running operations that didn't go into any official record. "That's the part I already know. I'm asking about the other part."

The cockpit hummed around them. Recycled air, the faint ozone of old circuitry, the particular smell of a ship that had carried people for a long time and would carry them for longer still. Ben could feel the wrongness pressing at the edge of his senses — not just in the ship's systems now, but in the space beyond the viewport, in the starfield that looked normal and felt like a lie.

"I don't know what it is yet," Ben said. "That's the problem."

Desh didn't answer. He turned back to the sensor panel, where the diagnostic was finishing its cycle, and studied the readouts with the quiet focus of someone who'd spent years learning to read instruments because instruments didn't lie the way people did.

Ben watched him work. Desh was a warm absence in the Force — the way all non-sensitives were, a space where the living presence of the galaxy didn't quite reach. Being around him was like standing in a room where one wall was missing. Not threatening. Just different. A reminder that most people moved through the universe without ever feeling the currents Ben had been swimming in since before he could walk.

It was comforting, in a way. Desh didn't register in the Force, which meant the wrongness couldn't touch him the way it touched Ben. He was outside the problem. Safe.

Except his instruments weren't.

"Drift pattern's back," Desh said. He tapped the sensor panel with one finger — a light, patient tap, the kind of gesture that said *I've seen this before and I'm going to be annoyed if I'm right*. "Same signature as before the jump. Chrono's still off. Gravitic ghost is still there. And it's directional."

Ben's hands stopped moving.

"Not random noise," Desh continued. He was reading the display, his voice flat and economical, the way he'd read mission briefings back when mission briefings had meant something else entirely. "It's bearing-consistent. Whatever's causing it, it's not ambient interference. It's pointing somewhere."

The cockpit felt smaller than it had a moment ago. Ben's chest tightened — not the wrongness itself, but the recognition of it, the way his body understood what his mind was still trying not to name. The bearing Desh was reading on his instruments. The pull Ben had been feeling since Coruscant. The survey route his father had handed him along with seventeen reports and a silence that meant *I couldn't localize it but you can*.

They were the same.

"Show me," Ben said.

Desh rotated the sensor display toward the copilot station. The drift pattern was clear — a consistent deviation from the expected readings, the chrono and gravitic anomalies clustering along a single vector. It pointed coreward-spinward, toward the first survey world on their route, and beyond it into the Outer Rim darkness where the amber flags on Luke's chart grew sparse and the reports grew thin.

Ben looked at the display. His hands were still, resting on the armrests, the caf rag crumpled in the storage pocket and the instruments clean and the wrongness pulling at him like a compass needle settling toward a bearing he could trace in his sleep.

"It's been there since Coruscant," he said. The words came out before he'd decided to say them — not an explanation, not a confession, just a fact laid on the console between them. "Whatever this is. I've been feeling it since before I left."

Desh didn't look surprised. He looked at the display, then at Ben, then back at the display, the way he'd look at a tactical readout that was telling him something he'd already suspected. "And you didn't mention it."

"I didn't know what it was."

"Do you now?"

Ben's jaw tightened. The wrongness pressed against his senses — not painful, not threatening, just *present* in a way that it hadn't been an hour ago. Stronger. Closer. The bearing was a vector now, not a suggestion, and the first survey world was waiting at the end of it.

"No," he said. "But I know where it's pointing."

Desh was quiet for a moment. Then he reached over and tapped the nav display, where the first survey world had appeared on the edge of the scanner range — a small, unremarkable sphere of rock and atmosphere that had been flagged for investigation six weeks ago and was now sitting directly on the bearing the instruments had mapped.

"Then I guess we'd better go look," he said.

The *Steady Returns* hummed around them. The drift intensified on the sensor panel, the chrono reading sliding another half-second out of alignment. Ben watched the survey world grow larger on the nav display and felt the wrongness tighten in his chest — a physical pressure, the body knowing what the mind was still trying to keep at arm's length.

His hands tightened on the copilot's armrest.

## Scene 02.02

The *Steady Returns* had set down on a pad that hadn't seen maintenance in thirty years.

Ben dropped the last meter from the boarding ramp onto packed earth, his boots kicking up a puff of reddish dust that the wind snatched away before it could settle. The air tasted dry and mineral, the way air tasted on worlds that had never supported anything more ambitious than scrub-grass and the occasional survey team that didn't stay long enough to name the place.

Desh came down behind him, a datapad already in his hand. "Landing beacon's civilian. Pre-Galactic Alliance, maybe older. Someone's been through here since the last official survey, but not recently enough to upgrade the hardware."

"How recently?"

Desh tilted the datapad so Ben could see the signal log. "Beacon's been pinging for at least twelve standard years. No registry in the local traffic net. Could be a smuggler waystation, could be a supply dump for the mining ops three systems over." He pocketed the datapad and scanned the horizon with the same flat, unhurried expression he'd worn through every mission briefing Ben had ever sat through with him. "Could be nothing."

"It's not nothing," Ben said.

The wrongness was stronger planetside. He'd felt it the moment the *Steady Returns* had dipped below the atmosphere — not a spike, not a warning, just a pressure that hadn't been there in orbit. The way a room felt different when someone was standing behind you, even before you turned around.

He didn't say that to Desh. Desh didn't need the Force to read a situation, and Ben didn't need to make his own unease someone else's problem.

Torin leaned against the bulkhead behind her, observing. His warmth was still there, but it had acquired a watchfulness that Ben hadn't seen in the archive. He was cataloguing the ship the way he'd catalogued the folios—making connections Ben couldn't follow.

Kael had found the jump seat in the aft compartment and strapped himself in without being asked. His silence was deliberate. Ben had spent enough time around quiet people to know the difference between silence that meant nothing and silence that meant *I'm waiting*.

Desh was already running the nav computer, his hands moving across the interface with the same efficiency he'd shown at the monitoring console. "Corridor heading's locked. First node is six hours out." He didn't ask if Ben was sure. He didn't ask what they were going to do when they got there.

Ben braced for what came next. The choice was made. The *Steady Returns* was climbing.
