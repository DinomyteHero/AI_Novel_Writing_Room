The datapad screen displayed fourteen flagged reports, each tagged with the same notation: *ANOMALOUS FORCE SIGNATURE — NO MATCH IN ARCHIVE.*

Ben stood at the edge of his father’s desk, scrolling through the data as the blue glow caught the underside of his chin. Survey teams. Hyperspace corridor assessments. A routine mineral extraction study that had somehow generated a Force-sensitivity incident report. Each flag pointed to the same stretch of the Outer Rim, clustering along a bearing he recognized without wanting to.

He had been feeling that bearing for three months—two months before the first report arrived.

"The first one came in when?" he asked.

"Twenty-two days ago." Luke set a cup of caf on the desk but remained standing. He moved with his usual office manner—unhurried, deliberate, a stillness that looked like patience but functioned as precision. "Three weeks before I pulled you from the meditation rotation."

Ben scrolled to the earliest entry. A survey team had flagged the anomaly as equipment malfunction, then added a handwritten addendum: *Instruments read correctly. The Force does not.* The note was unsigned. Whoever wrote it had thought better of attaching their name to the sentiment.

He understood the instinct.

The datapad felt cool against his fingers. Underneath the physical sensation, faint and persistent, the wrongness hummed. It was the same sourness he had been cataloging since before these reports began—an off-key note in the architecture of the Force, like a stress fracture no one else could hear. In Luke’s office, the sensation was quieter than it had been in the meditation chamber. The Grand Master’s presence had that effect. His father occupied the Force the way a gravitational body occupied space—not loudly or aggressively, but completely, everything nearby settling into the geometry of his mass.

Ben had grown up in that gravity. He knew how to stand in it without losing his footing.

He set the datapad on the desk. "What do you feel when you reach out to it?"

The question was level. Professional. But his fingers lingered on the datapad’s edge and he kept his gaze down—the tell of someone who had rehearsed the moment.

His father didn't answer immediately. He wasn't being evasive; he was considering. Luke’s hands wrapped around his cup as he stared into the middle distance, checking the currents of the Force rather than composing a speech.

"Dark side remnant," Luke said. "Old. Possibly pre-Republic or early Sith. The pattern suggests something that burned itself out centuries ago and left a cold spot." He paused. "Like Malachor. The wound is real, but it is closed."

Ben looked at the fourteen flags. The unsigned addendum. "It doesn't feel closed to me."

Luke’s attention sharpened. He set down the cup. "Tell me what you feel."

The request was genuine. That was the quality Ben could never quite dismiss, even when he wanted to: Luke Skywalker asked questions because he intended to use the answers. But Ben also heard the underlying challenge: *convince me.* 

Ben chose his words with care. "It isn't dark. There is no pull, no hunger. Nothing that reads like a presence." He paused, turning over three months of listening. "It’s structural. The way a building sounds different when a support column cracks. The Force isn't corrupted—it’s strained. As if the weight distribution changed and the rest is compensating."

Luke’s expression settled into a familiar mask: the careful attentiveness of a man whose model of a problem had just diverged from reality. He was weighing the discrepancy.

"That is a precise description for something three survey teams couldn't articulate."

"Three survey teams weren't listening for it." Ben picked up the datapad, the gesture a small assertion of ownership. "I’ve been hearing it for three months. Whatever this is, it isn't a remnant. Remnants fade. This is getting louder."

Luke didn't touch his caf. "Getting louder."

"Yes."

"How much louder?"

Ben met his father’s eyes. The question wasn't procedural. Luke was asking him to quantify the unquantifiable. He was asking Ben to stand by his intuition.

"Enough that I can't ignore it," Ben said. "Enough that I think the survey teams are hearing it too, even if they lack the framework to recognize it."

Silence followed—the specific kind that happened when two people who loved each other arrived at different conclusions from the same data. Ben had grown up in that silence, too. But this time it felt sharper, like the moment before a door closes on something that cannot be retrieved.

Luke moved to the window. The Temple spires rose beyond the transparisteel. He watched them as if recalibrating his perspective.

"I suspect it may be a cyclical phenomenon," Luke said. "There are Force-active sites in the Outer Rim that pulse on long timescales—centuries between fluctuations. The coordinates in these reports are consistent with known corridors of ancient Sith activity."

Ben heard the unspoken subtext. *Cyclical. Old. Known.* Luke was fitting the anomaly into an existing archive, the way a navigator interprets an unfamiliar star by triangulating from fixed points. It was the right methodology for ninety-nine problems out of a hundred.

But Ben had been present for the hundredth problem. Twice.

He had stood three meters from his cousin and felt the dark side wearing Jacen’s face. He had been inside the Maw when Abeloth’s influence bent the Force into nameless shapes. He had sat across from Vestara and felt her presence flicker between her own soul and what the Sith had carved into her.

The wrongness along that Outer Rim bearing didn't feel like any of those. Yet it possessed the same quality: the texture of something without precedent. No archive entry. No established protocol.

Luke’s framework was sound. It was also going to miss the mark.

Ben set the datapad back on the desk. He didn't look away from his father’s profile. "Send me."

Luke turned. "What?"

"Send me to the survey route. I can document what the teams are missing. I can listen in ways they can't."

Luke’s expression shifted toward surprise. "That is a standard survey assignment. I was going to ask—"

"I know what you were going to ask." Ben’s voice stayed level, but an edge surfaced. "Whether I wanted backup. Whether I thought I needed someone else."

Luke studied his son as if Ben were a puzzle he was still assembling. "Do you?"

Ben could have said yes. A partner was standard protocol for an uncertainty of this magnitude. His father was offering an out—the safe choice that would let Luke sleep better knowing his son wasn't alone on the edge of the unknown.

Ben didn't take it. "No."

Luke nodded slowly. He moved back to the desk and slid the mission datapad across the surface. It was a formal transfer, the bureaucratic ritual that turned a conversation into an order. "The route covers four systems. Standard survey protocols, Force-sensitivity incident documentation, full report to the Council."

"Understood."

His father looked at him. Not at the datapad or the flagged reports, but at Ben.

There was a look in his eyes Ben couldn't quite name—pride and worry occupying the same space. It was the expression of a man who had learned to give his son room and had never made peace with the cost. Ben had seen it at his Knighting. He had seen it at his mother’s memorial, when Luke had gripped his shoulder in silence.

He didn't look away.

"You'll check in from Terminus," Luke said. It was a command.

"Yes."

Luke picked up his caf. His hand was steady. The moment ended.

Ben tucked the datapad under his arm. The wrongness hummed beneath the Temple’s harmonics, patient and structural—the sound a building makes before the foundation gives way.

Neither mentioned that the Grand Master had just sent one man to investigate a phenomenon fourteen reports couldn't explain. Neither spoke of the choice Ben had made, or why Luke hadn't pushed back.

Ben crossed to the door. The warmth of the office receded behind him.
