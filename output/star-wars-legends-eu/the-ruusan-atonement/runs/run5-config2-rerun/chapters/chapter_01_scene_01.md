The Force had been off-key for three months, and Ben Skywalker was the only person in the Temple who couldn't stop hearing it.

He sat in the dark, palms flat against the meditation chamber floor. The stone felt cool. He tried again. The technique was basic—first-year initiate work assigned to children who couldn't sleep. Open the channel. Let the Force flow through rather than around. Stop fighting the current.

He’d been at it for an hour.

The Temple breathed. Hundreds of Jedi slept or sat in contemplative stillness, their presences threading together into a warm resonance that had likely built in these halls since before his father was born. On a good night, Ben could drop into that current in under a minute. The hum of so many centered minds usually felt like standing in a room where every instrument was in tune.

Tonight was different.

The dissonance persisted beneath the surface, the way a cracked bell sounds wrong even when you aren't listening for it. It wasn't loud or aggressive. It was just stubbornly, infuriatingly flat—one note in the architecture of the Force sitting a half-step off from where it belonged. Ben’s perception snagged on it like a tongue finding a chipped tooth. He couldn't unfind it. 

He shifted his weight, resettled his hands, and exhaled. Around him, the chamber remained constant: cool air, the mineral scent of old stone, the distant subsonic thrum of Coruscant’s infrastructure filtering up through thirty floors of duracrete. Everything felt tactile. Real.

The flat note remained.

Three months. He’d catalogued every possible explanation with the thoroughness of a man who spent too much time in his own head: hypervigilance, trauma masquerading as instinct, the weight of recent losses. Each served as a convenient exit ramp from the conversation he was avoiding.

During two separate briefings, Senior Masters had described the ambient disturbance as a mere weather pattern—unpleasant, temporary, not worth a mobilization. Ben had sat there and nodded, hands folded, his expression a mask of acceptance. The words had burned in his throat: *This isn't weather. It’s a gear grinding in the wall, and I’d like to find it before the ceiling collapses.*

He’d swallowed the warning.

He knew how it would land. The Skywalker kid, seeing shadows again. Understandable, given his history. Concerning, if it persisted. *Have you considered additional counseling?*

His father would have believed him. Luke would have moved the entire Order to investigate, which presented its own problem. Ben would have to explain why he’d waited three months to mention it. Why he hadn't escalated. Why he’d sat alone in a meditation chamber instead of walking into the Council chambers to lay it out. His father would have asked those questions with genuine confusion, the way someone asks why you didn't simply ask for help. Ben would have had to admit he couldn't handle it alone. He would have had to prove every doubt anyone ever harbored about whether a Skywalker could be trusted with his own mind.

Easier to sit in the dark.

He stopped trying to block the noise.

That was the one thing he hadn't tried—three months of attempting to sink beneath it, meditate through it, or file it away. He’d been treating it like interference to be filtered. 

He opened his perception and let the flat note in.

The Temple’s resonance remained, layered and heavy. He pushed past it, the way one ignores crowd noise to isolate a single voice. The dissonance sharpened as he focused. He held his position in the Force and studied its texture.

It wasn't dark. That was the first realization. He knew the dark—he’d spent months in his cousin’s orbit watching light bend around that presence. He’d felt the cold, specific weight of the dark side like the temperature drop before a storm. This held no malice, no hunger, none of the oily pull associated with a Sith presence.

He turned the sensation over.

*Structural.* The word arrived as a recognition rather than a thought. The wrongness didn't feel like a presence; it felt like an absence. It was as if a load-bearing beam had developed a crack so fine it remained invisible, yet the building settled wrong at night. The Force wasn't corrupted. It was strained. Weight had been redistributed across a system no longer intact, the remaining supports overcompensating, the entire architecture grinding with the effort.

He’d felt something like this before. He remembered standing in the Maw, where the Force thinned and stretched around gravitational anomalies. He remembered a corridor three weeks before everything changed, when the Force felt like a rope pulled too tight.

He hadn't told anyone then, either.

His hands pressed into the stone. His pulse quickened. That was the real problem—not that he didn't know how to speak, but that he’d chosen silence. Three months of sitting with this while the Council dismissed it as weather, while other Knights moved through their days undisturbed, while he gathered evidence in the privacy of his mind. He had chosen control over communication. He had chosen the image of the Skywalker he thought he had to be over the man who might actually need help.

His father would have believed him immediately. He would have moved mountains. He never would have sat in a dark room for three months keeping secrets.

The meditation chamber door hissed open.

Ben’s eyes snapped toward the light. His father stood silhouetted against the corridor, a datapad in hand. Luke’s expression in the gloom was the one Ben had learned to read before he could read anything else—the set of the shoulders, the sharp quality of his attention. It wasn't alarm. 

It was a mission.

Ben’s breath caught. The wrongness he’d carried alone was about to become real. Someone else would know. The isolation that had felt like safety suddenly felt like a cage he was about to exit, and he wasn't sure which side of the door was more dangerous.

He stood, the cold air hitting his palms where the stone had warmed them. The grinding persisted, but now it had a direction. A source. 

He crossed the chamber toward the light. His father waited. The reluctant thing in Ben’s chest—the part that had spent three months hiding—shifted into something sharper. It wasn't hope. It was necessity. 

It was time to break the silence.
