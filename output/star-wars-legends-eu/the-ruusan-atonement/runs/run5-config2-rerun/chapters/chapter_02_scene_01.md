The *Steady Returns* shuddered. Desh corrected the micro-oscillation before the instrument panel registered the drift. He hadn't looked at the readout; he felt the tremor through the yoke, the way a tongue finds a loose tooth—automatic, instinctive.

Ben watched from the copilot’s seat. "Does she always do that?"

"Every forty minutes or so." Desh kept his gaze on the hyperspace stream. "The port stabilizer has a thermal expansion issue. It overheats, compensates poorly, then corrects itself. I’ve filed it under *character*."

"Most mechanics would file it under *repair*."

"Most mechanics haven't met my parts budget."

The blue-white of hyperspace washed across the viewport in slow pulses. The cockpit smelled of recycled air and stale coffee. Desh’s mug sat wedged into a jury-rigged holder welded to the console at an angle that suggested improvisation under duress. Three secondary instrument panels ran on non-standard hardware, their indicator lights a sharp amber that didn't quite match the originals. Someone had labeled the life support override with a strip of adhesive tape and a marker. The handwriting was Desh’s.

The ship shuddered again. Desh caught it with the same effortless twitch.

"You do that without looking," Ben noted.

"She talks to me."

"Through the yoke."

"Through the yoke." Desh reached for his coffee. "You Force people have your gift. I have forty thousand hours of flight time and a ship with opinions."

Ben settled back. The chair had been reupholstered with mismatched material, then salvaged again with a patch on the left armrest. He had occupied this seat a dozen times over the past three years. He knew the patch by feel. His thumb traced the seam where the old fabric met the new.

Desh’s cockpit was knowable. Every jury-rigged component had a history, a reason, a fix. The instruments reported what they knew and admitted when they were blind. Nothing here pretended.

Ben reached out through the Force—not grasping for anything specific, just maintaining the reflexive awareness he kept like background hearing. Desh registered as a gap. It wasn't an alarming absence, but a settling one. Most people broadcast emotional texture or the faint hum of intention. Desh gave him nothing. Sitting next to him felt like standing in a room where the background noise had finally stopped. The quiet wasn't emptiness. It was rest.

"The motivator on the secondary hyperdrive is throwing a fault code," Desh said, tapping the glass. "Third time this week."

"Is it actually failing?"

"No. It's lying." Desh tapped it again, and the code cleared. "She does that too. I think she wants attention."

"Your ship is neurotic."

"My ship is forty-two years old and has been rebuilt from the frame up twice. She's earned the right to be difficult."

Ben looked at the fault code's ghost on the screen—cleared, yet the display held the flicker-trace of where it had been. "Most pilots would have scrapped her by now."

"Most pilots aren't sentimental."

"You're not sentimental."

Desh considered this, his expression shifting as he weighed the truth. "I'm practical," he said. "She's paid for. Breaking her in is free. Buying new costs money I don't have."

"That's not sentiment."

"No," Desh agreed. "But it gets the same result."

The oscillation returned. Desh caught it. Ben had stopped being surprised ten minutes ago.

He watched Desh's hands—the slight, automatic adjustments, the economy of motion that came from knowing a machine's vocabulary. There was something clarifying about watching a person do something they were genuinely good at. No performance. Desh flew the *Steady Returns* the way he did everything else: by paying attention to what was actually there rather than what the instruments claimed should be there.

The thought of their shared past arrived without invitation. It slipped in sideways, through a gap in the conversation. Ben looked away, turning his attention back to the viewport. He watched the hypnotic pulse of blue-white and held his focus there like a grip.

Desh cleared his throat. "The nav-computer's been throwing phantom errors since we crossed the Darpa Sector boundary."

Ben let the shift happen. "What kind of errors?"

"Course corrections that don't correspond to any gravitational source." Desh angled the diagnostic screen toward Ben. A column of flagged readings scrolled down the display—small deviations, each individually within the margin of instrument noise, but collectively forming a pattern. "The computer keeps trying to route around something that isn't there. Or that it can't identify. It's been doing this"—he tapped the timestamp on the first flag—"since approximately here."

Ben looked at the timestamp, then at the nav-chart beside it. The coordinates sat squarely on the bearing he'd been tracking for three months. He exhaled, slow and steady.

"What does the computer think it is?"

"That's the problem." Desh scrolled to the error classification. The field read: *OBSTRUCTION TYPE: UNCLASSIFIED. ROUTE DEVIATION: RECOMMENDED. SOURCE: UNKNOWN.* "It doesn't think it's anything. It just keeps trying to go around it anyway."

Ben stared at the screen. The recycled air tasted the same. The hyperspace stream moved the same way. Desh's coffee sat at the same angle. Nothing in the physical space of the cabin had changed.

But Desh had named it. Not the wrongness itself—Desh couldn't feel that. But he had named the displacement. It was the way water routes around a stone it can't see, because the stone moves the water regardless. The nav-computer couldn't classify the source, but it registered the effect. The practical man's instruments had caught what the Force-user had been carrying alone for months.

Desh leaned back. He didn't look at Ben. "That's not instrument error."

It wasn't a question.

"No," Ben said.

"And it started right where you said it would."

Ben's fingers pressed into the patched upholstery. "Yes."

Desh took a long pull from his coffee. When he spoke, his voice carried the same dry tone he'd used for the ship's neuroses, but something underneath had hardened—the tone of a man acknowledging a problem he had already decided to handle. "Then we keep flying toward it. That was the arrangement."

The nav-computer chimed. Approach coordinates. The first survey world was closing in.

The wrongness hit like a pressure drop in a sealed cabin—not violent, but total. Ben's grip on the armrest tightened until his knuckles turned white. The Force screamed with it now, a sound that had been building in register as they approached the source. In his father's office, it had been faint, suppressed by the weight of that presence. Out here, past the Darpa Sector boundary, it had grown with every jump.

And now Desh knew. Not the feeling, but the fact. The displacement was manifest in instrument readings the nav-computer couldn't classify.

Ben's breath came shallow. He kept his eyes on the viewport, but his peripheral vision tracked Desh's hands on the yoke. Steady. Unchanged. The practical man was flying toward an obstruction he couldn't feel, couldn't name, and couldn't avoid—except they weren't avoiding it.

They were flying straight into it.
