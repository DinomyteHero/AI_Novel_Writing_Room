Sera didn’t ask permission. She crossed to the archive console with the easy certainty of someone who had walked this room a thousand times, and her hand found the activation plate before Ben had finished the sentence he’d started to offer.

The console hummed to life. Not the hesitant flicker Ben had gotten from the stasis-ring displays — a full, immediate response, the system recognizing a user it had been waiting for. Holographic light washed over her face, and for an instant her expression snagged on something before settling smooth again. A thousand years, and the machine still knew her hand.

Desh shifted his weight behind Ben’s left shoulder. “That was fast.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was watching Sera’s fingers move over the interface — not the tentative tapping of someone learning an unfamiliar system, but the precise, economical motion of a user who had built her muscle memory before the language the display used had changed into the one Ben spoke now. She wasn’t reading the archive. She was calling it back.

His chest tightened. The unease he’d carried since they came into the fortress was still there — the missing-handrail sensation, the bad step — but it had changed shape. Before, it had belonged to the room. Now it belonged to him. Something was about to tell him the truth, and his body knew it before his mind caught up.

Torin moved to Sera’s right. He didn’t crowd her; there was a practiced distance between them, the sort that came from working together in places where one wrong move could kill you. His hand brushed the console’s edge with the same familiarity Sera had shown, and Ben watched his fingers settle on the old metal the way a pilot’s hand settled on a throttle. No hesitation. Just home.

“Index, please,” Torin said, and his voice had shifted. Still warm, but layered now with age — the voice of a man who had been telling stories in briefing rooms before briefing rooms had a name. “Reformations-era institutional record. Unredacted.”

The holographic display shifted. A timeline bloomed above the console — dates, names, structural reorganizations, the sort of institutional mapping Ben had studied for his Knight trials. He recognized the outline of it. The Ruusan Reformations as every modern Jedi learned them: the Army of Light dissolved, the Order decentralized, the Jedi Lords’ era closed. A thousand years of peace built on lessons from a war that had nearly broken the galaxy.

Except the timeline kept scrolling.

Ben’s focus narrowed. The names were the same — Gavran, the council signatories, the charter revisions — but the cause-and-effect trail was wrong. Decisions his history texts had framed as philosophical corrections were listed here as containment measures. Doctrinal changes he’d been taught to admire showed up under a header he didn’t know, a phrase in Old Galactic Standard the console’s translation matrix rendered as penance-structure.

Torin gestured at the display, and a second index slid forward — personal logs, council minutes, facility-status reports. “You know what I love about modern Jedi?” he said, and the wryness in his voice was casual enough that Ben almost missed the weight under it. “You still think the Reformations were about philosophy.”

“They were about philosophy,” Ben said. The words were out before he’d decided to speak.

Torin looked at him. Not unkindly. “They were about what Gavran couldn’t bring himself to put in the public charter.” He tapped the display. “This is the private version. The one the Order decided not to pass on.”

Ben’s breathing steadied. His mind did what it always did under pressure — compare, catalogue, search for the discrepancy he could fix. But this wasn’t a broken console or a mistranslated glyph. It was the foundation of the institution he’d been raised inside, sitting in front of him in a holographic index he couldn’t read because the language was a thousand years dead.

Kael moved.

Ben caught it from the corner of his eye — the man who had been standing against the wall with his back to the stone, silent since he’d spoken his name in the stasis ring. He stepped forward now, not fast, not hesitant, just there. His shoulder passed within a hand’s breadth of Sera’s, and she didn’t flinch. Didn’t acknowledge him either. The same old familiarity, the kind that came from years of knowing a person’s presence well enough to ignore it until it mattered.

Kael reached past her and touched the display.

One word. He highlighted a single entry in the index — a date marker in Old Galactic Standard — and replaced it with another. The correction was so small Ben almost missed it. A digit. The difference between the year the public Reformations charter was signed and the year the private penance protocols took effect.

Kael didn’t explain. He stepped back, his hand dropping to his side, his eyes already returning to their default scan — exits, threats, angles.

Ben stared at the corrected date. His vertigo sharpened. The survivors weren’t a single voice. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth, and the fact that Kael had chosen this moment — this detail — to break his silence meant something Ben couldn’t yet name.

Sera didn’t look at Kael. She didn’t thank him. She accepted the correction the way she’d accepted the console’s activation — as a thing that had happened, and that she had already folded into her next move.

And then she spoke.

“The Ruusan Reformations were dual-purpose.” Her voice was hoarse, scraped raw by a millennium of disuse, but the sentence was exact — formal, complete, the voice of someone delivering a finding she had waited a very long time to say aloud. “The public restructuring the Order remembers. And a private penance for what the Jedi Lords did at Veranthos.”

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t cushion it with context or apology. She gave it the way she’d given her name in the stasis ring — complete, composed, and finished.

Ben felt the words hit before his intellect caught up.

The floor seemed to drop an inch under him. Not a physical lurch, not quite. More like the centerline of his understanding had shifted and left him trying to balance on air. The Reformations weren’t a philosophical correction. They were damage control. The Order he’d been raised inside, the institution whose identity he had never questioned, had been built on what Gavran chose not to say.

He didn’t ask what the penance was for. He didn’t need to. Sera’s face told him the answer wasn’t in this room — and that she had carried it anyway, had been carrying it for a thousand years, would keep carrying it until the moment she decided he was ready to hear it.

The archive hummed around them. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case, unopened, its faint light steady against the dark stone. Desh hadn’t moved from Ben’s shoulder, and Ben could feel the question he wasn’t asking — the assessment, the threat calculus, the quiet patience of a man who had learned to let Force-users finish changing the world before asking what it meant for the mission.

Ben looked at Sera — the instrument of precision, honed against something harder than stone. At Torin — the keeper of narrative warmth, the one who could make a record feel like a life. At Kael — the quiet correction, the monk’s brevity that had just spoken for the first time since waking, and had chosen to speak about a date.

Three witnesses to the Order’s hidden history. Three people who had waited a thousand years to say what Sera had just said in one composed sentence.

The weight of it settled into Ben’s shoulders — not despair, but a hard new clarity about what he was responsible for. He held himself still, knowing the room had changed around him, knowing there was no way to unhear it now.
