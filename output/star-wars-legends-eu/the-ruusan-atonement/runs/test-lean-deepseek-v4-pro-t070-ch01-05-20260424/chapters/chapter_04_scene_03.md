Level 3 opened into a ring.

Ben stopped at the threshold. Not because he'd chosen to — because his body had stopped, and his mind was still catching up to what his eyes were reporting, and the report didn't make sense. The chamber was circular, built around a central shaft that dropped away into blue-tinged darkness. Around the circumference, spaced at even intervals, twelve alcoves had been cut into the stone. Emergency lighting ran along the floor in strips the color of old bruises. The air was cold and still and tasted of metal and time.

Most of the alcoves were dark.

He counted them without deciding to count them. One, two, three — the dark ones were not empty. The stasis fields were dead, the chambers open, but the shapes inside were still there. Preserved. Bodies that had been sealed and then failed, or been sealed and then died, and the stasis had held them anyway. Four, five, six. The pattern was already forming before he reached the far side of the ring, and the tightness in his chest was forming with it. Seven, eight.

Nine dark chambers.

His hand found the stone beside him. The cold grounded something that wanted to float away. The Force-sense through the dead chambers was nothing — not absence, not void, but a kind of sealed-off silence, like a door that had been closed so long the hinges had rusted shut. Whatever had been in those chambers had been sealed away from more than light and air. They'd been sealed away from the Force itself.

Three chambers were still lit.

The blue glow was faint, flickering — the color of a lightsaber's edge seen through water. The stasis fields were holding, but barely. Ben could feel the strain in them, the way the power fluctuated in cycles that had been repeating for longer than the Republic had existed. And through the fields, muted but distinct, three signatures pressed against his perception.

One was disciplined. Controlled. The Force-presence of someone who had learned to hold themselves still for a very long time and had not forgotten how. One was heavy — dense with a weight Ben couldn't name, the kind of presence that filled a room without trying. And one was clipped, abbreviated, as if someone had learned to make themselves small and hadn't remembered how to stop.

Three living people. Sealed in the dark for a thousand years, and still there. Still waiting.

Ben's chest tightened further. The instinct to move, to do something, was so strong it took him a moment to recognize it as horror. Not at the dead chambers — at the living ones. At the thought of being sealed, aware, for centuries. At the discipline it must have taken not to go mad. At the fact that someone had decided this was necessary and then walked away.

"Ben."

Desh's voice was flat. The operational register — the one he used when he was filing something he didn't like and wanted Ben to know he didn't like it.

Ben turned.

Desh was standing in front of the twelfth chamber. The one that wasn't dark and wasn't lit. The one that was broken.

The stasis field was gone. The chamber's interior was visible through the shattered transparisteel — a narrow space, barely large enough for a person to stand, its walls scored with deep parallel gouges that radiated outward from the center. Not damage from outside. Not structural failure. Something had clawed its way free.

Desh ran his fingers along the edge of one gouge. His expression didn't change. "Systematic. Whatever did this knew what it was doing. The scoring is consistent — same tool, same angle, same force. Took a while." He stepped back, his eyes tracking across the monitoring panel set into the wall beside the chamber. The indicators were still blinking — amber, amber, amber, a pattern that had been repeating since before they'd entered the fortress. "Emergency flags are still live. Breach was logged." He tapped the display. "Weeks ago."

The word landed like a physical thing.

Weeks.

Ben crossed the ring in four strides. The monitoring panel's display was archaic — the same pre-Ruusan script as the command post upstairs — but the timestamps were universal. The breach had been logged twenty-three standard days ago. The emergency protocols had triggered. No one had responded.

"Someone broke out," Desh said. "Recently."

He said it the way he said everything — practical, calm, filing the fact alongside the exits and the escape routes and everything else he was cataloguing in case they needed to run. But his jaw was tight, and Ben could feel the tension in him without reaching for the Force. Desh had been scared since the command post. Now he was something else.

Ben's hand moved to the monitoring panel. The display cycled through its logs — breach detected, containment failure, emergency protocols active — and then stopped on a single line of text that made his stomach drop.

The breach had been logged. The protocols had triggered. The system had screamed for help for twenty-three days.

And the thing that had broken out had had twenty-three days to move.

He looked at the three lit chambers. The three living signatures, still sealed, still dependent on systems that were failing. The empty chamber was behind them now, and whatever had been in it was already walking, already acting, already doing whatever it had spent a thousand years planning to do. The fortress's failure was not imminent. It was already in progress.

"There's another level," Ben said.

Desh looked at him. "You're sure."

"I can feel it." The Force-sense was pulling downward — past the ring, past the central shaft, toward something deeper. Something that wasn't a signature but wasn't an absence either. Something that had been waiting.

The stairwell was at the back of the ring, descending into the same blue-tinged light that flickered through the active chambers. The air grew warmer as they went down, and the recycled-air tang of the upper levels gave way to something older — dust and metal and the faint greasy smell of machinery that had been running too long without maintenance.

Level 4 was smaller than the ring above. A single chamber, circular, built around a control nexus that rose from the center of the floor like a console designed for someone who never intended to stand up again. Conduits ran from the nexus into the walls, into the ceiling, into the stone itself — a web of connections that fed upward into the ring, into the chambers, into the system that had been holding for a thousand years.

And at the nexus, still seated, still at his post, was the man who had built it.

Gavran.

Ben had never seen him before. Had never seen a holograph, a portrait, a record. But he knew the face the moment he saw it — the same way he'd known the wrongness in the entry, the same way he'd known the three signatures in the ring. The Force carried recognition that didn't need names.

Gavran's body was preserved — the cold of the lower level, the sealed environment, the same stasis-adjacent technology that had kept the chambers running. His hands were still on the control surfaces, the fingers resting where they'd been when he'd stopped moving. His posture was not peaceful. Peaceful was the wrong word for a man who had died at his post, alone, holding a system together with nothing but will and the Force and whatever desperate technique he'd used to bind himself to the seal.

Settled. That was the word. Settled, like a man who had finished what he could and then stopped. Like a man who had held on until there was nothing left to hold, and then let go.

The monitoring log was still open on the console beside him. The last entry trailed off mid-sentence — a report, a warning, something he'd been trying to record before the end. The pre-Ruusan script was difficult, but Ben could read enough. *Chamber twelve fluctuating. The field is —*

And then nothing.

Ben stood at the nexus and looked at the man who had built the fortress, designed the ring, sealed twelve people as living infrastructure, and then stayed behind to hold the system together when everyone else had died or fled or been forgotten. The weight of it settled into his chest — not grief, not yet, but the shape grief would fill when he had time to feel it. Recognition. The understanding that someone had chosen to do this, and the choice had cost everything, and the cost had not been enough.

Gavran had held until he couldn't. And now the holding was Ben's problem.

He turned away from the nexus. Climbed the stairs back to the ring. The three lit chambers were still flickering, the three signatures still pulsing — disciplined, heavy, clipped — each one a person, each one waiting, each one sealed in the dark for a thousand years without knowing the galaxy had moved on.

Ben stopped in front of the nearest active chamber. The control surface was set into the wall beside it — a simple panel, archaic, designed to be operated by someone who knew what they were doing. He lifted his hand and pressed his palm flat against it.

The stasis field hummed.

It was a low sound, almost subsonic, and it ran through the stone under his feet before it registered in his ears. The blue light flickered once, twice, and then steadied — not brighter, but more present, as if the field had noticed him and was waiting to see what he'd do. The three signatures pulsed faintly in acknowledgment. Aware at some level. Listening.

Ben's hand stayed on the control. The hum carried a question he wasn't ready to answer. The three signatures were distinct now — one disciplined, one heavy, one clipped — each one a personality, a will, a person who had been sealed for a thousand years and did not know the galaxy had moved on.

Behind him, the empty chamber's emergency flags still blinked. Twenty-three days. Whatever had broken out was already walking, already acting, already doing whatever it had spent a thousand years planning to do.

Below him, in Level 4, Gavran's body sat at the nexus of the conduit network. His hands were on the control surfaces. His last monitoring log entry trailed off mid-sentence. He held this together until he couldn't.
