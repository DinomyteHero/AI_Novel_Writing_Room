The archive was on Level 2, behind a door that had not been opened in a thousand years.

Sera crossed to it without hesitation. Her stride was the same stride she had used in the stasis chamber — economical, deliberate, the gait of someone who had never learned to waste motion. Ben watched her hand find the access panel and stop there, hovering half a centimeter from the surface, and he understood that she was not reading the interface. She was remembering where it had been.

Her fingers moved.

The sequence was faster than Ben's modern training — not because she was faster than him, but because she was not thinking about it. Her hand knew the shape of the code the way a pilot's hand knew a throttle. The console woke with a sound that was not quite a hum, something lower and older, a frequency the fortress had not produced since before the Republic existed.

"Gavran's archive," Sera said. She did not look at Ben when she said it. She was already reading the display, her grey eyes tracking across data in a script Ben did not recognize. "It is still here."

Desh had stopped near the door. His expression was the operational one — the one Ben had seen a hundred times during GAG operations, the face of a man filing facts he did not like and was not going to discuss until there was time. "That console should not have power."

"The fortress is failing," Sera said. "It is not dead."

Torin moved past Ben before Ben could respond. His hand found the console's secondary display the same way Sera's had found the primary — from memory, from a thousand years of muscle that had not forgotten the shape of the equipment it had been trained on. The holographic interface flickered and resolved into a timeline, dates and names and institutional markers scrolling upward in a script that shifted as Ben watched, the archive's translation protocols adapting to the presence of a modern reader.

"There," Torin said. His voice was still rough from stasis, but the warmth in it was already settling into something recognizable — the register of a man who told stories because stories were how he made sense of things. "The Reformations. You will know this part."

Ben knew it. Every Jedi knew it. The Ruusan Reformations: the restructuring that had ended the Jedi Lords, dissolved the Army of Light, decentralized the Order into something that could not become a military power again. A thousand years of peace built on the lesson that the Jedi were not generals.

The timeline Torin had pulled up did not match the one Ben had been taught.

The names were the same. The dates were the same. But the causal chain — the sequence of decisions, the logic connecting one event to the next — was wrong. Or not wrong. Different. The difference was in what the archive assumed the reader already knew.

Ben felt his breathing steady as his mind engaged the problem. The discrepancy was not a mistake. It was a gap. Something had been removed from the version of the story the modern Order told.

"You are seeing it," Torin said. He was watching Ben, not the display. "The shape of what is missing."

"This is not the Reformations I learned."

"No," Torin agreed. "It would not be."

Kael had not moved from the wall. He had been silent since the stasis chamber — the same closed-door presence, the same economy of motion, the same watchfulness that made Ben think of someone who had learned to make stillness into a weapon. But his gaze had settled on the display, on one line of text near the bottom of the timeline, and something in his expression had shifted.

He crossed the room.

It was the first voluntary thing he had done since waking. Sera did not move aside for him — she held her position at the primary console, her shoulders set, her attention fixed on the data — but Kael did not ask her to. He reached past her, one hand finding the interface with the same thousand-year-old familiarity, and he changed a single word.

The correction was small. A date, or a name, or a designation — Ben could not read the original script well enough to know. But the archive accepted it. The display flickered and reorganized, and the timeline shifted by a fraction of a degree, and Kael stepped back to the wall without a word.

Sera looked at him. Her expression did not change, but something passed between them — a glance that carried more weight than a sentence.

"Kael Drenn," she said. "Still correcting the record."

Kael said nothing. But his silence had changed. It was not the silence of someone who had nothing to say. It was the silence of someone who had just said the only thing that mattered.

Ben registered the shift before he understood it. The survivors were not unified. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth — Sera's precision, Torin's warmth, Kael's quiet refusal to let anything stand that was not accurate. The separation was not a weakness. It was the truth.

Sera turned back to the console. Her hand moved across the display, pulling data into a configuration Ben could not follow — the archive responding to her the way a ship responded to a pilot who had flown it for decades. The timeline collapsed and re-formed around a single entry, a marker Ben recognized from the modern histories: the formal declaration of the Ruusan Reformations, the document that had ended the Jedi Lords and reshaped the Order into what it was today.

She read it in silence. Her expression did not change. But something behind her eyes went very still — the stillness of someone who had waited a thousand years to say something out loud and was choosing, now, to say it.

"The Reformations were public reform," Sera said.

Her voice was precise. Controlled. The same register she had used in the stasis chamber, the same clipped clarity, the same century-of-discipline composure. She was not softening the sentence. She was not offering context or apology or explanation. She was stating a fact.

"And private penance."

The sentence landed.

Ben felt it before he understood it — a drop in his center of gravity, a vertigo that was not physical but registered in his body anyway, the way the wrongness had registered in the entry chamber. His mind was still catching up, still processing the words, but his body already knew what they meant.

The Reformations were penance.

The foundation of the modern Order — the restructuring that had defined a thousand years of Jedi identity, the institutional self-understanding every youngling learned in their first history lessons — had been built on something Gavran chose not to say. Not a reform. Not a lesson learned. A concealed wound. An edited history.

Sera did not elaborate. She did not say what the penance was for. She did not need to. Ben could read the rest of it off her face — the way her jaw was set, the way her grey eyes held his without flinching, the way her composure was not coldness but control. She had waited a thousand years to say that sentence to a Jedi. She was not going to soften it now.

The archive hummed in the silence. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case, faintly lit, unopened. Torin was watching Ben with something that might have been sympathy. Kael had not moved from the wall.

Ben felt the weight of the question settle into his shoulders. If the Reformations were penance, what were they penance for? What had the Order chosen to hide?

He did not ask. The room did not try to answer. The three survivors stood in the archive they had built a thousand years ago, and their presence was the testimony, and the testimony was enough.
