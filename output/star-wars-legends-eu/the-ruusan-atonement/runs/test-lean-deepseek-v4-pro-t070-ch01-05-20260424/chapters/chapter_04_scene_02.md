Level 1 was a command post.

Ben knew it the moment his glow rod swept across the room — the long curve of dead consoles, the raised platform where someone had stood to address a staff that hadn't existed for a thousand years, the communications array dark against the far wall. The air was still and cold and tasted of dust that had settled before the Republic was founded and never been disturbed. His footsteps echoed on stone worn smooth by foot traffic that had stopped before anyone living had been born.

Desh moved to his left, his own glow rod cutting a parallel track across the consoles. "Communications are dead. Power's still running to something, though." He tapped a panel with his knuckle. The sound was dull, hollow. "Residual. Not enough to run the array, but enough to keep the monitoring stations warm."

Ben reached out with the Force.

The sense that came back was not the wrongness he'd felt at the entry — the structured pressure, the held breath. This was something else. A low hum of potential, dormant but not dead, waiting in the walls like water behind a dam. The monitoring stations had been designed to persist. Someone had built them to outlast everything else in the room, and they had.

He found the nearest station and pressed his palm flat against the console.

The Force pulse was targeted and precise — not a push, not a demand, but the kind of contact he'd learned from his father. *Wake up. I'm here. Show me what you were watching.*

The console hummed.

It was a low sound, almost subsonic, and it ran through the stone under Ben's feet before it registered in his ears. The amber indicators along the station's face flickered once, twice, and then held steady — the color of old embers, of light that had been waiting a long time to be seen. The display screen above the console remained dark for another three seconds, and then it resolved into a map.

Ben's breath caught.

The map showed the Outer Rim. Not the modern Rim — the borders were wrong, the sector designations unfamiliar, the systems labeled in a script that predated the Republic's standardization. But the geography was unmistakable. Nodes scattered across the spiral arm like a net that had been cast wide and left to sink. Most of them were red. Dead. A dozen or so pulsed amber — degraded, barely holding. And one, far out near the edge of the network, was flashing white.

BREACH.

The word wasn't in Basic. The script was archaic, pre-Ruusan, but the meaning translated itself through the Force before Ben's conscious mind caught up — a single sharp syllable that meant *failure*, that meant *containment lost*, that meant something had gotten out.

"Ben." Desh's voice was level, the way it always was when he was filing something he didn't like. "What am I looking at."

"I don't know." The words came out before Ben could stop them, and they were honest in a way he didn't want them to be. His eyes tracked the flashing node. The Force around the display felt wrong — not the structured wrongness of the fortress, not the held-breath pressure of the entry, but something strained. Something screaming. The system itself was in pain, and the pain was old and ongoing and getting worse.

Desh didn't ask again. He pulled his datapad from his belt and started photographing the display — the map, the node distribution, the BREACH indicator, the archaic script. His movements were precise and economical, the same way he'd photographed the perimeter facility's data core, the same way he'd catalogued every exit they'd passed since the entry door ground open behind them. His jaw was tight.

Ben watched him work and felt the dread deepen.

Desh wasn't asking what the network was for because he'd already decided it didn't matter. What mattered was that they were standing in the control center of something that was failing, and the failure was spreading, and the only thing Desh wanted to know was how to get out before it reached them.

"You're cataloguing exits."

"I'm cataloguing everything." Desh didn't look up from his datapad. "Exits are part of everything."

"Desh."

The datapad lowered. Desh met his eyes across the console, and his expression was the same one he'd worn on the plateau — assessing, filing, making sure they both knew what they were walking into. "There's a stairwell at the back of the room. Descending. Emergency lighting's still running down there — blue-tinged, low intensity. I can feel the air moving. Warmer than up here." He paused. "You're going down."

It wasn't a question.

"The monitoring system is tracking something," Ben said. "A network. Nodes across the Rim. Most of them are dead. One of them is —" He stopped. The word BREACH was still echoing in his head, and he didn't have a translation that would make sense to someone who couldn't feel what he was feeling. "Something got out. Something that was supposed to stay contained."

"And you want to go toward it."

"I want to know what we're dealing with."

Desh was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded — not agreement, exactly, but acceptance. The same way he'd accepted the defensible position and the lack of extraction and the walls thicker than the weapons they'd brought. "I'll keep cataloguing. You find the stairwell. Try not to get killed before I finish the exit map."

It was almost a joke. Almost. The dry register was there, the Allston-channel deadpan, but the timing was off — the beat landed a fraction too late, and the humor didn't quite catch. Desh was scared. Not panicked, not compromised, but scared in the way a professional got scared when the situation stopped fitting the brief.

Ben felt it land. The warning he didn't have the vocabulary to name. The confidence he'd been carrying — *we can handle this, we've handled worse* — wavered slightly, and he didn't try to steady it. Desh's fear was contagious, and the fact that Desh was letting him see it meant something.

He turned toward the back of the room.

The stairwell was where Desh had said it would be — a dark mouth in the stone, descending into blue-tinged light that seemed to come from somewhere far below. The emergency lighting was low and steady, the color of a lightsaber's edge, and it cast the stairs in an otherworldly glow that made the stone look like something underwater. The air moved upward in a slow current, and it was warmer than the air in the command post — warmer and charged, the way the air felt before a storm.

Ben stepped to the edge of the stairwell and looked down.

The Force down there was alive.

Not wrong. Not strained. Not screaming. Alive, in a way the upper levels were not — pulsing with three distinct rhythms that pressed against his perception like heartbeats transmitted through stone. One was disciplined, controlled, the signature of someone who had learned to hold themselves still for a very long time. One was heavy, dense with something Ben couldn't name — not darkness, but weight, the kind of presence that filled a room without trying. And one was clipped, abbreviated, as if someone had learned to make themselves small in the Force and forgotten how to stop.

Three rhythms. Three heartbeats. Living people, sealed below.

Ben's hand found the railing. The stone was cold under his palm, and the blue light from below caught the edge of his fingers and made them look like someone else's. He didn't know what he was looking at. He didn't know who was down there or how long they'd been waiting or what they'd been sealed to contain. The names and histories were still waiting, still buried, still a level below anything he could reach from here.

But the rhythms were real. The rhythms were now. And the stairwell was descending, and the air was warm and charged and alive, and Ben was already moving before he'd made the decision to move.

Behind him, Desh's datapad clicked once — a photograph of the stairwell, filed alongside the exits and the escape routes and everything else he was cataloguing in case they needed to run.
