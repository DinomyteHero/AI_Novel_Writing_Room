The plateau was a lie.

From the ridge where the *Steady Returns* had set down, the mountain had looked like any other — weathered stone, wind-scoured and patient, the kind of geology that had been sitting here since before anyone thought to name the stars. The wrongness had been diffuse then, a pressure that filled the thin air like weather waiting to break. Ben had felt it through the hull during descent, a compression that made the cockpit feel fractionally too small, and he'd expected to find something waiting at the coordinates. A structure. A signal source. A shape that matched the weight.

What he hadn't expected was to walk halfway across the plateau before he saw it.

The fortress was built into the mountainside so precisely that it read as geology until you were close enough to see the angles. The stone was the same grey as the peak that framed it, weathered to the same patina, cut with the same wind-eroded edges — except where it wasn't. A vertical seam here, too straight for a fracture. A shadow there, too deep for a natural overhang. The alloy that reinforced the stone had dulled over centuries to something that caught the light like mica, and the overall effect was of a building that had been designed not to be seen from a distance.

No symbols. No markings. No indication that the people who'd built it had wanted anyone to know who they were.

Ben stopped twenty meters from the entry face. His breath steamed in the cold, and the thin air burned slightly in his lungs — not enough to slow him, but enough to register as a constant low pressure, the altitude reminding him with every inhalation that this world had not been terraformed for comfort. The wind cut across the plateau in a continuous low register, and the wrongness here was no longer diffuse. It had shape. It had direction. It moved through the rock like a held breath, concentrated and waiting, and the fortress was the thing holding it.

Desh stopped beside him. His breath was steadier — he'd spent more years in hostile environments than Ben had, and his body had learned to compensate for thin atmosphere without thinking about it. He studied the entry face the same way he'd studied the perimeter facility's data core: assessing, cataloguing, filing details for later use.

"No visible defenses," he said. "No emplacements, no sensor arrays, no firing slits. They weren't expecting a siege."

"They weren't expecting anyone to find it."

Desh nodded, accepting the correction. "The approach is defensible, though. One route in, open ground, no cover. Anyone trying to force entry would be visible for half a kilometer." He paused. "No extraction plan, either. If we need to leave in a hurry, we're running the same exposed route back to the ship."

Ben heard the assessment for what it was: not an objection, but a tactical read being filed alongside the other tactical reads Desh had been filing since they'd left the perimeter facility. Defensible position. No extraction. Walls thicker than the weapons they'd brought. Desh wasn't asking if Ben wanted to reconsider. He was making sure they both knew what they were walking into.

The entry mechanism was set into the stone at chest height — a panel of darker alloy, unmarked, seamless except for a palm-shaped depression worn smooth by use. No controls. No interface. Just a place for a hand.

Ben pressed his palm against it.

The mechanism resisted. Not physically — his hand made contact with the alloy, which was cold and slightly rough, the texture of metal that had been shaped by a technique he didn't recognize. The resistance was in the Force, a subtle repulsion that felt like pressing against a held breath. The panel pulsed amber, a light that seemed to come from somewhere deep inside the stone, and Ben felt the mechanism scanning him — not his biometrics, not his heat signature, but something deeper, something that reached into the place where his training lived and tested it against a calibration he didn't share.

The amber deepened. Held.

Refused.

Ben's jaw tightened. He kept his palm flat against the stone and reached again, this time with the technique he'd been taught — the modern Jedi signature, the discipline that had carried him through the Academy and the GAG and every crisis since. The mechanism's pulse didn't change. The amber held steady, patient and indifferent, and Ben felt the wrongness pressing against him from the other side of the door — not hostile, exactly, but discriminating. It knew what it was looking for.

He wasn't it.

"Problem?" Desh's voice was level.

"It doesn't recognize me."

"You're a Jedi. It's a Jedi fortress."

"It's a fortress from an era that didn't train Jedi the way I was trained." Ben's hand stayed pressed against the mechanism. He could feel its pulse like a heartbeat under his palm, slow and regular and utterly uninterested in his credentials. The frustration was a sharp physical sensation — the narrowing of his focus, the way his mind cycled through alternatives and discarded them. Force the lock. Find another entry. Retreat. None of them sat right. The wrongness here was not something you circumvented. It was something you were admitted to, or you weren't.

He stopped trying to impose his technique.

The shift was not conscious — not a decision he made so much as a decision he stopped resisting. He opened his palm flat against the mechanism and reached deeper, past the modern training, past the Academy discipline, past everything he'd learned to be. Into the older signature that lived in his bloodline. The thing that had been there before he'd ever held a lightsaber, before he'd ever sat in a Temple classroom, before he'd ever been anything except his parents' son.

The amber light flickered.

Hesitated.

Shifted to green.

The moment it changed, Ben felt something shift in the Force — not a dramatic surge, not a revelation, but a recognition. The wrongness here acknowledged him. The sensation was not comforting. It was unsettling in its specificity, the way a lock recognized a key it had been waiting for, and Ben understood with a clarity he didn't want that he was not the Jedi he'd trained to be in this moment. He was something older. Something the fortress had been built to admit.

He didn't know what that meant. He knew only that it had worked, and that the fortress knew something about him that he was still learning.

The door ground open.

The sound was low and sustained, stone grinding against stone, and the air that spilled out had not moved for a thousand years. It carried a weight that Ben's lungs registered immediately — a staleness that spoke of centuries of isolation, of corridors that had been sealed before the Republic existed and never unsealed. His hand dropped from the mechanism. The green light faded, and the entry corridor beyond the door was dark, and the wrongness that had been pressing against the hull and the plateau and the sealed stone was no longer outside.

It was inside. Concentrated. Shaped. Channeled through the mountain itself, moving through the rock like a held breath that had been waiting to exhale.

Desh pulled a glow rod from his belt and swept the corridor. The beam caught stone walls, a floor worn smooth by foot traffic that had stopped a millennium ago, a ceiling high enough to suggest the people who'd built this place had not been concerned with conserving space. No movement. No sound except the fading grind of the door settling into its open position.

Ben reached into the Force ahead of them.

The sense that came back was not blank. It was shaped — directed, almost architectural, the way a building's layout could be felt through its pressure differentials. The wrongness was not a wound or a void. It was something structured, something that had been maintained, and somewhere deeper in the fortress something was alive.

He didn't say it. Desh didn't ask. The two of them stood at the threshold of a tomb that had been sealed for a thousand years, and the air was cold and still and heavy with the weight of what it had been sealed to contain.

Ben stepped inside.
