Ben deleted the third draft before he'd finished reading it.

The cursor blinked at him from the empty message field, patient and indifferent. He'd gotten further that time — three paragraphs, the bones of an honest report, the kind his father would read once and understand — and then his thumb had found the delete key and held it down until the screen was blank again.

The cockpit of the *Steady Returns* was quiet except for the nav computer's idle cycling, a soft churn of course-correction probabilities that had been running since they'd lifted from the perimeter facility. The planet's curve filled the lower third of the viewport, cloud-streaked and unremarkable, a survey-stop world that had turned out to be anything but. Ben's hands rested on the console, not typing.

Three drafts. Three deletions. The transmission queue was still empty.

He could feel the wrongness through the hull. It had direction now — not the ambient pressure of the facility's warning message but something sharper, a pull that aligned with the coordinates he'd copied onto his datapad and could not stop checking. The nav computer had the route. The *Steady Returns* was fueled and flight-ready. The only thing between Ben and the jump was the report he wasn't sending.

He started a fourth draft.

*Dad — The survey stop at Ord Vareel turned up something I can't explain over comms. I'm forwarding the data we pulled from the facility's core, but the short version is this: there's a network of pre-Ruusan military installations arranged in a containment perimeter around uncharted space. One of them is still active. Its warning message has been cycling for a thousand years. The seal is failing. I'm following the coordinates to the central installation to assess the situation before —*

He stopped.

Before what? Before he called for backup? Before he waited for a committee to debate the protocol for responding to a distress signal that had been broadcasting since before the Republic existed? Before he let someone else decide how fast was fast enough?

His thumb found the delete key.

The screen went blank.

The cockpit hatch cycled open behind him. Ben didn't turn. He knew the sound of Desh's boots on the deck plating, the particular weight of his step when he'd been thinking about something long enough to want it said out loud.

Desh stopped at the copilot's station and leaned over Ben's shoulder. Close enough to read the empty message field. Close enough to see the transmission queue with nothing in it.

"You're not sending that."

It wasn't a question. Ben's hands stayed on the console. The wrongness pressed against the bulkheads — spatial, directional, a compression that made the cockpit feel smaller than it was. He could feel Desh waiting, the same way Desh had waited in a dozen GAG briefings for someone to say the thing everyone in the room already knew.

"Not yet."

The words landed in the quiet. Ben didn't elaborate. He didn't need to. Desh had read the first line of the unsent report — *Dad — The survey stop at Ord Vareel turned up something I can't explain over comms* — and recognized the shape of what Ben was doing. The two of them had served in the same machine. Desh knew the signature of institutional avoidance from inside. He knew what it looked like when someone decided the chain of command was the problem instead of the solution.

He didn't argue.

He reached across Ben to the nav computer and pulled up the coordinate entry field. His hand hovered over the interface, waiting.

"The fortress," Ben said.

Desh entered the coordinates. The nav computer accepted them with a processing chime and began its calculation — probability matrices cascading across the display, the system mapping a route through a sector that did not exist on any Republic survey. The display cycled through course corrections, hazard assessments, the slow arithmetic of threading a starship through unmapped space.

Ben watched the numbers resolve.

The wrongness was pulling harder now. Not the diffuse pressure of the perimeter facility's warning — something sharper, a compression that had a center and a direction and a clock that was running down. Every hour of calculation was an hour the seal kept degrading. Every hour of waiting was an hour Veraine's head start grew longer.

"Route's provisional," Desh said. "The computer's working off fragmentary data — the old chart from the core, plus what the *Steady's* sensors can extrapolate. I've got a sixty-percent confidence on the first leg. It'll improve as we get closer."

"Can you get us there?"

Desh glanced at him. "You're asking if I can navigate uncharted space with a thousand-year-old map and a nav computer that's never seen this sector before."

"Yes."

"I can try." He settled into the copilot's chair and began locking in the course corrections. "But if the route degrades mid-jump, we're dropping out of hyperspace blind. No beacon, no relay, no way to call for help."

Ben nodded.

Desh's hands moved across the console, steady and methodical, the same way they'd moved across the data core interface in the cold of the perimeter facility. He wasn't Force-sensitive. He couldn't feel the wrongness pressing against the hull, the spatial distortion that made the cockpit feel fractionally too narrow, the distance between the nav computer and the viewport not behaving the way distance should. But he'd read the first line of Ben's unsent report and understood exactly what it meant.

He was still here.

The nav computer chimed. Route locked. The display showed a course threading through a gap in the charted hyperlanes, a corridor of navigable space that followed the bearing of the wrongness like a plumb line. The *Steady Returns* was ready to jump.

Ben looked at the empty message field. The transmission queue was still clear. The report to Luke was still unwritten.

He closed the comm interface.

"Punch it."

Desh pulled the hyperspace lever.

The stars stretched into blue-shifted lines and the *Steady Returns* slid into the jump — and the wrongness did not fade. It should have. Hyperspace was distance, separation, the universe compressed into something you passed through rather than inhabited. The wrongness should have dimmed with the realspace receding behind them, the way pressure eased when you climbed out of a gravity well.

Instead it intensified.

The pressure came through the bulkheads — not a sound, not a vibration, but a compression that Ben felt in his ribs and behind his eyes and in the space between his shoulder blades. The cockpit was the same size it had been a moment ago, but the distance between the console and the viewport felt wrong, stretched and compressed at the same time, as if the jump had taken them into a medium that didn't obey the rules space was supposed to obey. The blue of hyperspace churned beyond the transparisteel, and the wrongness was not behind them anymore.

It was ahead.

Ben's fingers curled around the armrest. The suppressed doubt from the moment before the jump — the cost of what Desh had just done, the loyalty that enabled avoidance — crystallized into something harder. Not acceptance. Bracing. The seal's failure was no longer abstract. It was the medium they were traveling through, a pressure that should have diminished with distance and instead was pressing harder the closer they got to the source.

Desh was watching the nav display, his face lit pale by the course-correction feed. "That's not normal."

"No."

"The wrongness. It's not fading."

"I know."

Desh didn't ask what it meant. He'd felt the chrono drift in the perimeter facility, the wrong-growing vegetation, the grief without object that had pooled in the cold air of the data core room. He couldn't sense the Force, but he'd learned to read its damage the way a pilot learned to read turbulence — through the things that stopped behaving the way they should.

The *Steady Returns* plunged deeper into the unmapped corridor, and the pressure built.

Ben sat with it. The report to Luke was light-years behind them now, unreachable until they dropped out of hyperspace, and by then the decision would be locked in. No comm signal could catch them in uncharted space. No recall order could reach a ship that wasn't on any chart. The withholding was complete.

Desh had named it. Desh had entered the coordinates anyway.

The cost of that sat unexamined at the edge of Ben's awareness — the loyalty that enabled avoidance, the pattern they'd both recognized and chosen not to interrupt. He didn't reach for it. The wrongness was pressing too hard for introspection, the spatial distortion demanding his attention the way a hand on his shoulder would, and the only thing that mattered now was what waited at the center of the perimeter.

The nav computer painted a route through a sector that did not officially exist.

The *Steady Returns* carried them toward the source.
