The second world was nothing like the first.

Where the first had been dry hardpan and wind-scoured stone, this one was wet — a temperate belt of low hills and thick ground cover, the soil dark and heavy underfoot, the air thick with the smell of decaying vegetation. The *Steady Returns* had set down in a natural clearing a kilometer from the ruins, her landing struts sinking ankle-deep into the loam, and by the time Ben and Desh had the ramp down the humidity had already worked its way into everything. Ben's tunic clung to his back. The datapad screen fogged at the edges.

Desh pulled a leaf off his boot. "I preferred the dust."

"You said the dust was like a fungus."

"It was. This is worse."

The ruins were visible through the trees — the same rectilinear foundations, the same too-precise stonework, the same collapsed archways half-swallowed by vegetation instead of wind-scour. Different climate. Different geology. Same hand.

Ben felt it before he saw it. The wrongness had been waiting since they'd dropped out of hyperspace, a pressure behind his ribs that sharpened as they approached the site, the way a sound you'd been trying to ignore resolved into a voice when you got close enough to the source. It was stronger here than it had been at the first world — not dramatically, but enough that his body registered the difference before his mind could name it. The ground felt fractionally closer than his eyes said it was. His footsteps landed a half-beat out of sync with the rhythm his muscles expected.

He didn't say anything. He kept walking.

The central structure was in better condition than the first world's — two walls still standing above head height, the stone dark with moisture and patched with lichen. Ben ducked through a gap in the north wall and stopped.

The markings were there.

Cut into the interior face of the standing wall in the same angular columns, the same parallel tool-grooves, the same script that didn't match anything in the Temple archives. The stone was darker here, denser, a different composition entirely from the pale sedimentary rock of the first world — and the cuts were just as clean. The same hand had made them. The same tool. The same pressure behind each stroke.

Ben crouched. His fingers stopped an inch from the stone.

"Same marks," Desh said from behind him. His voice was neutral, but the neutrality was the kind that took effort.

"Yeah."

"Different rock."

"Yeah."

"Same carver."

Ben didn't answer. He reached out and touched the cut.

The wrongness hit him the same way it had at the first world — a low vibration up through his arm, a fractional error in the distance between his hand and the stone, the sense that the marking was simultaneously where his fingers touched it and somewhere slightly to the left. The Force was present, but it was wrong here, the way a note played flat was wrong, the way a handrail you reached for in the dark was wrong when your hand closed on empty air.

He pulled his hand back. The vibration stayed in his fingers for a heartbeat longer than it should have.

"Ben."

He stood. "It's the same."

"That's not what I was going to ask."

"I know."

Desh looked at him for a long moment. The look said he'd noticed the pause before Ben touched the stone, and the way Ben's shoulders had tightened, and the fact that Ben still hadn't turned around. The look also said he wasn't going to push, because pushing was for people who hadn't learned to read each other's silences in the back of a GAG transport.

"Fine," Desh said. "I'll get the scanner."

He left Ben alone with the markings. The wrongness pressed against Ben's perception like a second gravity, and he let it press, and he didn't name it, and he cataloged the site with the same professional precision he'd used at the first world. Shapes. Measurements. Context. No mention of the vibration or the fractional error or the way the Force had felt wrong the moment his fingers touched the stone.

The omission was easier this time.

---

Back aboard the *Steady Returns*, the climate control was struggling with the humidity. The air in the main hold tasted recycled and faintly metallic, and the deck plates were still cool underfoot from the hyperspace transit. Desh had the nav console up, the holographic display casting blue light across the hold, and he was laying in the coordinates from the first world with the methodical patience of someone who'd done this kind of work before the Order had ever asked him to.

Ben stood at the console's edge, watching.

"First site," Desh said, and a point lit up on the display — a system in the Outer Rim, unremarkable, one of a dozen stops on Luke's survey route. "Second site." Another point, further Rimward, in a sector that had been charted sixty years ago and barely visited since.

Desh drew the line.

It cut through the display in a clean vector, running from the first world through the second and extending outward into the chart-sector beyond. The line passed through a handful of mapped systems — none of them inhabited, none of them surveyed in detail — and then it hit the edge of the chart and kept going.

Into empty space.

Desh sat back. "That's not supposed to be there."

"What isn't?"

"The line." He tapped the display, and the vector extended further, running through a cluster of coordinates that the nav computer had flagged with a soft amber warning. "Those systems don't appear on any chart I have access to. Not the civilian registry, not the Temple survey database, not the old GAG astrogation files. They're not classified. They're not redacted. They're just not there."

Ben stared at the display. The line was a clean vector pointing into nothing — a direction with no destination, a bearing that led off the edge of the known galaxy and kept going.

"How many systems?" he asked.

"Four that I can resolve from the bearing. Could be more. The mass-shadow readings are inconsistent — something's throwing off the sensors." Desh turned in his chair. "Ben. This isn't a coincidence. Two sites, same markings, same era, aligned on a bearing that points into uncharted space. Someone built these things in a line."

"I know."

"And the survey route your father designed runs right along it."

Ben didn't answer. He'd seen it the moment Desh drew the line — the way the survey stops lined up with the bearing, the way Luke's coordinates followed the vector like a course already plotted. Coincidence or design. He didn't know which one was worse.

"Punch the coordinates," he said.

Desh's fingers moved across the console. The nav computer accepted the input, processed for a long moment, and then the display shifted. The amber-warning systems resolved into faint points of light — mass shadows, gravity wells, the suggestion of stellar bodies where the charts said there shouldn't be any. The computer couldn't confirm them. It couldn't name them. It could only acknowledge that something was out there, occupying space that the official records had left blank.

Ben looked at the empty sector. The wrongness was a steady pressure behind his ribs, a compass-needle that had been pointing in this direction since Coruscant, and now he could see where it led.

Something was out there.

Desh's voice was quiet. "Do we follow it?"

Ben didn't answer right away. He was thinking about the omission in his report to Luke, and the way the wrongness had felt when his fingers touched the stone, and the line on the display that ran exactly along the bearing his body had been tracking since before he'd had a word for it.

"Yeah," he said. "We follow it."
