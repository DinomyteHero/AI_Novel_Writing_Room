The access panel came away with a shriek of corroded metal.

Desh caught it before it hit the floor, bracing it against his hip while he peered into the cavity behind it. The emergency lighting painted his face in pale blue, and his breath plumed white in the cold. "This is going to be ugly."

"How ugly?"

"The storage media's older than anything I've ever rigged. Crystal matrix, pre-standard interface. The coupler's going to have to translate on the fly, and I don't know if the power draw is going to trip whatever's still running this place."

Ben stood at his shoulder, watching the ancient circuitry flicker with residual charge. The data core room was smaller than the command center they'd passed through — a circular chamber maybe four meters across, the walls lined with access ports and dead display panels, the air cold enough that he could feel it in his lungs. The wrongness was a steady pressure now, not the sharp weight of the warning message but something deeper, as if the facility itself was holding its breath.

"Can you do it?"

Desh glanced at him. "You're asking if I can jury-rig a thousand-year-old military data core with a coupler designed for modern civilian systems."

"Yes."

"I can try." He pulled the coupler from his belt — a compact cylinder with an interface port at one end and a nest of adapter cables at the other. "But if this sparks wrong, we're going to lose whatever's left in there. These old crystal matrices don't handle power surges well."

Ben nodded. "Do it."

Desh worked in silence for a long minute, stripping corroded connectors and splicing fresh leads, his hands steady despite the cold. Ben watched the displays at the far end of the room — the same scrolling characters, the same cycling warning, the same two words repeating on a loop that had been running for a thousand years.

SEAL FAILING.

SEAL FAILING.

The pressure in his chest tightened.

"Here we go." Desh seated the coupler against the ancient interface port and thumbed the activation switch.

Nothing.

Then a spark — blue-white, sharp, the smell of ozone cutting through the cold — and the coupler's indicator light flickered from red to amber. A second spark. The light held. Amber shifted to green.

"Connection's live," Desh said. "Data stream is fragmentary — the matrix is degraded, maybe sixty percent intact — but it's reading."

The displays at the far end of the room flickered.

The scrolling characters stuttered, froze, and then the holographic projectors in the center of the console bank hummed to life. Light resolved into shape — a star map, rendered in three dimensions, the points of light floating in the cold air like something Ben could reach out and touch.

It was not a modern chart.

The stars were in the wrong places. The constellations were unfamiliar, the sector boundaries marked in a notation system Ben's translation software struggled to parse, and the hyperspace lanes — the thin blue threads connecting system to system — followed routes that did not exist on any Republic survey. The map was old. Older than the Galactic Alliance. Older than the Empire. Older than the Republic that had come before.

"Desh."

"I see it."

"What am I looking at?"

Desh moved to the console, his eyes tracking across the display the way they'd track a threat assessment. "Navigation data. Military-grade, pre-Ruusan. These aren't survey charts — they're operational maps. Deployment routes, supply lines, strategic positions." He pointed at a cluster of markers near the map's center. "See the pattern? Those aren't random installations. They're a perimeter."

Ben stepped closer.

The markers formed a ring — a dozen points of light arranged in a rough circle around a central point that pulsed with a deeper, steadier glow. The perimeter installations were spaced at regular intervals, each one connected to the next by a thin thread of blue, and the whole network was oriented around the central point like a wheel around a hub.

A network.

Not one facility. Not one warning. A dozen installations, maybe more, arranged in a deliberate geometry around something at the center.

The pressure under his sternum sharpened.

"What's at the center?"

Desh was already working the console, his fingers moving across the ancient interface with the careful precision of someone who knew exactly how much force it took to break a thousand-year-old control surface. "The data's fragmentary. The central marker is flagged with a priority designation — military, high-clearance — but the identifier's corrupted. I'm getting a name, though. On the authorization logs."

He tapped a sequence into the console.

The display shifted. The star map receded, replaced by a cascade of status reports and system diagnostics — the same scrolling characters Ben had seen on the command center displays, but here they were organized, structured, the fragments of a military bureaucracy frozen in amber. Maintenance logs. Personnel rotations. Supply manifests. And at the bottom of each entry, a signature line.

The same signature, repeated across a hundred different documents.

Gavran.

Ben read the name without recognition. It meant nothing to him — a word in a dead language, a signature on a thousand-year-old maintenance log — but the weight of it landed anyway. Someone had built this. Someone had commanded it. Someone had signed off on the supply runs and the personnel rotations and the system diagnostics, and that someone had a name, and the name was still here, preserved in the crystal matrix like a voice that had forgotten how to stop speaking.

"Gavran," Desh said. "That's not a modern name. Pre-Ruusan, if I'm reading the rank structure right. Jedi Lord."

Ben looked at him.

"Jedi Lord?"

"Old title. New Sith Wars era. The Jedi organized into military ranks back then — Lords, Battle Masters, the whole command structure. This wasn't a temple. It was a fortress." Desh's voice was flat, professional, the same register he'd used in GAG briefings when the intel was bad and everyone in the room knew it. "Ben, this network — the perimeter pattern, the command-and-control architecture, the priority designations — this is containment infrastructure. Someone built a dozen installations around something they didn't want getting out."

The pressure in Ben's chest became a weight he couldn't shift.

"Can you get me the coordinates for the central installation?"

Desh hesitated. "The data's fragmentary. I can pull the route, but I can't guarantee the destination is still there. A thousand years is a long time for something to stay hidden."

"Pull it."

Desh worked the console. The star map returned, the perimeter ring of installations rotating slowly in the cold air, and then the display shifted again — zooming in on the central point, the pulsing marker at the hub of the wheel, the thing the network had been built to contain.

The coordinates resolved.

Ben copied them onto his datapad, then transferred the route data onto the Steady Returns nav computer through the coupler's uplink. The transfer took less than thirty seconds. The destination resolved on the display a moment later.

Deep space. Uncharted. No hyperspace lane, no Republic survey, no record of any settlement or installation or navigational hazard in any modern database.

Nothing.

Something had been deliberately hidden there.

Ben stared at the display. The pressure in his chest was a physical thing now, a compression under the sternum that made his ribs feel fractionally too tight, and the Force in the data core room had the quality of a recording — not a presence, not a warning, just the walls remembering who had walked these corridors and what they had been afraid of.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet. "We need to talk about what we do next."

"I know."

"This isn't a survey site anymore. This is a military installation with an active containment system that's been failing for a thousand years. The protocol is clear. We report this, we wait for backup, and we let the Order decide how to handle it."

Ben didn't answer.

"Ben."

"I heard you."

The star map rotated slowly in the cold air. The perimeter installations pulsed their steady blue. The central marker at the hub of the wheel waited, patient and silent, for someone to find it.

Ben copied the route a second time. Redundancy. Habit.

The weight of the decision had not arrived yet — not fully — but he could feel it forming, the shape of something he was going to have to carry whether he wanted to or not. The warning was still cycling on the displays behind him. The name on the authorization logs was still waiting for someone to remember it. And the coordinates on his nav computer pointed to a place that did not exist on any map, where something had been hidden a thousand years ago by people who had been afraid enough to build a dozen fortresses around it.

The wrongness was not ambient anymore.

It was directional.
