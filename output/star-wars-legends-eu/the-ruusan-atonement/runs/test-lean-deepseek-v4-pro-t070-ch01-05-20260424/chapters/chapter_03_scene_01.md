The third world was nothing like the first two.

Where the survey stops had been dry hardpan and wet loam, this one was bare rock and thin atmosphere, the sky a pale wash of violet that made the stars visible even in daylight. The *Steady Returns* had set down on a flat shelf of stone half a kilometer from the structure, her landing struts grating against the bedrock, and the moment the ramp lowered Ben felt the wrongness settle onto his chest like a weight someone had laid there.

It was denser here. Not ambient. Not diffuse. Close and specific, a pressure under the sternum that made his ribs feel fractionally too tight.

Desh stepped off the ramp behind him and stopped. "That's not ruins."

No. It wasn't.

The structure rose from the barren rock in clean lines — a low, angular building of dark stone and durasteel alloy, partially buried in the hillside, its entrance a recessed archway that faced the landing site like an eye. No collapsed walls. No weather-eaten foundations. The stone was intact, the alloy unrusted, and at the base of the archway a faint blue light pulsed in a rhythm Ben could feel in his back teeth.

The facility had power.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet. "How old did you say these sites were?"

"I didn't."

"Guess."

Ben didn't answer. He was already walking.

The terrain between the landing site and the structure was open — no cover, no elevation, just a long slope of wind-scoured rock that left them exposed the whole way. Ben kept his hand near his blaster. Beside him, Desh had unsnapped the retention strap on his holster without seeming to notice he'd done it. Old habits. The kind that kept you alive when the architecture stopped making sense.

The wrongness thickened as they approached. The pressure under Ben's sternum deepened, a slow compression that made his breathing shallow, and his Force-sense — the part of him that was always reaching, always reading — was pushing against something it couldn't get a grip on. The facility was there. He could see it. He could feel the power cells humming at the edge of his perception, a low-frequency vibration that traveled up through the soles of his boots. But the Force around it was wrong in a way he didn't have words for. Not missing. Not dead. Just — elsewhere. As if the structure occupied a space his senses couldn't quite map onto the space his eyes were showing him.

He stopped ten meters from the entrance.

The archway was three meters high, the stone cut with the same angular precision as the markings on the first two worlds. Above the entrance, a single line of script ran the width of the lintel — the same unfamiliar characters, the same parallel tool-grooves, the same hand that had carved warnings into stone on two different worlds and left them for someone to find.

The blue light pulsed.

"Desh."

"I see it."

"Any reading on what's powering it?"

Desh pulled the scanner from his belt and checked the display. His face didn't change, but Ben had known him long enough to read the stillness in his shoulders. "Nothing I can identify. The power signature doesn't match any known reactor type. It's not fission, not fusion, not hypermatter." He tapped the display. "The cells are drawing ambient energy from something, but the scanner can't resolve what. Whatever's running this place, it's been running for a long time."

"How long?"

"Long enough that it shouldn't still be running."

Ben stepped forward.

The blue light flared.

It came from the stone itself — a pulse of cold blue that crawled out of the walls and washed over him like a wave, starting at his feet and rising to his crown in a single smooth motion. Ben felt it as much as saw it: a pressure against his skin that wasn't physical, a presence reading him the way he read a room, layer by layer, checking something that didn't quite match. The pulse lingered at his chest for a half-second longer than it had anywhere else. Then it reached his head, and something in his lineage — something older than his name, older than his father's name — answered.

The archway went dark.

Then the doors opened.

They didn't grind. Didn't shudder. Two panels of dark alloy slid apart with a sound like a breath released, and the air that came out of the facility was cold and dry and carried the faint metallic tang of recycled atmosphere. The corridor beyond was lit by strips of pale blue phosphorescence set into the ceiling, and at the far end of it, thirty meters in, Ben could see the glow of active displays.

Desh drew his sidearm.

"I don't like this."

"Neither do I."

"Are we going in anyway?"

Ben stepped through the archway.

The corridor was wider than it had looked from outside — wide enough for three people to walk abreast, the walls lined with the same dark alloy, the ceiling high enough that the phosphorescent strips cast the whole space in a flat, even light. The floor was stone, worn smooth by centuries of foot traffic that had stopped a thousand years ago. Ben's boots made no sound. The wrongness was a stone on his chest now, heavy and specific, and his Force-sense was telling him something he couldn't parse — that the facility was awake, that it had been awake for a long time, that it had been waiting.

The displays came into view as they rounded the curve of the corridor.

They were set into a console bank that ran the length of the far wall — old-style holographic projectors, the kind that rendered data in three dimensions instead of flat on a screen, their crystals clouded with age but still functional. As Ben approached, the nearest display flickered and resolved into a cascade of characters: the same unfamiliar script, scrolling too fast to read, cycling through what looked like status reports and system diagnostics and something that might have been a countdown.

"Desh."

"I'm looking."

"What do you see?"

Desh moved past him, sidearm still out, his eyes sweeping the console the way they'd sweep a room. "Military command-and-control. Old architecture — pre-Ruusan, if I'm reading the layout right. This isn't a temple. It's a command post." He pointed at the console's edge, where the alloy was scored with a pattern of parallel lines. "See those? Access terminals for tactical displays. Someone ran a war from this room."

Ben touched the console.

The display nearest his hand shifted. The scrolling characters slowed, stuttered, and then resolved into something his translation software could parse — a warning message, cycling through a sequence of dead frequencies, repeating the same two words in archaic Basic over and over again.

The pressure on his chest became a weight that pinned him to the moment.

He read the words.

He did not name what they protected against. The meaning hadn't reached him yet. Only the weight of them, the way they landed in his chest like a second heartbeat, the way the wrongness that had been following him since Coruscant suddenly had a name he wasn't ready to say.

SEAL FAILING.

The message repeated. SEAL FAILING. SEAL FAILING.

Desh's voice came from somewhere behind him, flat and professional and too calm. "Ben. What does it say."

Ben didn't answer. He was standing in front of something the modern Jedi Order did not know existed, and the warning was still cycling, and the wrongness was not ambient anymore.

It was the sound of something breaking.
