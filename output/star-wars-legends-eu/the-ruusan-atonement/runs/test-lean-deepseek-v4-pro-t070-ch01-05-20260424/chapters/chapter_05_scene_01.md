Ben pressed his palm to the release.

The stasis field collapsed with a sound that was not quite a hiss — it was the sound of held breath finally let go, a pressure equalizing across a threshold that had been sealed for longer than the Republic had existed. The blue light flickered once and died, and the chamber's interior resolved into shadow and stone and the outline of a woman who had not moved in a thousand years.

She opened her eyes.

Ben had time to register the color — grey, pale as winter light — before she moved. Not the slow, tentative movement of someone shaking off disorientation. She dropped into a combat crouch in the space of a heartbeat, weight centered, hands coming up into a guard position that Ben did not recognize but understood immediately. Her body had decided she was in danger before her mind had finished waking.

Her Force presence hit him a half-second later — sharp and disciplined, the way a blade is disciplined, shaped by training that was older than the Temple on Coruscant. It was not the diffuse warmth of a modern Jedi's signature. It was focused. Deliberate. A thing that had been honed for war and never unlearned.

Ben did not move. He kept his hands at his sides, open, visible. Behind him, he heard Desh shift his weight — not reaching for a weapon, but ready to.

"Easy," Ben said.

The woman's eyes tracked across him, across Desh, across the ring of chambers and the nine dark alcoves and the emergency lighting running along the floor in strips the color of old bruises. Her breathing was controlled — too controlled for someone who had just woken from a millennium of stasis. She was cataloguing. Prioritizing. Doing exactly what Ben would have done.

"Identify yourself." Her voice was hoarse from disuse, but the command structure was intact. Complete sentences. Formal address. The register of someone who had given orders and expected them to be followed.

"Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight."

Her expression did not change. "You are Jedi-trained." It was not a question. "You are not of my era."

The second chamber's field collapsed before Ben could answer.

The man who emerged was taller than the woman, broader through the shoulders, and he staggered — caught himself on the doorframe with one hand, his other pressing flat against the stone as if he needed to confirm it was real. His presence arrived differently. Where the woman's was sharp, his was heavy — warm in a way that felt settled, as if it had been in one place for so long it had become part of the architecture. Ben felt it as weight, as pressure, as something he did not have a name for.

The man straightened. He was older than the woman — mid-forties, maybe, though the stasis made age difficult to read — and his face was the kind of face that had been handsome before it had been tired. He looked at Ben, then at the woman, and something passed between them that Ben could not read. A thousand years of shared history compressed into a glance.

"Well," the man said. His voice was rougher than the woman's, but the courtesy in it was archaic — the kind of courtesy that had been old when the Republic was young. "This is not where I expected to wake."

The third chamber's field collapsed.

The man who stepped out did not stagger. He did not crouch. He put his back to the wall and scanned the room in a single motion — exits, threats, sightlines — and then he went still. His presence was clipped. Guarded. Trained in a register Ben had not encountered before, something that had learned to make itself small and had not remembered how to stop.

He said nothing.

The silence stretched. The woman — Sera, Ben knew without knowing how he knew, the name arriving the way the wrongness had arrived in the entry — was still watching him with that grey, assessing stare. The tall man had found his balance and was standing with his weight on his heels, his hands loose at his sides, his presence filling the room without trying. The third man had not moved from the wall.

"What year is it?" Sera asked.

Ben told her.

The number landed in the room like a physical thing. Sera's expression did not change, but something behind her eyes went very still — the stillness of someone doing arithmetic they did not want to do. The tall man exhaled slowly, a breath that was almost a laugh and was not a laugh at all. The third man's gaze flicked to the dark chambers, one after another, counting.

Ben felt the weight of the answer settle into his own chest. He had just told three people that everyone they had ever known was dead. That the war they had fought was a thousand years gone. That the galaxy had moved on without them.

"I am Sera Varik," the woman said. She straightened out of her crouch, but the readiness did not leave her. "Lieutenant of Gavran's circle. You will tell me what has become of the fortress."

"The fortress is failing." Ben kept his voice level. "The containment is degrading. We came because the seal is breaking."

"You came." Her tone was flat. "Two of you."

"The rest of the chambers are dark," the tall man said. He was looking at the ring now, at the nine dead alcoves and the one that was not dark and not lit. "Nine dead. One broken." He turned back to Ben, and his expression was still warm, but the warmth had acquired an edge. "Who got out?"

Torin. The name surfaced the same way Sera's had — not recognition, but something deeper, something the Force was carrying that Ben's conscious mind had not yet processed.

"We don't know," Ben said. "The breach was logged twenty-three days ago. The emergency protocols triggered. No one responded."

"Twenty-three days." Sera's voice was precise. Controlled. "Someone has been walking for twenty-three days."

"And we have been sealed," Torin said. He looked at the dark chambers again, and something moved in his face — not grief, not yet, but the shape grief would fill when there was time. "Nine of us did not wake."

The third man had still not spoken. His back was against the wall and his eyes were on the exits and his presence was a closed door. Ben could feel the effort it took to maintain that stillness — the discipline of someone who had learned to make silence into armor.

"Your name," Ben said.

The man's gaze shifted to him. The pause was long enough that Ben thought he might not answer.

"Kael Drenn."

His voice was quieter than Ben had expected. Not hoarse, like Sera's — quiet by nature, the voice of someone who had never needed to be heard to be present.

Desh moved up beside Ben. His expression was the operational one — the one he used when he was filing facts he did not like. "We should move. The fortress is not stable, and whatever broke out of that chamber has a three-week head start."

Sera's attention shifted to him. "You are not Jedi."

"No."

"Then you are support staff."

Desh's mouth twitched. "I am the man with the ship."

Sera held his gaze for a moment, then nodded once — the kind of nod that was not agreement but acknowledgment. She turned back to Ben. "You woke us. That makes you responsible for us. Do you understand what that means?"

Ben understood. He had understood the moment his hand touched the release. Three people a thousand years out of time, with no resources, no allies, and no understanding of the galaxy they had woken into. Whatever came next, he had chosen it.

"Yes," he said.

Torin was the last to orient. He had moved to the center of the ring, his head turning slowly as he took in the full circumference of the chamber — the nine dark alcoves, the three that had held, the one that was broken from the inside. His presence was still that warm, settled weight, but something in it had shifted. Ben could feel him processing, the same way Sera had processed the date, the same way Kael was processing from his place against the wall.

Torin looked at the empty chamber. The scoring on the walls. The emergency lights still blinking amber, amber, amber.

"Who got out?" he said quietly.
