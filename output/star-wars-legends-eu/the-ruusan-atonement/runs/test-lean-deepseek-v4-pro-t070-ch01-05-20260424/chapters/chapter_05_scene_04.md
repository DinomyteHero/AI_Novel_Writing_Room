The command table on Level 1 was built for a different kind of war.

Ben spread the fortress data across its surface—Corridor maps rendered in the same amber light as the archive consoles, seal readings that pulsed with the wrongness he still could not name, and the chamber file. That one sat to the side, its display showing a name-shaped blank where the occupant's designation should have been. The translation protocols had chewed through the corrupted entries and found nothing. Not a name. Not a species marker. Not even a rank.

Absence, Ben thought. The wrongness had a shape now, and the shape was absence.

He did not say it out loud. The word would have made it real in a way he was not ready for.

Sera and Torin worked through the archive materials on the secondary console. Ben watched them without turning—registered the rhythm of their hands, the way Torin passed datacards to Sera without looking, the way Sera received them without acknowledgment. Muscle memory. A thousand years old and still running.

Torin's arrangement carried warmth. He grouped materials by narrative—here was the sequence of events, here was the context, here was what you needed to understand before the next piece made sense. A storyteller's instinct. Sera's arrangement carried precision. She sorted by tactical priority, by threat level, by what would kill them first if they got it wrong. No context. No narrative. Just the order of operations.

Ben filed the difference and said nothing.

His hands tightened on the edge of the command table. The Force-sense around him was still deadened—not absent, but unreliable, like trying to read a map through fogged transparisteel. He could feel the others in the room if he reached. Sera, a blade held perfectly still. Torin, a warmth that should have been reassuring and was not, quite. Kael, a closed door. Desh, the steady absence of someone who did not need the Force to be the most dangerous person in the room.

And the wrongness. Spreading outward from the fortress, diffusing through the wider sky. Present. Patient. Waiting.

"Desh."

Desh looked up from the tertiary console. He had been running his own analysis—the physical evidence from the broken chamber, the supply manifest, the pattern of the accessed terminals. His expression was the same hard assessment it had been since they found the empty chamber.

"The monitoring packets," Ben said. "The corrupted ones. Can you reconstruct them?"

"Most of them." Desh's voice was the operational register—the one he used when he was filing facts he did not like and was not going to discuss until there was time. "The corruption's concentrated on the most recent entries. Someone wanted those buried. But the response-quadrant data's intact underneath—the system was still logging even when the logs were being chewed through. Give me until morning."

"Morning."

"First light. The name will resolve."

The name. Ben heard the word land and felt something in his chest tighten—not quite relief, not quite dread. The thing that had escaped was not a mystery to be solved in this chamber. It was a moving target, already weeks ahead, already doing whatever it had spent centuries planning to do. The name would tell them who they were chasing. It would not tell them how to close the gap.

Kael spoke from the wall. "You are assuming pursuit."

Ben turned. Kael had not moved from his position near the door—the same closed-door stillness he had carried since waking, the same watchful distance. His arms were crossed. His voice was the dry register Ben was beginning to recognize as his default: observation delivered without inflection, a statement that was also a question.

"I am assuming we cannot stay here," Ben said.

"Those are not the same thing."

"They are right now."

Kael held his gaze for a beat. Then he nodded, once, and looked away. Not agreement. Acknowledgment. The distinction mattered, and Ben filed it next to everything else he was filing.

Sera set the last datacard into its sequence and straightened. Her hands fell to her sides with the precision of someone who had finished a task and was already calculating the next one. "The archive is organized for transit. Tactical priority only—the rest can wait for a return expedition." She did not say *if* there was a return expedition. The omission was deliberate. "Torin has flagged the Corridor node data. The first waypoint is three days' travel at sublight. The hyperlane access point is beyond that."

"We cannot make three days at sublight with five people aboard the *Steady Returns*," Desh said. "Not comfortably."

"We are not doing this comfortably."

Desh looked at Ben. The look said what Desh did not say out loud: *you are making a decision you have not told us about yet.*

Ben pulled his datapad from his belt.

The report to Luke should have been easy. He had written a hundred reports to his father—mission summaries, threat assessments, requests for additional resources. The format was muscle memory. The content was not.

He typed: *Survey complete. Uncharted world confirmed as pre-Ruusan Jedi installation. Anomalous Force readings consistent with degraded containment infrastructure. Continuing investigation along the Corridor. Will update when I have more.*

He stopped. His thumb hovered over the send key.

The report did not mention the survivors. It did not mention the seal. It did not mention the empty chamber, the broken door, the escapee with two months of head start and a name that would not resolve until morning. It did not mention the Reformations. It did not mention what Sera had said in the archive—*the Order does not know what it was built to bury*—or the way the words had landed in Ben's chest and stayed there.

It did not say *help*.

Every instinct Ben had trained told him to pick up the comm and call Luke in full. Tell him everything. Ask for a strike team, a research detail, the full weight of the Order behind him. That was the protocol. That was the responsible thing. That was what a Jedi Knight was supposed to do when he found something this big.

And every calculation he did not quite permit himself to finish told him that the Order's response time would cost more than the survivors had. That a fuller report would pull the mission out of his hands. That Luke would listen, and assess, and decide—and by the time the decision came back, the seal would have degraded further, the escapee would have gained another week, and the survivors would be someone else's problem.

Ben did not want them to be someone else's problem.

He did not examine that thought. He pressed send.

The datapad chimed once. Transmission confirmed.

Desh was watching him. Sera was watching him. Torin's hands had stilled on the console, and Kael's gaze had drifted back from the wall. The room was quiet in a way that was not silence—a held breath, a question nobody was asking out loud.

Ben put the datapad away.

"We lift in ten minutes," he said. "Pack what you need."

The *Steady Returns* strained off the plateau.

Ben felt it through the deck plates—the engines working harder than they were designed to, the inertial compensators struggling to smooth the climb. Five people in a ship built for two. The YT-series could handle it, barely, but the handling was not the point. The point was the weight.

He stood at the cockpit viewport and watched the fortress shrink below them. The mountain peak that had hidden it for a thousand years. The plateau where he had landed three days ago with a survey mission and a question he had not known how to ask. The wrongness was still there—diffused through the wider sky now, spread thin across the atmosphere, present but no longer concentrated. It would follow them. He knew that without knowing how he knew it.

Behind him, the survivors were finding places to sit in a ship that had never been meant to carry passengers. Sera had taken the copilot's station—not touching anything, but close enough to watch. Torin had folded himself into the jump seat behind her, his knees nearly touching his chest, his expression the same easy warmth he had worn since waking. Kael had found a corner in the crew compartment and made himself still. Desh was already running the first reconstruction pass on the monitoring packets, his hands moving across the secondary console with the efficiency of someone who had done this work before, in worse conditions, for higher stakes.

The *Steady Returns* cleared the atmosphere. The stars steadied. The Corridor stretched ahead of them—a thread of navigable space marked on Ben's nav display, the first waypoint already plotted.

Somewhere ahead, an unnamed anchor was moving. Somewhere behind, the Order did not know what Ben knew. And somewhere in the archive lists Desh was still compiling, the monitoring packets were resolving, byte by byte, toward a name.

Ben did not feel heroic. He did not feel triumphant. He felt braced.

The pattern had closed before he named it. The choice was already made.

The *Steady Returns* climbed toward the Corridor, and Ben let it.
