The *Steady Returns* had set down on a flat stretch of hardpan two klicks from the ruins, kicking up a cloud of reddish dust that took its time settling. By the time Ben and Desh had the ramp down, the dust had coated everything — the landing struts, the sensor package, the inside of Ben's mouth.

Desh spat into the gravel. "Charming."

"It grows on you."

"Like a fungus."

The terrain between the ship and the ruins was the kind of terrain that looked flat from orbit and turned out to be anything but. Loose scree over hard-packed clay, the occasional spine of darker rock jutting up at angles that suggested geological violence somewhere in the deep past. The wind came from the east, dry and steady, carrying a mineral grit that worked its way into Ben's collar and the creases of his boots. Overhead, the sky was a washed-out blue, and a single contrail — faint, high, probably a supply freighter on a Rimward run — cut across it like a scar that hadn't finished healing.

Ben walked with his datapad out, the scanner running passive. The readout was clean. No power signatures. No comm traffic. No evidence of settlement within fifty klicks in any direction. The world had been surveyed sixty years ago and recorded as uninhabited, and the survey had been right about that part.

It had been wrong about the ruins.

They came into view as Ben and Desh crested a low ridge: a sprawl of stone structures half-buried in wind-erosion, their outlines softened by centuries of grit and weather but still unmistakably artificial. Rectilinear foundations. Collapsed archways. A central structure that had probably been a hall of some kind, its roof long gone, its walls reduced to knee-high stubs that caught the wind and made it whistle.

Desh stopped at the top of the ridge and let out a low whistle of his own. "Sixty-year survey missed this?"

"Apparently."

"That's not a small miss. That's a whole complex."

Ben was already moving down the slope, his boots skidding on the loose rock. The wrongness had been a background pressure since they'd entered the system — the same directional pull he'd been feeling since Coruscant, the same compass-needle that had kicked them out of hyperspace — but it was sharper here. More specific. The way a sound you'd been hearing for hours would suddenly resolve into a voice when you got close enough to the source.

He didn't say that. He kept walking.

The ruins were older up close. The erosion patterns on the exposed stone suggested centuries of wind-scour, possibly longer, and the construction style didn't match anything Ben had studied in the Temple archives. The foundations were too deep for a temporary settlement. The stonework was too precise for a pre-industrial culture. Someone had cut these blocks with tools that left clean edges, and someone had laid them with an attention to load-bearing that suggested military engineering rather than civilian habitation.

Desh caught up with him at the base of the central structure. His blaster was still holstered, but his hand was resting on the grip — not tension, just habit. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stop talking and start cataloging. It's the same thing you did on the approach to Coruscant, right before you told me the power coupling was fine when it wasn't."

"The power coupling was fine. It was the chrono that was drifting."

"Uh-huh." Desh scuffed a boot against one of the foundation stones. "And this?"

Ben crouched. The stone at his feet had been cut with a tool that left parallel grooves — not the smooth ablation of a modern cutter, not the rough chipping of a primitive chisel. Something in between. Something he didn't recognize.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the problem."

The markings were on the interior face of the central structure's north wall — the only wall still standing above waist height. They were carved into the stone in columns, the characters angular and precise, and they didn't match any script Ben had ever seen. Not the graceful curves of Old Corellian. Not the blocky formalism of High Galactic. Not any of the pre-Republic variants the Temple archive had on file.

He reached out before he'd decided to reach out. His fingers brushed the cut.

The wrongness hit him like a physical blow.

It came up through his arm as a low vibration — a frequency that had no source in the stone or the wind or the ground beneath his boots. His hand was touching the marking, and then his hand was six inches from the marking, and then it was touching it again, and none of those distances felt correct. The Force was present — he could feel it, the way he could always feel it — but it was *wrong* here, the way a note played slightly flat was wrong, the way a handrail you reached for in the dark was wrong when your hand closed on empty air six inches before you expected the metal.

His body recoiled. His mind caught up a half-second later.

"Ben."

He was on his feet. He didn't remember standing. His hand was at his side, the fingers tingling, and his heart was beating faster than the hike justified.

"Ben." Desh's voice was sharper now. "What was that?"

Ben looked at the marking. The stone was just stone. The wind was just wind. The wrongness was still there — a pressure against his perception, a vibration in the space behind his ribs — but it had settled back into the background, the way it had been since Coruscant.

"Nothing," he said.

"That wasn't nothing. You touched the rock and you jumped like it bit you."

"Static discharge. Dry air."

Desh looked at him. The look said he didn't believe a word of it, and the look also said he wasn't going to push, because pushing was for people who hadn't served together in something the Order had dissolved.

"Static discharge," Desh repeated.

"Mm."

"Fine. Keep your secrets." He turned away, scanning the horizon with the practiced sweep of someone who'd learned to watch for threats in empty places. "I'll check the perimeter. You do your Jedi thing."

Ben waited until Desh's boots had crunched out of earshot before he pulled out his datapad.

The scanner captured the markings in high resolution — the angular characters, the parallel tool-grooves, the erosion patterns that suggested a minimum age of eight hundred years and probably more. He added notes on the foundation depth, the construction style, the geological context. He estimated the age based on the wind-scour and flagged the script as unidentified. He did not record what his Force-sense had told him.

The omission was deliberate. He knew it was deliberate before he'd finished typing the first entry, and he didn't stop to examine why. His hands moved through the catalog with professional precision — shapes, measurements, context, hypotheses — and the wrongness stayed where it was, a vibration without a source, a note played flat, a handrail that wasn't where his body expected it to be.

He transmitted the report to Luke before he could second-guess the decision.

The data packet was clean. Markings, age estimates, geological notes. A standard survey report, the kind a Jedi Knight filed a dozen times a year when the Order needed eyes on something in the Outer Rim. Technically accurate. Structurally complete.

Except for the thing he'd left out.

Ben stood in the ruins with the wind blowing grit into his collar and the wrongness pressing against his perception like a second gravity, and he waited for the transmission to clear. The datapad chimed. The packet was away. His father would read it within the hour, and he would see the markings and the age estimates and the geological context, and he would not see the vibration or the fractional error or the way the Force had felt wrong the moment Ben's fingers touched the stone.

The wind picked up. The contrail overhead had faded to nothing. Desh was a hundred meters away, walking the perimeter with his blaster still holstered and his head moving in slow arcs, the way it always moved when he was pretending not to be concerned.

Ben looked at the marking one more time. The stone was just stone. The cut was just a cut.

He turned his back on it and started walking toward the ship.
