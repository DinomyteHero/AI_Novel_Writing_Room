# Chapter 1

## Scene 01.01

Ben's guard dropped.

It was a half-second late — the kind of mistake a Knight didn't make, not in a training salle, not against Rendell, not in the third exchange of a form Ben had been running since he was fifteen. The training blade caught him across the shoulder with a sharp *crack* and the salle's ambient light flickered blue against the walls.

Ben stepped back, saber still humming, and registered the hit the way he'd been trained to: clean failure, own it, move on. His shoulder stung. The wrongness pulled at his attention like a note played slightly flat — there and gone, a half-beat lag in the ambient field that had been tugging at the edges of his perception all week.

Rendell deactivated his blade. "You all right?"

Third time this week. The first had been in solo practice — easy to dismiss. The second had been against a training remote Ben had been beating since he was a Padawan — embarrassing but private. This was neither.

"Fine." Ben thumbed his own saber off and rolled his shoulder. "Late night. Let's call it."

Rendell hesitated. He was a solid Knight, a few years older than Ben, the kind of sparring partner who pushed hard enough to be useful without turning every session into a statement. He didn't press. He also didn't look convinced.

"Sure." He clipped his saber to his belt. "Same time next week?"

"Count on it."

Ben kept his tone light — the specific register of a man who'd been tired all week and wasn't going to make it anyone else's problem. It was almost true. He *had* been tired all week. The wrongness had been costing him sleep, sharpening his attention at odd hours, pulling his focus sideways during meditation until he'd stopped trying to meditate at all. Fatigue was a reason Rendell would accept. Fatigue was normal.

The wrongness was not.

Rendell gave him a nod and headed for the door. His boots echoed on the salle floor — a clean, professional sound, a Knight leaving a training session at the end of a long shift. The door slid shut behind him.

Ben stood alone in the empty salle.

The Temple at this hour was quiet but not silent. Somewhere down the corridor a cleaning droid hummed past, its motivator a low whine against the stone. The turbolift chimed — someone heading up from the archives, probably, or a late-shift Knight catching a meal before the refectory closed. Coruscant's night cycle pressed against the Temple's walls, a city that never stopped breathing, and the salle held the residue of a dozen earlier sessions — sweat and ozone and the faint scorched-durasteel smell of training blades.

Ben didn't reignite his saber.

He moved instead to the center of the floor and settled into the opening stance of Form III — feet set, weight balanced, empty hand extended as though the hilt were still in his grip. He'd run this sequence a thousand times. His body knew it the way it knew how to breathe.

He began.

The first movement was clean. The second, precise. His body flowed through the form without the blade's weight, without the hum, without anything but muscle memory and the cool recycled air of the salle. He let his attention drift outward, past his body, past the floor, into the ambient field that saturated the Temple — the living presence of the Force as it moved through the stone and the night and the city beyond.

And there it was.

The lag.

It arrived between the third movement and the fourth — a half-beat where the Force should have been present and wasn't, a note that arrived late, a rhythm his body had known by reflex for years suddenly missing its downbeat. His foot landed. His arm completed the block. But the field hadn't been there to meet him. It had come after.

Ben stopped moving.

He stood in the center of the salle, breathing hard, and felt the wrongness settle into his chest like a tightening he couldn't shake. Not fatigue. Not a bad week. Not the residue of Abeloth or the Vong War or any of the other crises the Order had been attributing the background disturbance to.

Something was wrong with the Force.

He didn't articulate it. He didn't have the shape of it yet — couldn't have put it into words if Rendell had walked back through the door and asked. But his body knew. His body had been telling him all week, and now it had told him in front of a witness, and the only thing worse than knowing something was wrong was knowing it alone.

The cleaning droid hummed past again in the corridor. The turbolift chimed. The Temple went about its night, indifferent.

Ben let his empty hand drop to his side.

The door slid open.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben had seen a dozen times before — the specific look of a Jedi Master who had a mission and had already decided who was taking it. He was dressed simply, no robes, just the tunic and belt of a man who'd been working late and hadn't bothered to change. His presence in the Force was steady and warm, the way it always was, and Ben felt the wrongness recede slightly — not gone, but pushed back by the sheer familiar weight of his father's attention.

"Ben."

"Luke."

Luke glanced at the salle — the empty floor, the unlit training sabers on the rack, the hour. "Still training?"

"Done, actually." Ben kept his voice even. "Rendell left a few minutes ago."

Luke nodded. He didn't ask how the session had gone. He didn't ask why Ben was standing alone in an empty salle with no blade ignited. He just held up the datapad.

"I need you for a survey run."

Ben felt something shift in his chest — relief and reluctance tangled together, the hope that he might not be alone with this and the cost of what it would take to say so. He didn't say so. Not yet.

"What kind of survey?"

"The kind that leaves tonight." Luke stepped into the salle and handed him the datapad. "There's a route I want you to look at. Outer Rim. Nothing urgent, but I'd rather have your eyes on it than someone else's."

Ben took the datapad. The screen was already lit — a star chart, a plotted course, a bearing that ran out toward the Rim and kept going.

The wrongness pulled at his attention.

It wasn't ambient anymore. It had a direction.

## Scene 01.02

Luke's office smelled of old flimsi and the faint ozone tang of a holocomm left on too long. The door had been open when Ben arrived — it was almost always open — and Luke was already behind the desk, a datapad glowing blue in front of him, his presence in the Force the same steady warmth it had been in the salle ten minutes ago.

"Close the door," Luke said.

Ben did. The click of the latch cut the corridor noise — a droid's motivator, someone's boots on stone, the distant chime of the turbolift — and left them alone with the low wash of Coruscant traffic bleeding through the transparisteel window. Luke's office was functional, not ceremonial. A desk that had seen decades of briefings. A chair Ben had sat in since he was old enough to receive missions instead of homework. A holocomm console with a hairline crack in the casing that nobody had bothered to replace.

Luke gestured at the chair. Ben took it.

"I have a survey route I want you to run." Luke slid the datapad across the desk. "Outer Rim. Nothing urgent on the surface, but I'd rather have your eyes on it than someone else's."

Ben caught the pad before it reached the edge. The screen was already lit — a star chart, a plotted course, a bearing that ran out toward the Rim and kept going. Seventeen flagged reports clustered along the line like beads on a string. Anomalous Force readings. Instrumentation drift. Three separate survey teams had logged variations on the same note: *something off, something wrong, unable to identify source.*

The wrongness pulled at his attention. It wasn't ambient anymore. It had a direction.

He zoomed in on the first flagged report, then the fourth, then the twelfth. The data was thin — sensor logs, navigational anomalies, one pilot's notation that her astromech had reset itself three times in the same sector for no reason she could find. But the pattern was there, and it matched the sourness Ben had been carrying in his chest for weeks like a note played slightly flat.

"When did these come in?"

"The first one about six weeks ago." Luke's voice was even. "The most recent this morning. I've been sitting on them."

Ben looked up from the screen. His father's face was the same as it had been in the salle — the specific look of a Jedi Master who had a mission and had already decided who was taking it. But there was something else underneath it now. A pause that hadn't been there before.

"You've felt it too," Ben said.

It wasn't a question.

Luke set his hands on the desk. The gesture was deliberate — the way he placed them, the way he let the silence hold for a beat before he spoke. "I've felt something. A disturbance. It's been getting stronger."

"How long?"

"Weeks." Luke's gaze didn't waver. "The same as you, I imagine."

Ben heard the difference in his father's register. Luke was describing an anomaly — a Grand Master reading a pattern in survey data, correlating it with a background disturbance in the Force, making the kind of assessment that would eventually become a briefing for the Council. Clinical. Measured. The right words in the right order.

Ben wasn't measuring. He was recognizing a frequency he had lived inside since he was a boy — the half-beat lag, the missing handrail, the thing that had been wrong before he had words for it and was wrong now in a way that felt less like a disturbance and more like a wound.

His father saw the anomaly. Ben saw the damage.

The distinction mattered, and he couldn't name it.

He scrolled to the three readings that matched his own perception — the ones where the sourness was strongest, where the sensor drift had spiked, where the pilot's notation had shifted from *unable to identify* to *recommend further investigation.* His thumb hovered over the screen.

"These three," he said. "They're the same thing I've been reading."

Luke didn't ask what he meant by *reading.* He didn't ask how long Ben had been tracking it on his own, or why he hadn't said anything, or whether the sparring loss in the salle ten minutes ago had anything to do with the wrongness they were both now pretending to discuss as a routine survey assignment. He just nodded.

"That's why I want you on it."

Ben set the datapad down on the desk. The blue glow threw shadows across the flimsi stacks, the holocomm crack, the chair where he'd sat for a hundred briefings before this one. He could feel the weight of the mission settling into place — the route, the bearing, the seventeen reports that had been piling up on his father's desk while Ben had been losing sleep and losing sparring matches and telling himself it was fatigue.

It wasn't fatigue. Luke knew it wasn't fatigue. Neither of them was going to say so.

"I'll need a ship," Ben said.

"The *Steady Returns* is available. Desh Rolan's been running cargo out of the Temple docks for the past six months — he knows the ship, and he's available if you want a pilot."

Ben heard the offer underneath the offer. Luke wasn't assigning Desh. He was leaving the door open — a pilot, a partner, someone who wasn't a Jedi and wouldn't ask the kind of questions a Jedi would ask. Someone who had known Ben since the GAG, who had seen him at his worst and never mentioned it.

Ben didn't take the door.

"Desh is fine," he said. "I'll handle the survey."

Luke didn't push. He didn't ask if Ben wanted another Jedi, or if he was sure, or if running solo into a disturbance that had been costing him sleep for weeks was the kind of decision a Knight made when he was thinking clearly. He just looked at Ben across the desk with an expression that held warmth and distance in the same steady frame — the look of a father who trusted his son and a Grand Master who had just assigned a mission and a man who had lost enough people to know what it meant when someone walked out of a room alone.

"Leave tonight," Luke said. "The route's loaded. Desh can handle the nav."

Ben stood. The chair scraped against the stone — a small sound, the kind of sound a room absorbed without noticing. He didn't move toward the door.

He moved to the window.

Coruscant spread out below him in its endless vertical cascade — traffic lanes threading between towers, the distant glitter of a thousand speeders catching the last of the day's light, the city breathing its constant mechanical breath. The wrongness was still there, a faint sourness under the Temple's usual harmonics, but from the window it felt directional. It had a bearing. It had a destination.

Ben's shoulders settled into a readiness that was also a closing. He could feel his father's attention on his back — steady, patient, asking nothing.

"I'll send a report when I have something," Ben said.

"I know you will."

Ben turned from the window. Luke was still behind the desk, his hands still resting on the flimsi stacks, his presence in the Force still the same steady warmth it had been when Ben walked in. The datapad sat between them with its seventeen flagged reports and its plotted course and its bearing that ran out toward the Rim and kept going.

Neither of them mentioned a partner. Neither of them mentioned the sparring loss. Neither of them acknowledged what it meant that Ben was walking out of this room alone with a mission his father had been sitting on for six weeks and a disturbance neither of them had named out loud.

Ben nodded once — a Knight to his Grand Master, a son to his father — and left.

## Scene 01.03

The *Steady Returns* sat in its berth with the preflight board already green. Ben had run the checks twice — once because he was thorough, once because he was stalling — and now the cockpit smelled of recycled air and the faint copper tang of a power coupling that needed replacing before the next maintenance cycle. He'd noted it on the datapad. Desh would note it too, probably with a dry comment about Jedi and their tolerance for burnt wiring.

His thumb hovered over the crew manifest.

The Jedi partner roster sat at the top of his contacts — three Knights with survey experience, all available according to the Temple duty board, all capable of handling a routine Outer Rim run without asking the kind of questions Ben didn't want to answer. Rendell had done three surveys in the last year alone. Solid. Reliable. The kind of partner who'd file his own reports and not wonder why Ben was quiet.

Ben's thumb slid past the roster and opened his personal contacts.

The motion was smooth. Efficient. His hand moved the way it moved in the salle — before the brain caught up, before the weight of the choice registered. He tapped Desh's comm code and let the connection build while the preflight indicators blinked their steady green.

The hangar echoed behind him — a service tech's voice bouncing off durasteel, the whine of a repulsor sled, the distant clang of a docking clamp releasing. Temple hangar sounds. Familiar enough that he stopped hearing them until they were gone.

The comm crackled.

"You're calling late." Desh's voice came through tinny over the long-range relay, warm under the static. "Either you need a pilot or you've finally decided to return my hydrospanner."

"I need a pilot."

A pause. Ben could hear the shift in Desh's breathing — the half-second recalibration of someone who'd known him long enough to hear what he wasn't saying.

"Survey run," Ben said. "Outer Rim. Luke's got a route he wants eyes on."

"How bad?"

"I don't know yet. That's the problem."

Desh didn't ask why Ben wasn't calling a Jedi. He didn't ask who else was on the manifest, or whether the Council had signed off, or what kind of survey needed a Knight instead of a standard team. He just let the silence hold for a beat — the specific silence of someone who'd served in something the Order had dissolved, who knew what it meant to operate outside institutional channels without needing it explained.

"The *Returns* is still loaded with cargo from the last run," Desh said. "Give me twenty minutes to clear the manifest."

"Fine."

"Ben."

"Yeah."

Another pause. The static hissed. When Desh spoke again, his voice had shifted — still dry, still casual, but with something underneath it that Ben recognized and didn't name. "It's good to hear from you."

Ben's shoulders dropped slightly. He hadn't realized he'd been holding them tight. "You too."

The comm clicked off.

Ben sat in the cockpit with the preflight board glowing and the hangar noise filtering through the hull and the wrongness pulling at the edge of his perception like a note played slightly flat. It should have diminished when he left Luke's office. It hadn't. It should have faded when he walked through the hangar doors and started thinking about departure vectors instead of disturbances. It hadn't.

It was sharper now. More directional.

He ran the preflight checks a third time.

---

Desh arrived in eighteen minutes, not twenty, carrying a duffel and a datapad and the particular ease of someone who'd been running cargo out of Temple docks long enough that the hangar crew knew his name. He was older than Ben by a handful of years — late twenties, lean, with the kind of face that didn't register surprise because surprise was a luxury intelligence operatives learned to skip. He'd been a teenager in the GAG, same as Ben. He'd done things he didn't talk about, same as Ben. The difference was that Desh had walked away clean when the Guard dissolved and built a life that didn't require him to explain himself to anyone.

Ben respected that more than he knew how to say.

"You look tired," Desh said, dropping into the copilot's seat.

"I'm fine."

"Mm." Desh pulled up the nav display, his fingers moving across the console with the practiced efficiency of someone who'd flown this ship more recently than Ben had. "That's the bad kind of fine."

Ben didn't answer. He was watching the approach vector resolve on the nav screen — the plotted course Luke had loaded, the bearing that ran out toward the Rim and kept going. The wrongness pulled along the same line. He could feel it now, not as a general sourness but as a specific pressure, like a hand on his shoulder that he couldn't shake off.

"Route's loaded," Desh said. "Multiple jumps. We'll hit the Hydian Way first, then angle Rimward. You want to tell me what we're looking for, or should I guess?"

"Anomalous readings. Sensor drift. Seventeen flagged reports along the bearing."

"That's what the mission says. What are we actually looking for?"

Ben's jaw tightened. The wrongness pressed against his perception — not painful, not urgent, just *present* in a way it shouldn't be. Disturbances were supposed to be ambient. They were supposed to fade when you moved away from the source. This one was following him.

"I don't know," he said. "Something's wrong."

Desh looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned back to the nav console and started running the jump calculations without another word.

---

The *Steady Returns* lifted from the Temple hangar on repulsors, angled toward the orbital departure lane, and cleared the approach vector with the kind of smooth efficiency that came from a ship that had been maintained by someone who cared. Coruscant fell away beneath them — the endless vertical cascade of towers and traffic, the glitter of a thousand speeders catching the last of the day's light, the city breathing its constant mechanical breath.

Ben watched it recede on the aft display and felt the wrongness sharpen.

It should have been diminishing. The Temple was behind him, the Council chambers, the salle where he'd lost a sparring match to a Knight he should have beaten. The disturbance should have been fading into background noise, the way disturbances did when you put distance between yourself and the source.

It wasn't.

It was pulling.

He could feel it now as a direction — a specific bearing, a compass needle settling toward something he couldn't see. The sourness was still there, the wrongness, the note played slightly flat. But underneath it was something else. Something that felt less like damage and more like a signal.

His hands tightened on the controls.

"Jump in thirty seconds," Desh said. "You want to tell me why your knuckles are white?"

"I'm fine."

"That's twice you've said that. It wasn't true the first time."

The nav computer chimed. The jump coordinates locked in. Ben watched the countdown tick toward zero and felt the wrongness press against him like a second gravity — not pulling him back toward Coruscant, not pushing him away, just *there*, oriented, waiting.

The ship punched into hyperspace.

The stars stretched into lines and the cockpit filled with the blue-white swirl of the hyperspace tunnel and Ben's instruments flickered — once, twice, a brief stutter in the power coupling he'd noted on the preflight — and then steadied.

The wrongness didn't fade.

It didn't dissipate into the background hum of hyperspace travel the way every disturbance Ben had ever felt had done. It didn't muffle or blur or lose coherence. It sharpened instead, acquiring weight and direction, pulling toward a specific point along the bearing Luke had plotted — something waiting at the end of the route, something that had been calling to him for weeks without him knowing it.

Ben's breath caught.

The Force didn't work this way. Disturbances didn't follow you into hyperspace. They didn't acquire direction. They didn't *pull*.

But this one did.

He braced against the seat, his hands still on the controls, his eyes on the swirl of the tunnel ahead. The wrongness had stopped being ambient. It had stopped being a problem he could ignore. It was a compass now, and it was pointing toward something specific, and Ben was already flying straight toward it.

"Ben," Desh said quietly.

"I know."

"What is that?"

Ben didn't answer. He didn't have words for it — not yet, not in a way that would make sense to someone who couldn't feel the Force, not in a way that didn't sound like he was losing his grip. But he could feel it. The pressure. The direction. The wrongness settling into place like a needle finding north.

The *Steady Returns* flew on into the blue.

# Chapter 2

## Scene 02.01

The hyperspace reversion hit like a physical blow.

The *Steady Returns* shuddered out of the tunnel with a groan that ran through the deck plates and up into Ben's spine, and the caf he'd been holding — lukewarm, third cup of the transit, wedged against the copilot's console in a spare mug that had seen better decades — sloshed across the instrument panel in a brown wave.

"Kriff," Ben said, and meant it.

Desh's hands were already moving across the nav controls, steady despite the rattle. "That wasn't me."

"I didn't say it was."

"You were thinking it."

Ben grabbed the sleeve of his tunic and started mopping caf off the console before it could seep into anything important. The fabric soaked up liquid and left streaks across the status displays. The chrono flickered — once, twice — and then steadied. The nav readout cycled through a recalibration sequence that Ben had seen Desh run a hundred times and had never seen take this long.

The cockpit smelled like burnt caf and recycled air and the faint copper tang of that power coupling Ben had noted on the preflight. The hyperspace blue had vanished from the viewport, replaced by the starfield — unfamiliar stars, Rimward stars, the kind of stars that didn't show up on standard nav charts unless someone had paid for the update.

Desh tapped the sensor panel with two fingers. The motion was patient. The kind of patient that meant he'd already seen something he didn't like.

"Diagnostic says the jump coordinates were clean," he said. "Nav computer didn't burp. Power coupling's throwing the same fluctuation you flagged back at the Temple, but that's not what kicked us out."

"What did?"

"Good question." Desh pulled up the sensor log and let it scroll. Numbers Ben didn't need to read. "Chrono drift. Started about twenty minutes into the jump. Small at first — microseconds. Then it started accumulating."

Ben finished wiping the console and sat back. His sleeve was soaked. The caf had gotten into the seam between two panels, and he could smell it starting to burn off the housing. "Chrono drift doesn't kick a ship out of hyperspace."

"This one did." Desh highlighted a section of the log. "It's not random. Look at the bearing."

Ben looked. The drift wasn't scattered across the sensor array — it was clustering along a single vector, a narrow cone pointing Rimward and slightly spinward, the same direction the *Steady Returns* had been flying since they'd cleared the Hydian Way.

The same direction the wrongness had been pulling since Coruscant.

He didn't say that. He sat with his caf-soaked sleeve and the smell of burnt wiring and the pressure in his chest that had stopped being ambient somewhere between the Temple hangar and the first jump point and had started being directional instead. A compass needle. A hand on his shoulder. A thing he couldn't shake and couldn't name and couldn't put into a sentence that wouldn't sound like he was losing his grip.

"Ben."

"Mm."

"That's the third time the sensors have flagged that bearing since we left." Desh's voice was dry. Casual. The voice of someone who'd learned to deliver bad news without making it sound like bad news, because panic was a luxury intelligence operatives skipped. "First time it was a blip. Second time I thought it was the coupling. Third time, it's a pattern."

"Patterns have causes."

"This one has a bearing." Desh reset the sensor array and watched the drift re-form along the same vector. "It's not random noise. It's directional. Consistent. And it's getting stronger the further Rimward we go."

Ben's jaw tightened. He could feel the wrongness pressing against the edge of his perception — not pain, not urgency, just *presence*, the way a sound you'd stopped noticing would suddenly resolve into a voice. His body had been tracking it since Coruscant. His shoulders had been tight since the Temple hangar. His hands had been white on the controls since the jump to hyperspace, and he'd told Desh he was fine, and he'd said it twice, and neither time had been true.

"Same bearing as the flagged reports," Ben said. His voice came out level. Good. "The ones Luke loaded."

"Same bearing as the survey route." Desh turned in his seat. The copilot's chair creaked — old ship, old components, the kind of wear that came from a vessel that had carried people for a long time. "You want to tell me what we're actually looking for, or should I keep guessing?"

Ben didn't answer right away. He was watching the sensor display, the drift clustering along its vector, the chrono still flickering at the edge of his vision. The wrongness pulled along the same line. He could feel it now — not as a general sourness but as a specific pressure, a physical thing, the way a missing handrail felt when you reached for it in the dark and found nothing.

"I don't know," he said. "Something's wrong."

"That's what you said before we left."

"It's still true."

Desh looked at him for a long moment. The cockpit hummed around them — the environmental systems, the power coupling, the faint vibration of a ship that had just been yanked out of hyperspace by something its nav computer couldn't explain. Through the viewport, the starfield hung motionless. The wrongness pressed against Ben's perception like a second gravity.

"All right," Desh said. "Let me run the full diagnostic. We're not jumping again until I know what kicked us out."

"Fine."

"And you're going to sit there and not tell me why your knuckles are white."

Ben looked down at his hands. They were gripping the armrests. He hadn't noticed.

"That's the third time," Desh said. "Since we left. White knuckles."

"I'm fine."

"That's the fourth time you've said that. It wasn't true the first three."

The diagnostic chimed. The sensor log kept scrolling. Ben's caf had dried on the console in a brown stain that would probably be there for the rest of the run, because Desh never cleaned the cockpit and Ben was too tired to care. The wrongness pulled along the bearing — the same bearing, the one his body had been tracking since Coruscant, the one he'd been trying to ignore by staying in motion.

Desh tapped the sensor panel again. The drift re-formed. Same vector. Same intensity. Same quiet refusal to be anything other than what it was.

"It's got teeth," Desh said. His voice was still dry, still casual, but there was something underneath it now — the specific tone of someone who'd served in something the Order had dissolved, who knew what it meant to watch a problem get worse and not have the vocabulary to name it. "Whatever it is. It's not going to stop."

Ben's hands tightened on the armrests.

The nav display pinged. The first survey world had resolved on the long-range scanners — a small, unremarkable dot at the edge of the sensor cone, right along the bearing. The drift intensified. The chrono flickered again.

Ben didn't move. He sat with the pressure in his chest and the wrongness pulling toward the world on the scanners and the weight of something he couldn't name settling into place like a door closing behind him.

The bearing was the one he'd been carrying since Coruscant. The one he'd been trying to ignore. The one that had stopped being ambient and started being a vector, a destination, a thing that could not be outrun.

Desh was watching him. The sensor log was still scrolling. The first survey world hung on the display like a question Ben didn't know how to answer.

His hands tightened on the armrests.

## Scene 02.02

The *Steady Returns* had set down on a flat stretch of hardpan two klicks from the ruins, kicking up a cloud of reddish dust that took its time settling. By the time Ben and Desh had the ramp down, the dust had coated everything — the landing struts, the sensor package, the inside of Ben's mouth.

Desh spat into the gravel. "Charming."

"It grows on you."

"Like a fungus."

The terrain between the ship and the ruins was the kind of terrain that looked flat from orbit and turned out to be anything but. Loose scree over hard-packed clay, the occasional spine of darker rock jutting up at angles that suggested geological violence somewhere in the deep past. The wind came from the east, dry and steady, carrying a mineral grit that worked its way into Ben's collar and the creases of his boots. Overhead, the sky was a washed-out blue, and a single contrail — faint, high, probably a supply freighter on a Rimward run — cut across it like a scar that hadn't finished healing.

Ben walked with his datapad out, the scanner running passive. The readout was clean. No power signatures. No comm traffic. No evidence of settlement within fifty klicks in any direction. The world had been surveyed sixty years ago and recorded as uninhabited, and the survey had been right about that part.

It had been wrong about the ruins.

They came into view as Ben and Desh crested a low ridge: a sprawl of stone structures half-buried in wind-erosion, their outlines softened by centuries of grit and weather but still unmistakably artificial. Rectilinear foundations. Collapsed archways. A central structure that had probably been a hall of some kind, its roof long gone, its walls reduced to knee-high stubs that caught the wind and made it whistle.

Desh stopped at the top of the ridge and let out a low whistle of his own. "Sixty-year survey missed this?"

"Apparently."

"That's not a small miss. That's a whole complex."

Ben was already moving down the slope, his boots skidding on the loose rock. The wrongness had been a background pressure since they'd entered the system — the same directional pull he'd been feeling since Coruscant, the same compass-needle that had kicked them out of hyperspace — but it was sharper here. More specific. The way a sound you'd been hearing for hours would suddenly resolve into a voice when you got close enough to the source.

He didn't say that. He kept walking.

The ruins were older up close. The erosion patterns on the exposed stone suggested centuries of wind-scour, possibly longer, and the construction style didn't match anything Ben had studied in the Temple archives. The foundations were too deep for a temporary settlement. The stonework was too precise for a pre-industrial culture. Someone had cut these blocks with tools that left clean edges, and someone had laid them with an attention to load-bearing that suggested military engineering rather than civilian habitation.

Desh caught up with him at the base of the central structure. His blaster was still holstered, but his hand was resting on the grip — not tension, just habit. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stop talking and start cataloging. It's the same thing you did on the approach to Coruscant, right before you told me the power coupling was fine when it wasn't."

"The power coupling was fine. It was the chrono that was drifting."

"Uh-huh." Desh scuffed a boot against one of the foundation stones. "And this?"

Ben crouched. The stone at his feet had been cut with a tool that left parallel grooves — not the smooth ablation of a modern cutter, not the rough chipping of a primitive chisel. Something in between. Something he didn't recognize.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the problem."

The markings were on the interior face of the central structure's north wall — the only wall still standing above waist height. They were carved into the stone in columns, the characters angular and precise, and they didn't match any script Ben had ever seen. Not the graceful curves of Old Corellian. "Twenty-three days." Sera's voice was precise. Controlled. "Someone has been walking for twenty-three days."

"And we have been sealed," Torin said. He looked at the dark chambers again, and something moved in his face — not grief, not yet, but the shape grief would fill when there was time. "Nine of us did not wake."

The third man had still not spoken. His back was against the wall and his eyes were on the exits and his presence was a closed door. Ben could feel the effort it took to maintain that stillness — the discipline of someone who had learned to make silence into armor.

"Your name," Ben said.

The man's gaze shifted to him. The pause was long enough that Ben thought he might not answer.

"Kael Drenn."

His voice was quieter than Ben had expected. Not hoarse, like Sera's — quiet by nature, the voice of someone who had never needed to be heard to be present.

Desh moved up beside Ben. His expression was the operational one — the one he used when he was filing facts he did not like. "We should move. The fortress is not stable, and whatever broke out of that chamber has a three-week head start."

Sera's attention shifted to him. "You are not Jedi."

"No."

"Then you are support staff."

Desh's mouth twitched. "I am the man with the ship."

Sera held his gaze for a moment, then nodded once — the kind of nod that was not agreement but acknowledgment. She turned back to Ben. "You woke us. That makes you responsible for us. Do you understand what that means?"

Ben understood. He had understood the moment his hand touched the release. Three people a thousand years out of time, with no resources, no allies, and no understanding of the galaxy they had woken into. Whatever came next, he had chosen it.

"Yes," he said.

Torin was the last to orient. He had moved to the center of the ring, his head turning slowly as he took in the full circumference of the chamber — the nine dark alcoves, the three that had held, the one that was broken from the inside. His presence was still that warm, settled weight, but something in it had shifted. Ben could feel him processing, the same way Sera had processed the date, the same way Kael was processing from his place against the wall.

Torin looked at the empty chamber. The scoring on the walls. The emergency lights still blinking amber, amber, amber.

"Who got out?" he said quietly.

## Scene 05.02

The archive was on Level 2, behind a door that had not been opened in a thousand years.

Sera crossed to it without hesitation. Her stride was the same stride she had used in the stasis chamber — economical, deliberate, the gait of someone who had never learned to waste motion. Ben watched her hand find the access panel and stop there, hovering half a centimeter from the surface, and he understood that she was not reading the interface. She was remembering where it had been.

Her fingers moved.

The sequence was faster than Ben's modern training — not because she was faster than him, but because she was not thinking about it. Her hand knew the shape of the code the way a pilot's hand knew a throttle. The console woke with a sound that was not quite a hum, something lower and older, a frequency the fortress had not produced since before the Republic existed.

"Gavran's archive," Sera said. She did not look at Ben when she said it. She was already reading the display, her grey eyes tracking across data in a script Ben did not recognize. "It is still here."

Desh had stopped near the door. His expression was the operational one — the one Ben had seen a hundred times during GAG operations, the face of a man filing facts he did not like and was not going to discuss until there was time. "That console should not have power."

"The fortress is failing," Sera said. "It is not dead."

Torin moved past Ben before Ben could respond. His hand found the console's secondary display the same way Sera's had found the primary — from memory, from a thousand years of muscle that had not forgotten the shape of the equipment it had been trained on. The holographic interface flickered and resolved into a timeline, dates and names and institutional markers scrolling upward in a script that shifted as Ben watched, the archive's translation protocols adapting to the presence of a modern reader.

"There," Torin said. His voice was still rough from stasis, but the warmth in it was already settling into something recognizable — the register of a man who told stories because stories were how he made sense of things. "The Reformations. You will know this part."

Ben knew it. Every Jedi knew it. The Ruusan Reformations: the restructuring that had ended the Jedi Lords, dissolved the Army of Light, decentralized the Order into something that could not become a military power again. A thousand years of peace built on the lesson that the Jedi were not generals.

The timeline Torin had pulled up did not match the one Ben had been taught.

The names were the same. The dates were the same. But the causal chain — the sequence of decisions, the logic connecting one event to the next — was wrong. Or not wrong. Different. The difference was in what the archive assumed the reader already knew.

Ben felt his breathing steady as his mind engaged the problem. The discrepancy was not a mistake. It was a gap. Something had been removed from the version of the story the modern Order told.

"You are seeing it," Torin said. He was watching Ben, not the display. "The shape of what is missing."

"This is not the Reformations I learned."

"No," Torin agreed. "It would not be."

Kael had not moved from the wall. He had been silent since the stasis chamber — the same closed-door presence, the same economy of motion, the same watchfulness that made Ben think of someone who had learned to make stillness into a weapon. But his gaze had settled on the display, on one line of text near the bottom of the timeline, and something in his expression had shifted.

He crossed the room.

It was the first voluntary thing he had done since waking. Sera did not move aside for him — she held her position at the primary console, her shoulders set, her attention fixed on the data — but Kael did not ask her to. He reached past her, one hand finding the interface with the same thousand-year-old familiarity, and he changed a single word.

The correction was small. A date, or a name, or a designation — Ben could not read the original script well enough to know. But the archive accepted it. The display flickered and reorganized, and the timeline shifted by a fraction of a degree, and Kael stepped back to the wall without a word.

Sera looked at him. Her expression did not change, but something passed between them — a glance that carried more weight than a sentence.

"Kael Drenn," she said. "Still correcting the record."

Kael said nothing. But his silence had changed. It was not the silence of someone who had nothing to say. It was the silence of someone who had just said the only thing that mattered.

Ben registered the shift before he understood it. The survivors were not unified. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth — Sera's precision, Torin's warmth, Kael's quiet refusal to let anything stand that was not accurate. The separation was not a weakness. It was the truth.

Sera turned back to the console. Her hand moved across the display, pulling data into a configuration Ben could not follow — the archive responding to her the way a ship responded to a pilot who had flown it for decades. The timeline collapsed and re-formed around a single entry, a marker Ben recognized from the modern histories: the formal declaration of the Ruusan Reformations, the document that had ended the Jedi Lords and reshaped the Order into what it was today.

She read it in silence. Her expression did not change. But something behind her eyes went very still — the stillness of someone who had waited a thousand years to say something out loud and was choosing, now, to say it.

"The Reformations were public reform," Sera said.

Her voice was precise. Controlled. The same register she had used in the stasis chamber, the same clipped clarity, the same century-of-discipline composure. She was not softening the sentence. She was not offering context or apology or explanation. She was stating a fact.

"And private penance."

The sentence landed.

Ben felt it before he understood it — a drop in his center of gravity, a vertigo that was not physical but registered in his body anyway, the way the wrongness had registered in the entry chamber. His mind was still catching up, still processing the words, but his body already knew what they meant.

The Reformations were penance.

The foundation of the modern Order — the restructuring that had defined a thousand years of Jedi identity, the institutional self-understanding every youngling learned in their first history lessons — had been built on something Gavran chose not to say. Not a reform. Not a lesson learned. A concealed wound. An edited history.

Sera did not elaborate. She did not say what the penance was for. She did not need to. Ben could read the rest of it off her face — the way her jaw was set, the way her grey eyes held his without flinching, the way her composure was not coldness but control. She had waited a thousand years to say that sentence to a Jedi. She was not going to soften it now.

The archive hummed in the silence. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case, faintly lit, unopened. Torin was watching Ben with something that might have been sympathy. Kael had not moved from the wall.

Ben felt the weight of the question settle into his shoulders. If the Reformations were penance, what were they penance for? What had the Order chosen to hide?

He did not ask. The room did not try to answer. The three survivors stood in the archive they had built a thousand years ago, and their presence was the testimony, and the testimony was enough.

## Scene 05.03

The archive's silence had followed them up from Level 2.

Ben led the group back through the fortress's arterial corridor, the same route they had walked an hour earlier with the Codex fragment sealed in its case and the Reformations' true shape still settling behind his ribs. Now the case was stowed aboard the *Steady Returns*, and the thing that had been settling had become a weight he was carrying without yet knowing its dimensions. The fortress hummed around them — the same low, ancient frequency the console had produced in the archive, a sound that belonged to a building that had been waiting a thousand years for someone to listen.

The empty chamber was on Level 3, at the end of a row of twelve identical doors.

Nine of those doors stood open. Ben had counted them on the way in — the chambers where the monitoring system had flagged catastrophic failure, the bodies inside long since gone to dust and silence. Three chambers still glowed with the faint amber of active stasis. One chamber was different.

The door was not open. It was broken.

Ben stopped three meters from the threshold. The damage was visible from the corridor — not blast scoring, not forced entry from outside, but something that had worked its way outward. The metal was peeled back in layers, the edges smooth in a way that suggested time rather than violence. Centuries of pressure applied in the same direction. Patient. Methodical. The kind of work you could only do if you had nothing but time and a reason to keep going.

Desh moved past him without speaking. His hand found the doorframe, his fingers tracing the peeled metal with the same cataloguing attention he had used on the entry chamber's damage. Ben watched him file the facts — the direction of the stress, the absence of tool marks, the pattern of the erosion — and saw the moment Desh's expression shifted from assessment to something harder.

"Someone was awake in there," Desh said.

It was not a question.

The monitoring console was mounted in the wall beside the broken door, its display flickering with the same unsteady amber as the surviving chambers. Ben crossed to it. The interface was archaic — the same pre-Republic script Sera had navigated in the archive, the same translation protocols adapting to his presence with a half-second lag that made the data feel like it was arriving from somewhere very far away.

He pulled up the logs.

They stretched back centuries. The earliest entries were granular — temperature, atmospheric composition, Force-signature stability, all recorded in the same meticulous hand Ben was beginning to recognize as Gavran's. The middle entries were sparser. Maintenance flags. Minor fluctuations. The rhythm of a system that was holding.

Then the pattern changed.

Ben scrolled forward. The entries became more frequent, more focused — not the routine monitoring of a stable chamber but the attention of someone who had noticed something wrong and was trying to understand it. Force-signature anomalies. Power fluctuations localized to a single chamber. A note, repeated three times across two decades, that the anchor's presence was registering at partial awareness when it should have registered none.

The readable portion ended two months ago.

Ben stopped scrolling. The display flickered, the translation protocol struggling with whatever came next — corrupted data, or deliberately erased data, or data that had degraded past the point where even the archive's adaptive systems could reconstruct it. The screen showed fragments: a date stamp, a power reading, a string of characters that might have been a name before something chewed through the record.

"Desh."

Desh was already looking. "I see it."

"Two months?"

"Roughly." Desh's voice was the operational one — the register he used when he was filing facts he did not like and was not going to discuss until there was time. "The corruption pattern isn't random. It's concentrated on the most recent entries. Someone didn't want those read."

"Or something."

Desh did not answer. He did not need to. The peeled doorframe was answer enough.

Sera had entered the chamber.

Ben turned. She was standing in the center of the empty space, her back to him, her head moving slowly as she took in the walls. The damage inside was worse than the door — not the frantic gouging of someone trying to break free in desperation, but the systematic erosion of someone who had been working at the same problem for a very long time. The walls were layered with marks. Not scratches. Channels. Grooves worn into the stone by repetition, by pressure applied in the same places year after year after year. The pattern was almost architectural. Deliberate. Intelligent.

Sera's hand rose to touch one of the channels. Her fingers followed its curve, and Ben saw her shoulders go still in a way that was not composure.

It was recognition.

"Varik."

She did not turn. Her hand stayed on the wall for one beat, two, and then dropped to her side. When she faced him, her expression was the same precise mask she had worn in the archive — the century-of-discipline composure, the grey eyes steady, the jaw set. But something behind the mask had shifted. A hairline fracture. A half-second break that she sealed before it could become anything more.

"This was not a breakout," she said. "It was an extraction. Self-performed. Over centuries."

Ben waited. The silence between them was the kind that asked a question without asking it.

Sera did not answer it. She stepped past him, back into the corridor, and her stride was the same economical stride she had used since waking — no wasted motion, no hesitation. But her shoulders were tighter than they had been. And she did not look at the walls again.

Ben let her go. The weight in his chest had acquired another layer — the knowledge that whatever Sera had seen in those erosion marks, it was bad enough that she had chosen not to say it out loud. He filed that next to the Reformations revelation, next to the corrupted logs, next to the growing list of things he did not yet have words for.

Torin was standing in the doorway.

Ben had not heard him approach. The man moved quietly for someone his size — or maybe Ben had been too focused on the chamber to register the footsteps. Torin's arms were crossed, his head tilted slightly, his eyes moving across the damaged walls with an expression Ben could not quite read. Not assessment, the way Desh assessed. Not recognition, the way Sera had recognized. Something else. Something that sat at an angle to the scene.

"Patient," Torin said.

The word was quiet. Almost warm. The register of someone noting a quality they admired.

Ben's attention caught on the inflection. It was not wrong, exactly. It was one degree off from where it should have been — the way a note played slightly flat in familiar music registered as dissonance before the ear could name the error. Torin should have been unsettled. The chamber was unsettling. The evidence was unsettling. And Torin's voice had carried something that was not unsettlement.

It passed. Torin's expression shifted back into the warmth Ben was already beginning to associate with him — the easy presence, the storyteller's ease — and the moment was gone before Ben could decide whether it had been there at all.

He filed it. The way he had filed Sera's silence. The way he was filing everything in this fortress, stacking the pieces in the back of his mind, waiting for the shape to emerge.

"We need the map," he said.

---

The monitoring station on Level 1 was smaller than the archive — a functional room, built for work rather than preservation. The main display was a holographic projection of the fortress and its surrounding terrain, the topography rendered in the same amber light as the consoles, the Corridor marked as a thread of navigable space running outward from the fortress's position toward the nearest hyperlane access point.

Ben pulled the escapee's activity timestamps from the corrupted logs — what was left of them — and overlaid them on the map.

The pattern was immediate. The first activity clusters were inside the fortress: accessed terminals on Level 2, equipment moved from a storage bay on Level 1, supplies taken from a cache Ben had not known existed. The timestamps were spread across weeks. Not the frantic scrambling of someone fleeing. The methodical preparation of someone who knew exactly what they needed and where to find it.

Then the activity moved outward. A waypoint at the fortress perimeter. A second waypoint ten kilometers along the Corridor. A third at the edge of the map's range, timestamped three days before Ben and Desh had dropped out of hyperspace.

The direction was clear. The name was not.

Ben stood at the display, his hands braced on the console's edge, his shoulders carrying the weight of what the map was telling him. The escapee was not a mystery to be solved in this chamber. They were a moving target already weeks ahead, already moving along the Corridor, already doing whatever they had spent centuries planning to do. The fortress was not the primary theater. The fortress was the starting line, and someone else had crossed it before Ben even knew the race existed.

"Desh."

Desh looked up from the secondary console. He had been running his own analysis — the physical evidence, the supply manifest, the pattern of the accessed terminals — and his expression was the same hard assessment it had been at the broken door.

"Pull every monitoring packet," Ben said. "Every log, every flag, every data fragment between here and the Corridor's edge. Start reconstructing."

"Timeline?"

"Answer first light."

Desh nodded. He did not ask how Ben planned to close a weeks-old gap against an opponent who had already demonstrated centuries of patience. He did not need to. The question was sitting in the room with them, unasked and unavoidable, and Ben did not have an answer yet.

The display flickered. The amber light caught the edges of the map, the Corridor stretching outward into uncharted space, the escapee's waypoints marking a trail that was already growing cold. Kael had not spoken since they entered the monitoring station. He stood near the wall — the same position he had taken in the archive, the same closed-door stillness — but his gaze was on the map, and something in his silence had shifted.

Ben did not ask. The weight of what he now knew, and what he still did not, settled across his shoulders and stayed there.

## Scene 05.04

The command table on Level 1 was built for a different kind of war.

Ben spread the fortress data across its surface—Corridor maps rendered in the same amber light as the archive consoles, seal readings that pulsed with the wrongness he still could not name, and the chamber file. That one sat to the side, its display showing a name-shaped blank where the occupant's designation should have been. The translation protocols had chewed through the corrupted entries and found nothing. Not a name. Not a species marker. Not even a rank.

Absence, Ben thought. The wrongness had a shape now, and the shape was absence.

He did not say it out loud. The word would have made it real in a way he was not ready for.

Sera and Torin worked through the archive materials on the secondary console. Ben watched them without turning—registered the rhythm of their hands, the way Torin passed datacards to Sera without looking, the way Sera received them without acknowledgment. Muscle memory. A thousand years old and still running.

Torin's arrangement carried warmth. He grouped materials by narrative—here was the sequence of events, here was the context, here was what you needed to understand before the next piece made sense. A storyteller's instinct. Sera's arrangement carried precision. She sorted by tactical priority, by threat level, by what would kill them first if they got it wrong. No context. No narrative. Just the order of operations.

Ben filed the difference and said nothing.

His hands tightened on the edge of the command table. The Force-sense around him was still deadened—not absent, but unreliable, like trying to read a map through fogged transparisteel. He could feel the others in the room if he reached. Sera, a blade held perfectly still. Torin, a warmth that should have been reassuring and was not, quite. Kael, a closed door. Desh, the steady absence of someone who did not need the Force to be the most dangerous person in the room.

And the wrongness. Spreading outward from the fortress, diffusing through the wider sky. Present. Patient. Waiting.

"Desh."

Desh looked up from the tertiary console. He had been running his own analysis—the physical evidence from the broken chamber, the supply manifest, the pattern of the accessed terminals. His expression was the same hard assessment it had been since they found the empty chamber.

"The monitoring packets," Ben said. "The corrupted ones. Can you reconstruct them?"

"Most of them." Desh's voice was the operational register—the one he used when he was filing facts he did not like and was not going to discuss until there was time. "The corruption's concentrated on the most recent entries. Someone wanted those buried. But the response-quadrant data's intact underneath—the system was still logging even when the logs were being chewed through. Give me until morning."

"Morning."

"First light. The name will resolve."

The name. Ben heard the word land and felt something in his chest tighten—not quite relief, not quite dread. The thing that had escaped was not a mystery to be solved in this chamber. It was a moving target, already weeks ahead, already doing whatever it had spent centuries planning to do. The name would tell them who they were chasing. It would not tell them how to close the gap.

Kael spoke from the wall. "You are assuming pursuit."

Ben turned. Kael had not moved from his position near the door—the same closed-door stillness he had carried since waking, the same watchful distance. His arms were crossed. His voice was the dry register Ben was beginning to recognize as his default: observation delivered without inflection, a statement that was also a question.

"I am assuming we cannot stay here," Ben said.

"Those are not the same thing."

"They are right now."

Kael held his gaze for a beat. Then he nodded, once, and looked away. Not agreement. Acknowledgment. The distinction mattered, and Ben filed it next to everything else he was filing.

Sera set the last datacard into its sequence and straightened. Her hands fell to her sides with the precision of someone who had finished a task and was already calculating the next one. "The archive is organized for transit. Tactical priority only—the rest can wait for a return expedition." She did not say *if* there was a return expedition. The omission was deliberate. "Torin has flagged the Corridor node data. The first waypoint is three days' travel at sublight. The hyperlane access point is beyond that."

"We cannot make three days at sublight with five people aboard the *Steady Returns*," Desh said. "Not comfortably."

"We are not doing this comfortably."

Desh looked at Ben. The look said what Desh did not say out loud: *you are making a decision you have not told us about yet.*

Ben pulled his datapad from his belt.

The report to Luke should have been easy. He had written a hundred reports to his father—mission summaries, threat assessments, requests for additional resources. The format was muscle memory. The content was not.

He typed: *Survey complete. Uncharted world confirmed as pre-Ruusan Jedi installation. Anomalous Force readings consistent with degraded containment infrastructure. Continuing investigation along the Corridor. Will update when I have more.*

He stopped. His thumb hovered over the send key.

The report did not mention the survivors. It did not mention the seal. It did not mention the empty chamber, the broken door, the escapee with two months of head start and a name that would not resolve until morning. It did not mention the Reformations. It did not mention what Sera had said in the archive—*the Order does not know what it was built to bury*—or the way the words had landed in Ben's chest and stayed there.

It did not say *help*.

Every instinct Ben had trained told him to pick up the comm and call Luke in full. Tell him everything. Ask for a strike team, a research detail, the full weight of the Order behind him. That was the protocol. That was the responsible thing. That was what a Jedi Knight was supposed to do when he found something this big.

And every calculation he did not quite permit himself to finish told him that the Order's response time would cost more than the survivors had. That a fuller report would pull the mission out of his hands. That Luke would listen, and assess, and decide—and by the time the decision came back, the seal would have degraded further, the escapee would have gained another week, and the survivors would be someone else's problem.

Ben did not want them to be someone else's problem.

He did not examine that thought. He pressed send.

The datapad chimed once. Transmission confirmed.

Desh was watching him. Sera was watching him. Torin's hands had stilled on the console, and Kael's gaze had drifted back from the wall. The room was quiet in a way that was not silence—a held breath, a question nobody was asking out loud.

Ben put the datapad away.

"We lift in ten minutes," he said. "Pack what you need."

---

The *Steady Returns* strained off the plateau.

Ben felt it through the deck plates—the engines working harder than they were designed to, the inertial compensators struggling to smooth the climb. Five people in a ship built for two. The YT-series could handle it, barely, but the handling was not the point. The point was the weight.

He stood at the cockpit viewport and watched the fortress shrink below them. The mountain peak that had hidden it for a thousand years. The plateau where he had landed three days ago with a survey mission and a question he had not known how to ask. The wrongness was still there—diffused through the wider sky now, spread thin across the atmosphere, present but no longer concentrated. It would follow them. He knew that without knowing how he knew it.

Behind him, the survivors were finding places to sit in a ship that had never been meant to carry passengers. Sera had taken the copilot's station—not touching anything, but close enough to watch. Torin had folded himself into the jump seat behind her, his knees nearly touching his chest, his expression the same easy warmth he had worn since waking. Kael had found a corner in the crew compartment and made himself still. Desh was already running the first reconstruction pass on the monitoring packets, his hands moving across the secondary console with the efficiency of someone who had done this work before, in worse conditions, for higher stakes.

The *Steady Returns* cleared the atmosphere. The stars steadied. The Corridor stretched ahead of them—a thread of navigable space marked on Ben's nav display, the first waypoint already plotted.

Somewhere ahead, an unnamed anchor was moving. Somewhere behind, the Order did not know what Ben knew. And somewhere in the archive lists Desh was still compiling, the monitoring packets were resolving, byte by byte, toward a name.

Ben did not feel heroic. He did not feel triumphant. He felt braced.

The pattern had closed before he named it. The choice was already made.

The *Steady Returns* climbed toward the Corridor, and Ben let it.
