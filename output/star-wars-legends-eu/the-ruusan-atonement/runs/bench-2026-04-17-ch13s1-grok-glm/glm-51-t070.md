The world looked like home.

Ben pressed his palm against the viewport and watched the planet fill the frame—blue oceans, green landmasses, white cloud cover wisping across the southern hemisphere. A temperate world. The kind of place colonists picked for the mild seasons and the arable land. The kind of place that should have been humming with life.

"Settlement's on the northern continent." Desh Rolan's voice came from the sensor station, flat and professional. "Population readings are inconsistent."

"Inconsistent how?"

"Scanners show three thousand life signs. Then five thousand. Then zero." Desh's fingers moved across the console. "Cycling every twelve seconds. Either the planet's population is experiencing rapid demographic shifts, or—"

"Or the scanners can't lock." Sera Varik stood at the secondary viewport, arms crossed, her posture rigid as a parade-ground soldier. "What about chrono-sync?"

Desh checked. "Drift. Point-three seconds per minute relative to shipboard time."

Silence on the bridge. Ben looked back at the planet. Still green. Still blue. Still wrong.

"Standard approach," Torin Hal said from the pilot's seat. He didn't sound concerned. He sounded like a man reading a menu. "We go down, we assess, we adjust."

Kael Drenn leaned against the bulkhead near the airlock, watching. He hadn't spoken since they'd dropped out of hyperspace. His silence had a quality Ben had learned to read—not contemplation, but the patience of someone waiting for everyone else to finish being wrong.

The Steady Returns descended through atmosphere that tasted stale and recycled, even through the filtration systems. Ben watched the ground approach through the canopy—rolling hills, a river, the geometric patterns of a settlement. Buildings stood in neat rows. Streets traced orderly paths between them.

Everything was where it should be.

The landing gear touched down on a permacrete pad outside what had been the settlement's central market. Ben unclipped his lightsaber from his belt and clipped it back. A nervous habit. He caught Sera watching him and stopped.

"Stay sharp," Torin said, and led them down the ramp.

The air hit Ben first—cool, slightly damp, carrying the green smell of growing things. Normal. His lungs accepted it without protest. His eyes told him the same story the viewport had: a pleasant settlement on a pleasant world.

His feet told him something different.

The permacrete under his boots felt wrong in a way he couldn't name. Solid, yes. Weight-bearing, yes. But the texture was off. Like walking on a stage set instead of real ground.

Then he saw the vines.

They climbed the eastern wall of the market building—standard kudzu analogue, probably imported by the original colonists. But they were growing downward. Not drooping with the weight of leaves, but actively reaching toward the soil. New growth pointed at the ground instead of the sun.

Ben blinked. When he looked again, the vines had moved. Not much—a centimeter, maybe two. But they'd shifted while he was watching. Growing in the wrong direction, toward the wrong anchor, and doing it visibly.

"Look at this." Sera had stopped near a planter box. Purple flowers bloomed in the shadow of the building's overhang, their petals open wide. In the direct sunlight three meters away, identical flowers had closed tight. "They're reversed. The phototropism is inverted."

Ben walked toward the street. No birds called from the rooftops. No insects hummed in the planters. The silence wasn't empty—it was the silence of a room where everyone had stopped talking at once.

He stopped in the center of the main thoroughfare and reached for the Force.

The habit was older than memory. Every Jedi learned to extend their perception outward, to feel the currents of life and energy that connected all things. It was as natural as breathing—more natural, sometimes. The Force had been the background of Ben's existence since before he could walk.

His perception fired outward and hit nothing.

No—not nothing. That wasn't right. The energy was there. He could feel it, vast and present, pressing against his awareness like water against glass. But there was no structure to it. No current. No direction. No texture that distinguished one thing from another.

He reached for the light side and his hand closed on air.

He reached for the dark side and found the same absence.

The Force was here. He could feel its power, raw and immense, filling every corner of his perception. But it was formless. Light pouring into a room with no walls, no floor, no ceiling—nothing to catch it, nothing to reflect it, nothing to give it shape or meaning.

His inner ear screamed. The ground tilted forty-five degrees and his knees buckled. He caught himself on one hand, permacrete scraping his palm, and the world snapped back to horizontal—but he'd never actually fallen. His body had reacted to a shift that hadn't happened.

"Ben." Sera's voice, sharp. "Your breathing."

He hadn't noticed. Short, shallow pulls of air, his chest tight. He forced himself to stop. To stand still. To exist in a body that had forgotten which way was down.

"I can't—" He stopped. Tried again. "The Force is here. But it's not structured. There's no lattice. No framework."

Torin moved to his side, steadier. "I feel it. Or I don't feel it. The energy's present, but—"

"There's no handrail," Kael said from behind them.

Ben turned. The former Sith agent stood with his hands in his pockets, watching the inverted vines with an expression that might have been curiosity or might have been something harder.

"What?" Sera demanded.

"The Force." Kael's voice was flat, observational. "When you use it, there's always something to push against. Something that pushes back. Light, dark, it doesn't matter—the structure gives you purchase. Here?" He pulled one hand from his pocket and held it up, examining his fingers. "Nothing to grip. The handrail's gone."

Desh had moved ahead, his attention on the buildings. Now he stopped and pulled his chrono from his pocket. He held it up where they could see.

The second hand ticked forward. Stopped. Jumped three seconds ahead. Ticked twice more. Stopped again.

"Chrono drift," Desh said. "Measurable. I've been tracking it since we landed. We're losing time—losing it in chunks, not consistently."

Sera stared at the skipping hand. "That's not possible. Time doesn't—"
