The training saber caught him across the shoulder and Ben's first thought, before the sting even registered, was *third time*.

Third time this week. Same half-second gap. Same beat he should have read and didn't.

He stepped back and lowered his blade. His sparring partner — one of the younger Knights, whose name Ben kept failing to learn — was already dropping into a neutral stance, free hand raised in apology.

"Good hit," Ben said. He deactivated the saber and clipped it to his belt.

"Your guard was—"

"Tired." Ben rolled his shoulder, registering the dull throb where the blade had landed. "I'm fine. Call it here."

The Knight hesitated. Ben recognized the look — the slight calculation of whether to push, whether the son of Luke Skywalker wanted his ego managed or his session taken seriously. Ben kept his expression easy, which was its own kind of answer.

"Sure," the Knight said. "Same time Benduday?"

"Maybe. Get some sleep."

He waited, not watching the Knight leave, listening instead to the soft scrape of boots on the floor and then the hiss of the doors, and then silence.

The salle settled around him. Late evening meant the rest of the Temple had moved on to dinner or meditation or the comfortable routines that marked a Jedi Order slowly reassembling itself after years of damage. Through the walls, he could pick out the low hum of occupied meditation chambers, the distant percussion of another session two rooms over, the collective weight of sixty-some Force presences going about their business with no particular urgency.

None of them felt it.

That was the part he kept returning to.

Ben moved to the center of the floor and drew his unlit saber. He ran through the opening sequence of Soresu — guard, pivot, redirect — without power, without light, just muscle and balance and the forms his mother had drilled into him before he was old enough to understand why. His footwork was clean. His transitions were clean. Nothing in his body had forgotten anything.

He accelerated through the second sequence, then the third. The forms came the way they always came: without thought, which was the point.

He was four moves into the fourth sequence when it happened again.

Not a sound, exactly. More the absence of one — a pitch that should have been present and wasn't, the way a choir with one missing voice leaves a gap the ear goes looking for. It lasted less than two seconds. His weight shifted wrong on a pivot, not because his body had forgotten the step but because the Force had simply not been there when he reached for it, and the adjustment he'd been making without knowing he was making it since he was fifteen years old had nothing to catch.

He stopped. Stood still.

Two seconds. Gone before he could examine it.

He'd written off the first incident as fatigue. The second as distraction — there had been a Council briefing that afternoon about Outer Rim patrol coverage, and he'd been half-processing it through the whole session. The third time, tonight, he'd told himself the same thing he'd told the Knight: tired.

He wasn't tired.

Ben lowered the saber and stood in the center of the empty room, paying attention in the specific way his father had taught him — not reaching, not pushing, just listening. The Force was present. He could feel it, the familiar texture of it, the Temple full of people who used it as naturally as breathing. His connection was intact. His body was intact.

The wrongness was somewhere else.

He ran the fourth sequence again, slower. Deliberately placed himself in the moment before the gap and waited. It came back — fainter, almost subliminal, but there. Like pressing a finger against a wall and finding one spot where the material behind it was hollow.

He held that contact as long as he could.

The texture of it was what stopped him cold.

Two years inside Jacen's radius had left marks he could still identify — not trauma exactly, more like scar tissue that changed the shape of certain memories. Centerpoint, and Abeloth pressing against the edges of the Force like something too large for the space it occupied, had left different marks. Both were still present if he went looking.

This wasn't like those. Jacen had been a *weight*. Abeloth had been a *pressure*. This was neither.

A gap. Not a wound — a wound implied damage to something present. This was more like a place where the Force's normal architecture had simply been removed. Clean edges. No residue. The way a missing tooth left a space the tongue kept finding, not because anything hurt but because the geometry was wrong.

He'd never encountered that before.

And it had a direction.

He wasn't certain enough to call it precise, but his perception kept orienting the same way — southwest of galactic center, deep Outer Rim, past the trading corridors and the mapped survey routes into the dark. The bearing was consistent. It had been consistent across all three incidents, he realized now, and he'd been too focused on the combat failure to notice the pattern.

Ben clipped the saber to his belt and stood very still.

The Order had debriefed the Abeloth crisis extensively. The Council had catalogued every anomalous Force reading from the past five years and attributed most of them to lingering resonance. When he'd raised the wrongness in a routine after-action note three weeks ago, Cilghal's response had been measured and thorough and had essentially amounted to *the Force is still settling, Knight Skywalker, give it time.*

He hadn't pushed. He'd filed it and moved on, because that was what you did with a well-reasoned answer from someone you respected — and because the alternative was to be the Skywalker who saw disaster in everything and cried catastrophe until people stopped listening.

Except the wrongness had a direction now. And a texture he recognized, not from personal experience but from the historical records — Nathema, Malachor V, the sites where something had happened to the Force that the Order's archivists still didn't have clean language for.

Not a living threat. Not a wound. Something structural.

He was still working through what to do with that when the salle doors opened.

His father stood in the doorway. Luke Skywalker, sixty-seven years old and carrying it well, held a datapad at his side with the particular quality of stillness that meant he'd already made a decision and was waiting to see if Ben would make him explain it.

Ben recognized the posture. He'd grown up watching it across breakfast tables and mission briefings and the long silences after things went wrong.

He crossed the floor and stopped a few meters short.

"You're up late," Ben said.

Luke held up the datapad.
