The Jedi partner roster had forty-three names on it.

Ben's thumb rested over the first one — Korrel Vane, good in the field, solid instincts — and didn't move.

The hangar stretched around him, vast and echoic, the *Constellation* sitting twenty meters to his left with her running lights already cycling through their pre-departure sequence. Somewhere below, a loader droid knocked against a fuel coupling. The sound bounced off the ceiling and came back smaller. Ben looked at the roster. Vane. Then Pellan, who'd logged the absent signal distinction and apparently not known what to make of it. Then eight others he'd worked beside in the past two years, competent and trustworthy by any reasonable measure.

His thumb slid down past all of them and opened his personal contacts.

He told himself it was practical. The wrongness wasn't behaving like a Force phenomenon — it was behaving like a structural problem, and structural problems didn't require Force-sensitive backup. They required someone who could think without the Force as a crutch. Someone who would see what the Force wouldn't show him.

He told himself that.

Desh Rolan was four names down. Ben tapped the comm icon and pocketed the datapad.

The channel opened with the particular hiss of a long-range connection, the carrier signal crossing several sectors before it found its target. Ben waited. The hangar's environmental system pushed recycled air across the deck in slow, regular pulses.

"Rolan."

Tinny over the distance, but unmistakably Desh — flat vowels, the slight pause before speaking that meant he'd already clocked the incoming ID and decided how to answer it.

"Ben Skywalker," Desh said. "Eight months?"

"Seven."

"Seven. Right." A beat. "You calling to catch up, or because you need something?"

"I have a survey run. Outer Rim, southwest corridor, duration unknown. I need someone who can handle ship systems, read a situation without the Force telling them what to think, and not ask too many questions about why I'm calling you instead of pulling from the Temple roster."

Silence. Not hesitation — Desh processed fast — but the particular quiet of a man letting an unasked question sit exactly where it landed.

Ben had known Desh during the GAG years, when both of them had been doing work that didn't always survive contact with daylight. Desh had never needed the Force to read a room, and he'd never needed an explanation for why certain things stayed out of reports. That had been useful then. It was useful now.

"What's the pay?"

"Standard contract rate. Plus expenses."

"Define 'duration unknown.'"

"Weeks. Possibly longer. I'll know more when I get there."

Another pause. Ben heard what might have been a chair scraping back, or a bag being shifted. "I'm in the middle of something."

"I know."

"You know."

"You'd have said no already if it was something you couldn't hand off."

The silence this time had a different quality. Ben could picture Desh's expression — the slight compression around the eyes that passed for amusement in a man who didn't waste it on actual smiling.

"Rendezvous point?" Desh said.

"Ord Mantell. I'll send coordinates. Departing in twenty minutes."

"Two days."

"Confirmed." Ben paused. "Desh."

"Yeah."

"Thanks."

"Don't thank me. I'm billing you for the missed work." The channel clicked shut.

Ben pocketed the comm unit and walked toward the *Constellation*.

The boarding ramp was down. He went up it without glancing back at the Temple's interior access corridor, keeping his attention on the ship — the familiar weight of a well-maintained Ardak-class under his feet, the faint vibration of systems warming toward departure. He ran the preflight from memory, hands moving across the console in the sequence he'd repeated a hundred times on a dozen different vessels. Nav computer. Atmospheric seals. Comm array.

The wrongness was there the whole time. He'd stopped expecting otherwise.

He lifted the *Constellation* off the deck at a forty-degree angle, cleared the hangar's magnetic containment field, and fed power to the repulsors as the vessel climbed into Coruscant's lower atmosphere. The city spread in every direction — light layered over light, the planet's surface invisible beneath ten thousand years of construction. He'd grown up looking at it. It had never calmed him.

Traffic control gave him a departure lane. He took it, threading the *Constellation* through the upper transit corridors until the lanes thinned and the sky ahead shifted from orange-gray to deep violet to black. Coruscant's gravity fell away in stages. The hull ticked as the temperature dropped.

He set course for Ord Mantell and reached for the hyperdrive.

The jump took.

Hyperspace opened around the *Constellation* in its usual way — the smear of starlines, the blue-white tunnel, the faint vibration that lived somewhere between hearing and touch. Ben had made hundreds of transits. He knew exactly what they felt like.

The wrongness was still there.

He sat with his hands on the console and ran the instruments. Nothing anomalous — the nav computer tracking clean, the hyperdrive at standard output, all readings within normal parameters. He pushed out with the Force, the way he would when checking for threats during transit, and the wrongness met him the same way it always had: not a pressure, not a sound, but a geometric hollow in the direction the ship was already traveling.

It hadn't diminished when Coruscant fell away. It hadn't blurred when the hyperspace transition stripped away the normal sensory reference points. If anything, the transit had clarified it — the way background noise dropping out of a room reveals a signal you'd been half-hearing for hours.

Ben pulled his hands back from the console.

He'd been framing this as a survey run. Go to the bearing, take readings, report back. Seventeen flagged reports and a clean line on a grid, and he would find what waited at the end of it.

But the hollow wasn't at the end of a bearing.

It was pulling. Steady and directional, with the patient insistence of a compass needle that had found north and had no interest in pointing anywhere else.

He sat in the blue-white light and let that settle.

Then he checked the nav display, confirmed the Ord Mantell waypoint, and went to make caf.
