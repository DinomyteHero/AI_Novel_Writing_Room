Luke's office smelled like old flimsiplast and cooling caf. A single lamp threw amber light across a worktable buried in stacked datapads, and the holocomm in the corner blinked with something waiting.

Ben stepped inside and the door whispered shut behind him.

His father was already looking up. Luke Skywalker sat with the ease of a man who had learned to rest between crises, and his presence in the Force was the same thing it had always been — vast and warm and slightly difficult to stand next to without feeling like you were in orbit around something. Ben had grown up calibrating himself against that gravity. He'd learned to hold his own weight in it, the way you learned to walk on a ship with uneven thrust: constant, unconscious correction.

"I heard about the session," Luke said.

"Word travels."

"Teth filed a routine report. Third consecutive timing failure in the same sequence." Luke set down the stylus he'd been holding. "Sit down, Ben."

Ben sat. The chair across the worktable was the functional kind, no cushion worth mentioning. He'd occupied it a hundred times. He put his hands on his knees and waited.

Luke slid the datapad across.

The screen showed a survey grid — deep Outer Rim, southwest of galactic center, a string of waypoints along the Perlemian's outer spur. Seventeen reports were flagged in amber. Ben picked it up and scrolled.

"Teams have been filing anomalous Force readings for six weeks," Luke said. "No consistent signature. Nothing that matches a known phenomenon. Cilghal's working theory is post-Abeloth resonance, but the distribution is wrong for that — it's too directional."

*Directional.* Ben kept his expression flat and scrolled to the third flagged report.

There. A reading from a frigate three weeks ago, logged by a Knight named Pellan who'd noted the anomaly as *absent signal rather than corrupted signal* and then apparently not known what to do with that distinction. Ben zoomed in. Then the seventh report. Then the twelfth.

The same gap. Different locations, different observers, different equipment. The same gap.

He set the datapad down between them.

"You feel it," Ben said.

Luke's hands folded on the worktable. "I feel a disturbance. Unresolved, in the Outer Rim sectors. It's faint — I had to look for it. But yes."

"How would you describe it?"

A pause. Luke's expression did the thing it did when he was choosing words carefully — a slight stillness that wasn't hesitation but precision. "Discordant. Like an instrument that needs tuning."

Ben looked at the table surface. Not at the datapad, just at the grain of the material.

"I'd call it an absence," he said. "Not discord. More like a place where something was removed. Clean edges. No residue." He picked up the datapad again and turned it so the grid faced Luke. "These three readings fall in a direct line. The bearing runs southwest from the inner spur all the way out to the edge of the mapped corridor. It matches what I've been picking up in the salle for three weeks."

Luke looked at the grid. His expression didn't change, but Ben watched his father's focus shift — the quality of attention a Jedi gave something when it stopped being abstract and became real.

"You've been tracking it that long."

Not a question. Ben filed that away. "The timing failures gave me a consistent reference point."

"You didn't bring this to the Council."

"I brought it to Cilghal. She said the Force was still settling." Ben kept his voice even. "She wasn't wrong with the information she had."

Another pause. Longer.

"Ben." Luke's voice dropped half a register — not softer, exactly, but different. The voice he used when he was talking to his son rather than a Knight of the Order. "The readings you're describing. The clean edges. Where are you drawing that comparison from?"

There it was. Ben had seen the question coming and prepared a measured answer, the kind that communicated the relevant data without the weight of the experience behind it. He gave it anyway.

"Nathema. The archive records. Malachor." He met his father's eyes. "The accounts that describe what those sites *feel* like, not just what they are. This isn't identical, but the texture is closer to that than to anything living."

Luke was quiet for a moment that stretched past comfortable.

The warm gravity of his presence didn't change, but something in it tightened — the way a hand closes around something it's just recognized as fragile. Ben watched his father's face and saw, briefly, the thing Luke almost never showed: uncertainty. Not doubt in Ben, but the particular stillness of a man recalibrating against information that had landed harder than expected.

It lasted about three seconds.

Then Luke said, "That's a significant read."

"Yes."

"And you're confident in it."

"Confident enough to tell you."

Luke looked at him for a moment longer. Ben held the eye contact and did not look away, and something passed between them that wasn't quite spoken — his father acknowledging that Ben had brought this *here*, to this office, rather than continuing to carry it alone. The recognition that it had cost something to do that.

Luke picked up the stylus again and made a note on the physical pad beside his elbow. Old habit. He'd been doing it since before Ben could read.

"The route runs along that bearing," Luke said. "I was going to assign a standard two-ship team. But if your read is accurate, I want someone with direct perception of the anomaly on the lead vessel." He set the stylus down. "That's you."

Ben looked at the datapad. The seventeen amber flags. The clean line they made across the grid.

He stood and walked to the window.

Coruscant at night was the same thing it had been for ten thousand years — light stacked on light, the city-planet burning in layers, and somewhere below all of it the original ground that no one living had ever seen. He'd stood at windows like this his whole life and never once found the view calming. Too large. Too indifferent.

"Departure timeline?" he said.

"Three days. The *Constellation* is available — Ardak-class survey vessel, crew of twelve, fully provisioned."

"I'll review the waypoints tonight."

"Ben."

He turned.

Luke was still at the worktable, his expression settled back into the careful, considered look — the Grand Master's face, the one that held everything at a managed distance. But his hands were flat on the surface, not folded, and there was a set to his shoulders that wasn't quite official.

"The route is a starting point," Luke said. "Whatever you find out there — use your judgment."

Ben nodded once. "Understood."

He crossed back and picked up the datapad. Luke didn't reach for it. Neither of them said anything about the absence of a second ship, a partner, a backup contact. Ben tucked the datapad under his arm.

"Good night," he said.

"Good night."

The door opened and closed. The corridor outside was empty and quiet, the Temple's nighttime rhythms settled into their familiar patterns, and Ben walked back toward his quarters with the datapad's edge pressing against his ribs and seventeen amber flags mapped behind his eyes.

Somewhere to the southwest, past the last waypoint and the edge of the mapped corridor, the wrongness waited with its clean, hollow geometry.

He hadn't asked for anyone to come with him. His father hadn't offered.

Neither of them had said a word about what that meant.
