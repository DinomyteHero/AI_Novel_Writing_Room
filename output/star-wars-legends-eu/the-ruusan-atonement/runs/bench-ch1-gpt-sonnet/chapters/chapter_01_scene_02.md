Luke's office door slid shut behind Ben with a soft seal and a click from the lock plate.

Luke was already at the desk. He did not waste time on preliminaries. He took a datapad from the stack at his right and slid it across the polished surface into Ben's reach. Blue light climbed over Ben's fingers, cool and flat against the warmer wash of the office lamps.

"Survey reports," Luke said. "Outer Rim route. Seventeen in six weeks."

Ben looked down.

The notation caught his eye first. Same mark, repeated line after line in the margin field — spare, neat, and increasingly hard to dismiss when it turned up seventeen times. Below it: coordinates, timestamps, short field descriptions, names he knew and a few he didn't. Through the office window, Coruscant traffic ran on as a low constant wash, airspeeders threading somewhere far below. Closer in, a comm unit blinked silently on the shelf, and voices from the corridor leaked through the door in brief bursts whenever someone passed.

He scrolled.

First report: uncertain reading, localized distortion, no standard match. Second: the same notation, more certain. Third: a survey Knight had crossed out Force-field disruption and replaced it with a request for review.

Ben's thumb paused.

Three entries farther down, he opened the attached data trace and zoomed in on the readout. Not the numbers, not the jargon wrapped around them. Pattern. Timing. The same miss in the beat. The same sour note tucked under what should have run clean.

His breath slid out by degrees. He had not noticed it had gone tight until it began to loosen.

Luke said nothing.

Ben moved to the next flagged report. Then the next. Three of them matched so closely he could almost have mistaken them for his own impressions translated into institutional language by someone who had not known what to call them. His shoulders eased a fraction. Not relief, exactly — relief would have implied he was done carrying it. This was simpler and sharper. The thing had left evidence. It had reached other Jedi. It existed outside his own skull, which was good to have confirmed after years of considering the alternative.

"Three?" Luke asked.

Ben looked up.

Luke had not shifted position. One hand rested near a scatter of hardcopy briefs on the desk, the old habit he had never entirely dropped, no matter how good datapads got. His face was composed, Grand Master neutral, but not blank. Ben had spent enough of his life reading the spaces around Luke's words to know when silence meant waiting and when it meant caution. This one meant: tell me what you have.

"Three are close," Ben said. "Very close."

Luke nodded once. "I thought those might matter."

Ben went back to the pad. The office smelled of caf gone cool, old flimsiplast, and the recycled air that circulated through half the Temple. Under it sat the wrongness — thin but present, the same sour undernote he'd carried into the room. At this distance Luke's presence in the Force should have settled things by simple proximity. It usually did. Warm, steady, familiar enough that Ben's body registered it before his mind caught up.

Today the wrongness sat beneath that warmth and refused to move.

He zoomed in on the most recent report. The attached note was concise, almost irritated: route deviation recommended pending further review; phenomenon appears directional.

Ben felt Luke watching him work, not pressing, not filling the silence with explanation. For once that landed less like restraint and more like permission.

"When did these start?" Ben asked.

"First report six weeks ago," Luke said. "The last three came in over eleven days."

Ben tipped the pad, checking dates again. "And you sat on them."

"I collected them," Luke corrected.

Ben glanced up at that. "That sounds better in an archive."

Luke's mouth moved at one corner. "It usually does."

There it was. Small, dry, carrying a weight underneath that did not belong in jokes. Ben looked back at the screen before the moment could become more exposed than either of them wanted.

"Standard classifications did not fit," Luke said. "So I flagged them under a new notation and started looking for a line."

"And found one."

"Yes."

Ben scrolled to the route overlay and watched the points appear, one after another, a long sparse bearing through the Outer Rim. Not random. Not clustered. A line. His line, though nobody in the report field had called it that.

The back of his neck went cool.

Luke rose, came around the desk, and stopped close enough that Ben caught the scent of fresh caf — not the stale cup on the desk but the one Luke must have finished recently. He leaned one hip against the desk's edge and looked at the pad instead of at Ben.

"I've felt something too," he said.

Simple words, evenly spoken. Not disturbance. Not anomaly. Not any of the labels that made problems easier to file and harder to admit. Something.

Ben kept his eyes on the route map. His fingers were very still on the edge of the datapad.

"For eleven days," Luke went on. "Directional. And getting closer."

The traffic noise beyond the window seemed to flatten for a moment into pure sound. Ben heard the difference in Luke's voice more clearly than the words themselves — still measured, still formal, but the measure sat over strain the way a cloak sits over armor. Luke was not offering a theory. He was telling Ben where the line between public record and private knowledge had finally given way.

Dad, Ben thought, with the old reflexive weight of it.

Out loud he said, "Closer to Coruscant?"

"Or we're closing on it," Luke said. "I'm not prepared to say which."

Ben turned the pad, pulled up the navigational layer, and overlaid the planned survey route. The line matched so neatly it would have been almost funny if it had not sent his pulse up a notch. He tapped three points in sequence.

"These," he said. "These are the strongest matches."

Luke looked where Ben indicated. "That was my read."

Ben almost smiled at that. Almost. Trust Luke to phrase shared alarm like a cross-check in a tactical briefing.

He set the pad flat and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt. "It's not stationary."

Luke waited.

"It's either moving toward us," Ben said, "or pulling us toward it. The bearing's been there the whole time. It's just harder to ignore now."

Luke's gaze lifted from the screen to Ben's face. No surprise, no skepticism. Just attention — warm and unguarded for one short beat before the Grand Master settled back into place.

"All right," he said. "Then the mission stands."

Ben gave a small nod. Of course it did. That was the language they used for everything that mattered too much to name directly. Mission. Survey. Route assessment. A packet of flagged reports and a plotted course, instead of his father saying I need you to go because you've been hearing this longer than I have, and Ben answering I know.

Luke pushed off the desk and crossed to the cabinet by the window. He keyed it open, took out a hardcopy folder, and set it beside the datapad. "Officially, you're checking the reports in order, confirming false positives, and filing live updates through the notation channel."

"Officially," Ben echoed.

Luke's glance said he'd heard the word exactly as Ben intended it.

"The route is already cleared," Luke said. "Temple transport authority is filed. Local contacts are attached where they exist. If this turns out to be nothing, you lose a week and some sleep."

Ben set the datapad down and picked up the folder more to give his hands something to do than from any need for flimsiplast. "You believe that?"

Luke folded his arms. "No."

Honest. Immediate. That too was a kind of gift.

Ben crossed to the window with the folder tucked against his side. The transparisteel gave Coruscant back in layers: towers, traffic lanes, distant lights still visible through the daylight haze, the whole planet busy with people who had cargo to move and meetings to make and no reason to care that the Force had gone wrong along some empty Outer Rim bearing. Temple life moved behind him in muted fragments — footsteps in the corridor, a droid rolling past, someone arguing softly two offices down about requisitions.

He stood a moment and watched a cargo hauler bank between lanes.

The wrongness was still with him. Not louder. Just pointed now — a direction instead of a constant irritation. Useful, which was almost insulting.

Luke spoke behind him. "You can take a partner if you want one."

The line landed cleanly enough to pass for routine. It was not routine. The duty board existed. Half the point of being a Knight in a functioning Order was not doing every difficult thing alone unless the mission demanded it.

Ben looked out at the traffic a second longer before turning back.

He could have asked. There were names on the board marked green — competent Jedi, good people. He could also picture the questions, the watchfulness, the careful recalibrations when someone else caught his timing miss at the wrong moment and tried not to look concerned. Desh's face crossed his mind with inconvenient speed — not a Jedi, not on any board, and likely to say yes before Ben finished the sentence.

Ben set the folder on the broad sill and met Luke's eyes.

"I can handle a survey," he said.

Luke held his gaze. Warmth, worry, and something close to resignation moved there and were gone again behind professional stillness.

"I know," Luke said.

That was all.

Ben inclined his head, the motion halfway between acknowledgment and acceptance. "I'll depart within the hour."

Luke nodded once.

Ben picked up the folder. He did not suggest a partner. Luke did not push. Neither of them acknowledged what that choice meant.
