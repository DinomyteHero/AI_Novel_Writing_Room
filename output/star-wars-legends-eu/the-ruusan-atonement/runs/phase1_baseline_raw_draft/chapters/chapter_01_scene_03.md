The hangar smelled like exhaust wash and hot metal, the recycled air carrying the faint sharpness of recent engine cycling. Ben's footsteps rang off the durasteel deck as he walked toward slip fourteen, where the *Bantha's Luck* sat fueled and preflight-checked, a mid-range scout vessel that had the virtue of looking like nothing in particular.

He pulled out his datapad before he reached her.

The Jedi Knight roster loaded automatically — alphabetical, twenty-three names, most of them people he'd trained alongside or worked with in the last two years. His thumb scrolled the list. Aven. Corsali. Dreth. Good Jedi, all three. Aven had been on Shedu Maad with him; Corsali had covered his exit on Toydaria six months back. Dreth was probably the best slicer in the Order under the age of forty.

He kept scrolling.

The justification assembled itself with the practiced ease of something he'd rehearsed without knowing it. The mission was survey work, not combat. Force-sensitives read the anomaly differently from each other — he'd already seen that in his father's office. A second Jedi wouldn't double his data, it would just give him someone else's interpretation to weigh against his own. What he needed was someone grounded. Someone who would tell him when he was pattern-matching phantoms.

His thumb reached the bottom of the roster and slid past it into his personal contacts.

The guilt came and went in less than a second.

He found Desh Rolan's tag three entries down and hit the comm icon.

---

The connection took forty seconds to establish — Desh was on Corellia, which meant routing through three relay stations. Ben walked a slow circle around the *Bantha's Luck*, checking the landing strut seals out of habit while the comm chimed.

"Rolan." The voice that came through was flat and unhurried, slightly compressed by the long-range codec. "It's late."

"It's oh-four-hundred here. On Corellia it's—"

"Still late." A pause. "What do you need?"

Ben stopped at the port strut. "A job. Outer Rim survey run, three weeks minimum. I need a second set of eyes — someone who can run ground-level assessments while I handle the Force-side readings."

"Outer Rim." Desh's tone didn't change. "Anything I should know about the situation?"

"Anomalous Force readings along a corridor of systems. Probably post-Abeloth residue." He paused. "Probably."

"But you're calling me at four in the morning."

"I'm calling you because you're good at what you do and I trust your read on environments that don't have obvious answers."

There was a beat of silence on the line. Not hesitation — Desh didn't hesitate, it was one of the things that made him useful — but consideration. Ben waited, aware he was bracing, and aware of why he was bracing. The last three Jedi he'd worked with closely had each, in their own way, required him to manage them. Manage their reactions to the name. Manage the careful deference that was somehow more exhausting than outright friction. Manage the unspoken weight of expectation that followed him into every briefing room.

"When do you need me in position?" Desh asked.

The tension Ben had been carrying since his father's office dropped about three centimeters. "I'm lifting in two hours. You can meet me at—"

"Coronet spaceport. Bay seven, public docking. I'll be there."

"You don't want to know the pay rate?"

"You'll be fair." A dry pause. "You always are."

Ben ended the call and stood for a moment with the datapad at his side, looking at the *Bantha's Luck*. The relief was real and he didn't examine it closely. Desh had been part of the Galactic Alliance Guard's intelligence division — the quieter end of things, the part that didn't make the news feeds — and they'd crossed paths twice during the war in ways that left a residue. Not friendship, exactly, not at first. The kind of professional trust that grows from watching someone make a hard call correctly in a situation where the wrong call was easier. Desh had never asked Ben about Jacen. Ben had never explained. The silence on both sides wasn't avoidance so much as mutual acknowledgment that some things were understood without requiring documentation.

He trusted that. He trusted it more than he trusted most things.

---

Departure protocols took twelve minutes. Ben ran them manually, the checklist giving his hands something to do while his mind stayed on the survey corridor. Seventeen systems. The wrongness had been present in the Temple for five days — directional only in the vaguest sense, more like pressure than bearing. He'd mapped the intervals. They hadn't been random, but the pattern had been too diffuse to read.

The *Bantha's Luck* lifted clean on repulsors, the hangar deck falling away below him. He angled the bow toward the exit track, the Temple's upper spires visible through the viewport for a moment before the approach corridor cut them from view.

He didn't look back.

The ascent through Coruscant's upper atmosphere took eight minutes. The planet's light-scatter faded as the sky darkened, stars hardening from suggestion to fact. He filed his exit vector with orbital traffic control — Outer Rim survey, standard clearance, no escort required — and got acknowledgment in under thirty seconds. Coruscant was efficient, if nothing else.

The wrongness was still there. He'd half-expected it to ease once he was moving, the way a headache sometimes loosened when you finally did the thing you'd been putting off. It didn't ease. It sat at the edge of his perception, that same sourceless absence, the place where something should have been registering and wasn't.

He set the nav computer to the first survey system and verified Desh's rendezvous coordinates. Then he waited out the orbital traffic queue, watching freighters and transports move in their orderly lanes, and tried not to think about the seventeen flagged reports.

He thought about them anyway.

The jump point cleared. Ben advanced the throttle and felt the familiar compression of a hyperspace entry — the stars elongating, the viewport going white-blue, the brief physical sensation of the universe rearranging itself around the ship.

The wrongness didn't fade with the jump.

He'd expected it to. Hyperspace was its own thing — Force perception went soft in transit, impressions arriving muffled and slow, like sounds through water. Whatever he felt in realspace usually quieted during a jump. It was one of the few reliable constants.

This didn't quiet.

It sharpened. Not louder, exactly — the absence didn't have volume. But it acquired shape. Direction. The way a compass needle swings and then holds, quivering slightly but committed, pointing somewhere specific.

Ben stared at the hyperspace tunnel for a long moment.

He was going the right way. He didn't know how he knew that, only that the wrongness was no longer just *present* — it was ahead of him, out along the survey corridor, waiting.

He checked his instruments. Everything normal. He checked them again.

The needle held.
