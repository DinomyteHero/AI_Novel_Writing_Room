Luke's office smelled like warm electronics and the faint, dry scent of old flimsiplast — survey reports, probably, the physical kind he still preferred for field data. The desk lamp was off. The room's light came mostly from the holocomm array on the far wall and the city-glow bleeding through the transparisteel, Coruscant's perpetual orange haze softening the edges of everything.

Luke slid the datapad across the desk without preamble.

Ben caught it. The blue glow of the display threw light up across his father's face, and for a moment Luke looked older than he had any right to look — not tired, exactly. Attentive in a way that cost something.

Seventeen flagged survey reports. Ben scrolled the list, his thumb moving through entries tagged with the same anomaly marker — *FR-UNCLASSIFIED/RECURRING* — strung out along a corridor of Outer Rim systems like a fault line on a geological map. The timestamps ran back three weeks.

"You've been tracking this."

"Monitoring," Luke said. "There's a difference."

Ben kept scrolling. The readings were dry, technical — spectral deviation indices, Force-resonance baselines flagged against regional historical norms. He almost passed the third entry, then stopped. Zoomed in. The deviation pattern was a notch-shaped dropout in the resonance baseline, clean-edged, not gradual. He'd seen that shape before.

He scrolled to the seventh report. Same notch. The fourteenth. Same.

"These three." He turned the datapad and pushed it back across the desk, finger resting on the entries. "They don't read like post-crisis settling. The dropout is too clean. There's no gradient — it's not fading from something, it's just absent."

Luke studied the screen. "That's one interpretation."

"What's yours?"

Luke leaned back. His Force presence was what it always was — vast and warm, the familiar weight of it like standing near a low fire, something Ben had oriented by his entire life. But underneath that warmth, now that Ben was looking for it, was the same faint sourness he'd been cataloguing in the salle. His father felt it too. He'd felt it for three weeks and said nothing, which meant he hadn't been certain, or hadn't wanted to alarm anyone, or both.

"Post-Abeloth disruption is still the most probable explanation," Luke said. "The Force has been recalibrating since the Maw. These readings could be residual."

"They could be." Ben set the datapad down. "I don't think they are."

Luke's eyes moved to him. Not challenging. Waiting.

"I've been feeling it in the Temple," Ben said. "Five days. Not constantly — intervals. It's not dark, it's not corrupted, there's no emotional residue. It's just — absent. Like something that should be there isn't." He paused. "I felt something adjacent to this twice before. Once with Jacen. Once in the Maw."

The name sat between them for a moment. Luke didn't flinch from it.

"I know," Luke said. He was quiet for a beat, and something in his presence shifted — not the warmth cooling, but the attention behind it sharpening, turning inward for a moment the way it did when he was reaching rather than just listening. "I've felt the wrongness for three weeks. I haven't felt what you're describing. The absence." He looked at the datapad. "Your read may be sharper than mine on this particular shape."

Ben waited. There was more.

"I've been through enough disruptions that my baseline has adjusted," Luke said. It wasn't self-deprecation. Just observation, delivered with the same directness he used for everything. "Centerpoint. Zonama Sekot. The Maw. After a certain number of large disturbances, the Force stops feeling fully settled. I may have been treating this as noise because noise is what I've learned to expect." He met Ben's eyes. "I'm not dismissing your interpretation. I'm telling you why mine is different."

Ben sat with that. His father, not managing him — placing the cards on the table, explaining the calculus, trusting him to weigh it. He'd half-expected the careful deflection, the referral to Cilghal, the gentle suggestion that pattern-matching under stress was understandable. Instead he was getting this: *here is my reasoning, here is its limit, here is where yours may exceed it.*

His shoulders dropped a fraction. The faint tightness across his upper back, the one he'd been carrying since the salle, eased slightly. He picked up the datapad again and looked at the survey corridor.

Seventeen systems. The route ran spinward along the Outer Rim, skirting the edge of known space. Long haul, light traffic, minimal support infrastructure. The kind of assignment that took weeks.

"You want a survey team out there."

"I want eyes on it," Luke said. "Someone who can cross-reference what the instruments show against direct Force perception. Someone whose read I trust." A pause. "The mission is yours if you want it."

Ben looked at the corridor map. The wrongness was there — he could feel it even at this distance, faint and directional, like a draft from a door left open in a room you couldn't quite locate. The survey route ran straight through it.

"Solo?"

"That's your call."

He turned toward the window. Coruscant stretched below in its usual density — lights stacked on lights, the city's endless industry indifferent to whatever was wrong with the Force above it. He'd spent five days alone in the Temple with this, mapping intervals, building a case, not telling anyone because telling anyone required admitting he didn't know what he was looking at. The survey mission was the same calculation extended outward. Go look. Come back with data. Figure it out.

The alternative was taking a partner, which meant briefing someone on five days of private observation and a theory he couldn't fully articulate, and watching them calibrate their reactions to his name rather than his argument. It wasn't self-pity. It was just the math of being a Skywalker. Even the good ones did it.

"I'll take the survey," he said.

Luke nodded once. He reached across the desk and pulled the datapad back, and for a moment his hand rested on it without moving — not hesitation, not reluctance. Something else. The gesture of a man who had already accepted a decision he wasn't certain about and was choosing not to reopen it.

"I'll have the mission packet transmitted to your quarters by oh-six-hundred. The *Jade Shadow* is available if you want her."

"I'll check the hangar roster." Ben turned from the window. "Is there anything else?"

"No." Luke looked at him. The warmth was still there, unchanged, the way it always was. "Be careful."

"Always am."

He picked up his jacket from the chair by the door and left.

Behind him, the office was quiet. The datapad sat on the desk with its seventeen flagged reports, and neither of them had said the obvious thing, and the obvious thing would still be true in the morning when the mission packet arrived.
