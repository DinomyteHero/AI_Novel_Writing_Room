The training blade caught him across the shoulder before he processed that his guard had moved wrong.

Not slow. Wrong. The lag wasn't in his arm — it was underneath his arm, in the read he should have had a full beat before the strike arrived. His partner's weight shift, the angle of the blade's arc: he'd seen it. He'd processed it. And then the Force-sense that should have sharpened that data into reflex had arrived half a step late, like an echo that forgot it was supposed to lead.

The hit wasn't hard. Training sabers weren't built for hard. The sting across his shoulder was barely worth noting.

He noted it anyway.

"Third time this week," his partner said, not unkindly. She was already rolling her wrist, resetting her stance — an invitation.

Ben deactivated his saber. "I'm going to claim fatigue and see if you're polite enough to let me."

She was. She offered a decent imitation of believing him, which was the most he could ask. Boots on the floor, the soft click of the door, and then the salle was his.

He stood still for a moment, hilt loose in one hand, and listened to the Temple settle around him. Somewhere down the corridor a cleaning droid was running its cycle, the low mechanical hum reaching him through the walls. A lift chimed — the late shift changing over, probably, the skeleton crew that kept the administrative wings functioning after dark. Coruscant's ambient light bled through the high ventilation slats, that particular orange-grey that meant the city was never quite sleeping and never quite awake.

Three times. Same week.

His body was fine. He'd been genuinely tired before — bone-deep tired, the kind that came off weeks in the field when the mission refused to end. This wasn't that. His reflexes, tested in isolation, held steady. It was the layer underneath that kept arriving late to the conversation.

Ben walked to the center of the salle and raised his unlit hilt.

The form was Shien, third sequence — not because it was difficult, but because he'd drilled it until it ran below thought. Muscle memory was reliable. Muscle memory didn't depend on anything outside him. If the problem was in his body, the form would show him. If the problem was somewhere else, the form would show him that too.

He moved through the opening stance.

The first six counts were clean. His weight distributed correctly, his transitions landed where they were supposed to land, the hilt tracing its arc through positions he could have managed in his sleep. His body knew this. His body was fine.

At the seventh count, he felt it.

Not heard — felt. A gap in the room's texture, the way the Force-presence of the salle hit him a half-beat behind the physical space he was standing in. Like the room's echo arriving before the sound. Like reaching for a grip in the dark and closing your hand on air for a fraction of a second before the grip was there.

His foot came down wrong. Not badly — no one watching would have flagged it. But he knew.

He stopped.

Sweat had dried on his palms during the conversation with his partner and was starting again now, slower. He pressed his back against the wall and stayed there, the unlit hilt held against his sternum, and let himself actually sit with the fact that this was the fourth time in five days. Not three — he'd miscounted when his partner said it. Four. The one on Kuat he'd written off as environmental interference. The two in the Temple corridors he'd written off as distraction. Tonight made the Kuat reading look like the start of something, not an anomaly.

His father had spent years telling him that the Force wasn't furniture — it didn't just stay where you left it, it moved, it changed, it reflected back what was happening in the galaxy whether you wanted it to or not. Ben had understood that intellectually for most of his life. He'd understood it less abstractly after Abeloth, when the whole Order spent six months feeling like they were reaching into a drawer that had been rearranged in the dark.

This wasn't that. He'd know that feeling anywhere now.

This was something that hadn't happened before, which was its own kind of problem, because his list of things that hadn't happened before was getting shorter every year, and nothing that made the list had ever turned out to be minor.

He pushed off the wall.

Two circuits of the salle, not sparring, not running forms — just walking, letting his feet find the rhythm his head wasn't providing. The overhead lights had dimmed to their low evening setting, the practice dummies standing in their locked positions along the far wall like an audience that had lost interest. He'd trained in this room since he was twelve. He knew the particular way the ventilation moved the air, the slight give in the third panel from the left where the flooring had settled. He knew this room better than most of the people who used it.

The room felt like the room. The Force-presence of the room felt like it was running slightly behind the room.

That wasn't fatigue. That wasn't post-Abeloth residue, because he'd felt that and it didn't feel like this, and he was done pretending the distinction didn't matter.

He stopped walking.

He wanted to tell someone. That was the irritating part — not the wrongness itself, but the specific social geometry of trying to explain it. He'd raised it once, early in the week, framed carefully enough that it could be dismissed as residue from the crises, and it had been dismissed as residue from the crises. He understood why. The Order had spent the better part of three years firefighting one Force-related catastrophe after another, and the collective appetite for new anomalies was approximately zero. He got it. He'd have dismissed it too if someone else had brought it to him.

Except he was the one standing in the salle at the twenty-second hour unable to trust his own timing, and that was a different kind of problem.

He was still holding the unlit hilt. He looked at it.

His father's generation had its own list of things that had never happened before. His grandfather's generation had had a longer one. If there was a Skywalker family tradition worth carrying forward, it was probably the one where someone noticed a problem that everyone else was calling residue, and the noticing turned out to matter.

He wasn't going to write that down. It was too neat, and the neat version was always wrong.

He clipped the hilt back to his belt and was reaching for the light panel when the door opened.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben had seen enough times to read without a word between them.

A mission.
