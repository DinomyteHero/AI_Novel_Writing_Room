The coupler sparked twice. Blue-white light flickered across Desh’s face before the splice locked.

“There we go,” he muttered, no triumph in it.

The access panel hung open, its corroded seam pried wide by a multi-tool that had no business touching something this old. He’d wedged the coupler in at an angle—modern hardware pressed against storage media that predated the Reformation by decades. The connection hummed, two languages forced to share one throat.

Ben stayed back, hands shoved in his pockets. Everything in him wanted to shove Desh aside and do it himself—the Skywalker reflex: see a problem, fix it faster. He didn’t.

The room was cold. Not climate-control cold, but the kind that had forgotten warmth was possible. Recycled air cycled through filters that had lost their charge centuries ago. His breath fogged and vanished. The durasteel under his boots felt wrong—too rigid, too deliberate, laid by people who expected to stand on it under fire.

He reached, briefly, not hunting for anything specific.

The Force here had texture. Not the thin, unraveling drag of deep space, but something dense and institutional—bootprints of minds that had moved through with absolute certainty. Heavy boots. Long shifts. Orders given and received without ceremony. Whatever warmth those people carried, they’d kept it somewhere else. This room had been for work.

Ben pulled back. The weight of all that certainty pressed against his ribs.

“Got something,” Desh said.

The holotable groaned. Displays flickered up—low-resolution phosphor green over grey—resolving into shapes almost like star maps, but wrong. The projections skipped, dropped frames, recovered. Star positions were wrong. Not corrupted. The charts predated the galactic surveys that had standardized the modern frame. Someone would have to rebuild the coordinate matrix from scratch to align them with current nav data.

“C2 architecture,” Desh said, sliding along the table’s edge, reading the layout the way he read a room. “Sensor ring outside, analysis middle, command core. Not a library. Not a temple.” He tapped the console frame. “Ops table.”

“Pre-Ruusan Jedi didn’t wall off military command from religious function,” Ben said.

“Shows.” Desh waved at the weapon racks, the kill-box entry, the total absence of decoration. “My first posting had more soul, and it was a prefab hut on Mantell.”

“You’re comparing it to a GAG forward base.”

“I’m saying I know the type.” Desh straightened. “Someone here believed very strongly in not dying.”

Ben stepped to the primary display. His fingertips worked the interface, navigating menus built for hands that had known the system for years. The controls were logical once you stopped expecting them to behave like anything built in the last three centuries. He found the message archive on the fourth try.

The warning looped. He’d already read the translation twice, standing at the main console when the lights came up. Two words. Endless repeat. Broadcasting on a frequency no modern receiver could hear.

He pulled the attached structural data. Degraded. Compression artifacts. Whole sections collapsed to unreadable strings. He sorted methodically, flagging recoverable fragments, discarding hash.

*Containment integrity—*

*—anchor cascade, nodes—*

*—seal failing at—*

The pieces didn’t form a picture. They formed a silhouette. Something held. Something needed a lattice of nodes to hold it. That lattice was breaking.

His throat went dry.

“The message has support files,” Ben said, voice steadier than his pulse. “Most are gone. What’s left talks about containment nodes and a seal.”

Desh leaned in, scanned the flagged lines. His face didn’t shift. He’d already finished the math. “How long’s it been broadcasting?”

“Timestamp encoding puts it before the Ruusan Reformations.”

A beat.

“And nobody heard.”

“Frequency’s wrong for anything built since. You’d need pre-Republic hardware, or—” Ben stopped.

“Or you’d need to already know to look,” Desh finished.

Silence stretched between them, thick as the dust on the consoles.

Desh stepped back from the table—always putting physical distance between himself and the conversation he was about to have professionally.

“Ben.” He crossed his arms. “We’ve got evidence of a major unclassified anomaly, a failing containment system of unknown scope, and a facility somebody worked hard to erase from the modern charts. SOP says we call Coruscant. Report to Luke. Wait for a specialized team.”

Ben’s fingers flexed at his sides. “I know what SOP says.”

“Then I’m not telling you anything new.” Desh’s voice stayed flat, not pushing. “Just saying it aloud so it’s on record. We file. We document. We let people with bigger budgets and more context take over. That’s the play.”

Ben kept his eyes on the display. Fragments cycled. *—nodes failing—seal—*

He knew Desh was right. He also knew calling Coruscant meant committee review, cross-reference against classified archives, jurisdictional wrangling between Council and Senate, preliminary team dispatch. Weeks. Optimistically.

The message had broadcast for centuries without an answer.

The nav computer interrupted—not with sound, with light. The holotable blanked white, then rescaled so hard Ben rocked back a half-step. The local star map—this system, the Outer Rim cluster, the three worlds where he’d shot the carvings—shrunk to a single dot in the lower corner.

A new map filled the table.

Stars he didn’t recognize. Dozens of installation markers, each pulsing the same signature as the room they stood in, arranged along a sweeping corridor from Mid Rim to Outer Rim. Not random. Not scattered. A perimeter.

A fence.

He counted twice. Twelve nodes.

“That’s not a bunker,” Desh said quietly, and for the first time since they’d entered, his professional calm cracked wide enough for Ben to hear the whistle of breath through it.

Ben didn’t answer. He traced the arc, reading the geometry. Each node calculated to cover a sector. Overlapping fields of—what? Sensor range? Containment envelope? The data wouldn’t say. It only gave the shape, and the shape crossed multiple sectors.

Whatever the ancient Jedi built, they hadn’t built one.

His hand moved without waiting for permission. He pulled his datapad, activated scanner, copied everything—coordinates, routing, broadcast frequency, node positions—then squirted the packet to the *Steady Returns* nav computer through his comlink.

“Ben.”

“I heard you,” Ben said, sharper than intended. “I know what you recommended.”

Desh didn’t argue. He watched the transfer with the stillness of a man who’d seen enough ops to know when the decision was already made and commentary would only burn daylight.

The nav computer pinged. Ben waited.

Coordinates resolved.

No hyperspace lane reached it. No Republic survey marker. No name. The point sat in a region the modern charts rendered as blank—not unexplored, but erased. Surrounding systems had survey data. That location had nothing, a void shaped exactly like a secret.

Someone had locked the door and scorched the address.

Ben’s stomach dropped into his boots. They weren’t following breadcrumbs anymore. They were staring at a map drawn by people who’d built something so dangerous, they’d erased themselves to keep it hidden.
