Sera crossed the archive before Ben had finished taking in the room.

Her hand went to a recessed strip on the console, then another, then a third in quick succession. No hesitation. No testing. The dead panel beneath her fingers answered with a low hum and a wash of pale blue light that rolled up the wall in bands.

Ben stopped half a step behind her. The recycled air carried dust, cold metal, and the sharp cooked smell of electronics waking after too long asleep. Off to the side, inside its sealed case, the Codex fragment gave off its own faint light, steady and watchful.

Sera did not look impressed with herself. She looked angry in the controlled, efficient way of someone who had no intention of giving the anger any extra room.

“You remember all that?” Ben asked.

“I remember my work.”

The words came flat and exact. Not pride. Not nostalgia. Ben felt his chest pull tight anyway. A thousand years was supposed to turn people into history. Sera had just keyed a live archive faster than he could have managed with a modern Temple interface and a help menu.

Desh moved up on Ben’s left, boots quiet on the durasteel. “That’s not encouraging.”

“No,” Ben said.

Data columns snapped into place across the main display. The script was Basic, technically, but not the Basic Ben had grown up reading. The forms were older, more formal, built for legal weight and administrative certainty. He could pick out words, then lose the sentence around them.

Containment. Custody. Verge authority. Binding articles.

His skin prickled.

Torin came in beside Sera as if he had done it yesterday instead of before Ben’s great-great-grandparents had been born. He rested one broad hand against the edge of the console, and that simple familiarity hit Ben harder than the waking display had. The room had never belonged to ruins. It had belonged to people.

“Veranthos Central Index,” Torin said, and there was that warmth again, the easy cadence that kept making Ben want to trust him before he had time to check himself. “Still ugly. Gavran always did think clarity was a moral virtue. Usually while burying three things inside one sentence.”

His mouth tilted. Not enough to make light of it. Just enough to remind the room it still contained human beings.

He brushed two controls, and the main index collapsed into nested branches. One branch pulsed amber.

“There,” Sera said at once.

Torin nodded. “Yes. We used to joke that if the fortress ever burned, that file would survive out of spite.”

Ben watched the file expand. More dense script. A chain of attached orders and sealed authorizations. Names. Some marked by title, some by military command, some by temple office. Brotherhood references buried in casualty and transfer records. Structural diagrams of the level beneath them. Chamber numbering.

Not thirteen. Twelve.

His stomach dropped a little farther when he saw the center notation attached to the network hub.

“Thirteen anchors,” Desh said quietly, reading over his shoulder.

“Twelve chamber-bound,” Sera corrected without looking away. “And Gavran.”

Her tone made the distinction feel less like arithmetic than indictment.

Torin’s hand stayed near the display. “The center had to hold the load together. The outer twelve took the distributed draw. Without the keystone, the work failed.”

Ben looked at the chamber map again. A ring of prisons around a single point. The room felt smaller.

“You said ‘anchors’ like you meant people,” Desh said.

Sera’s jaw shifted once. “I did.”

Silence held for a beat. The archive hummed under it.

Torin exhaled through his nose. “Veranthos was a campaign world by then. Too many wounded, too many dead, too many people calling necessity by cleaner names. That was the climate Gavran worked in.” He tapped one of the attached records. “This index is from before the Seventh Battle’s aftermath. Before any of your later standardizations. Before any of what came after had names we would know.”

Ben’s focus tightened. That mattered. That mattered a lot.

He pulled out his datapad, thumb already moving over the local archive cache he’d brought down from the ship. Standard Jedi history. Training references. Public records his Order still used when it taught the end of the New Sith Wars: the Army of Light dissolved, the old Jedi Lords gone, training centralized again, the Order tied closer to Republic and Judicial oversight. Broad strokes. Foundational. Real.

His pulse had picked up by the time he matched date clusters and key phrases.

“No,” he said under his breath.

Desh glanced at him. “You got something?”

Ben turned the datapad so Desh could see. “These terms. Not all of them, but enough. Custodial language, containment authorities, sealed implementation provisions. The public Ruusan Reformations after the New Sith Wars use descendants of this wording.”

Torin’s head came up. Not with recognition. With attention.

Sera went very still.

Ben scrolled, comparing one screen to the other, feeling the mismatch hit him before he had a clean sentence for it. The old file sat there in archaic legal script, cold and specific. His own datapad held the history he’d inherited through his father’s Order, the account every Jedi in his generation absorbed as part of learning how the old Order had stopped being an army and become something else.

Both could be true.

That was the problem.

“The public reforms were real,” Ben said, mostly to hear the words outside his own skull. “Demilitarization, ending the Jedi Lords, centralized training, Republic oversight. That happened. But this—”

He stopped because Kael moved.

Until then he had kept to the edge of the room, silent as a fixture, watching the display with that unreadable patience of his. Now he stepped in past Ben, reached around Sera with two fingers, and altered one line in the index header.

ARCHITECT-GAVRAN

became

CUSTODIAN-GAVRAN

Kael withdrew his hand.

“Keeper,” he said. “If you want the older shade of it.”

No one spoke for a moment.

Ben looked at him. Not at the correction. At Kael himself. It was the first thing the man had offered without being pulled into it, and he had spent it on a title.

Torin’s expression changed by a fraction. Not surprise. More like a quiet acknowledgment of a line nobody else had seen until Kael drew it.

Sera said, “Yes.”

That was all. But her yes carried weight.

Ben looked back at the display. Architect would have made Gavran the author of the thing in the broad, sweeping sense. Builder. Designer. A man standing over history with both hands on it. Custodian was narrower and worse. It made him keeper of something he knew should not be seen.

Desh let out a breath. “That’s unpleasantly specific.”

Kael stepped back to the wall again. “Most useful words are.”

There it was: dry, stripped down, almost idle on the surface. Ben had the odd urge to laugh, not because anything was funny, but because the alternative was standing there while history rearranged the floor under his boots.

Sera opened the primary record. Her eyes moved once across the first paragraph. When she spoke, her voice had gone even flatter.

“This is Gavran’s protocol.”

Ben waited.

She did not give him a lecture. She did not give him comfort, either.

“Private penance-and-containment.”

The room seemed to tip.

Not physically. The floor stayed where it was. The lights kept burning. The archive kept humming. But Ben’s balance shifted in a way his body understood before his mind did, the same miserable fraction of wrong footing he had been getting around the wound since this mission started. He put one hand on the edge of the console without deciding to.

Penance.

The word hit harder than containment.

He looked from Sera’s unreadable profile to the old legal text to the modern summary on his datapad, and pieces that had refused to connect a minute ago began slamming together in ugly silence. The public Ruusan Reformations after the New Sith Wars had not been fake. They had rebuilt the Order. They had cut it away from the worst habits of the Jedi Lords. They had done exactly what history said they did.

And somewhere under that clean account, this older thing had been carried forward anyway.

Not proclaimed. Not taught in the broad version. Folded in. Buried deep enough that a Jedi Knight with decent training and a Skywalker name could stand in the middle of it and only now start to see the outline.

“What was it penance for?” Ben asked.

No one answered at once.

Torin’s warmth did not leave his face, but it drew tighter around the edges. “Something bad enough that sensible people began saying necessary before they said right.”

Sera’s hand rested on the console. Steady. Controlled. Ben had seen officers hold themselves that still before delivering casualty numbers.

Desh studied the two screens, old archive and modern reference side by side. “So the official history isn’t wrong.”

Ben shook his head slowly. “No.”

That would have been easier, maybe. Easier to hate. Easier to throw out. This was worse. This was looking at something true and feeling the hidden seam under it with your fingertips.

Torin touched one of the attached names on the display. Not long enough to select it. Just a brush, almost affectionate. “Veranthos had clerks, quartermasters, healers, line officers, temple staff. People drank bad caf and complained about rotations and borrowed each other’s boots. Then this file starts talking, and they become functions.”

Ben glanced at him. There was no performance in that line. Just a man remembering that the dead had been ordinary before institutions got hold of them.

Sera said, “Functions are easier to seal than persons.”

Kael’s gaze stayed on the word custodian. “That is why institutions prefer them.”

Ben swallowed. The archive no longer felt like a puzzle box or a cache of lost lore. It felt crowded. Not with ghosts. With human use. With hands on consoles, orders issued, objections ignored, titles chosen because they made the work easier to live beside.

He looked at Sera and saw the instrument the system had relied on, precise enough to make it function. He looked at Torin and saw the keeper of the people inside it, the one who still remembered they had names and voices and habits. He looked at Kael and saw the one place the record had already tried to smooth itself into something cleaner than it was, and the man who had refused that smoothing with a single word.

The Codex fragment glowed in its case. The archive lights washed the room in cold blue. Old machinery murmured through the walls.

Ben stood in the middle of it with his father’s history in one hand and this room’s in the other, and neither one canceled the other. That was what made it land. The archive had stopped being records. It had become witness, and whatever the penance answered to still had no name.
