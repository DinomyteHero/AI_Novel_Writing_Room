The structure rose from the barren plain like a bunker that had forgotten it was supposed to be hidden. Low and angular, its lines broken by centuries of wind-scour but its silhouette still military — still purposeful. A command post, not a temple. The kind of thing you built when you expected someone to try to take it from you.

Ben stopped fifty meters out, one hand resting on his blaster grip. The weight on his chest had been growing for the last kilometer, a dense pressure that sat behind his sternum and made each breath feel like he was pulling against something. Not pain. Not exactly. More like the moment before a storm broke, when the air went thick and your body knew something was coming before your mind could name it.

"Power signature?" he asked.

Desh crouched beside him, scanning the structure through his field glasses. "Active. Low-draw, but steady. That building's been running for a long time." He lowered the glasses, his face unreadable in the way it got when he was doing threat math. "Shouldn't be. Nothing out here should still have power."

"Mm."

"That's the bad kind of quiet."

"Yeah."

They crossed the remaining distance on foot, the terrain crunching under their boots — loose stone and the occasional brittle husk of something that might have been scrub grass before the climate turned. Ben's Force-sense was reaching ahead of him without conscious direction, probing the structure's edges, and what came back was wrong in a way he couldn't bracket. The pressure on his chest wasn't diffuse anymore. It was specific. Localized. The building wasn't just powered — it was *present*, radiating something that his perception kept sliding off, like trying to grip a surface that wasn't quite there.

The entry was a recessed blast door, half-retracted, its housing scored by what looked like old blaster fire. Ben ducked under it, Desh a half-step behind and to his right — covering the angle, sidearm still holstered but his hand never more than an inch from the grip.

Inside, the air was still and cold and tasted of recycled oxygen, the tang of a scrubber system that had been running longer than any machine had a right to. The corridor beyond the door was dark, but as Ben stepped forward, something flickered at the edge of his vision — a blue pulse of light that crawled out of the walls and across his body, slow and deliberate, like a hand reading a page it hadn't touched in a very long time.

He froze.

The light lingered. It pooled at his chest, at his hands, at his face — checking, comparing, hesitating. Ben felt the scan as a pressure distinct from the wrongness outside, a presence that was *looking* at him rather than pressing on him. Something in the mechanism's calibration didn't quite match what it was finding. The pulse stuttered, reconsidered, and then withdrew.

A low hum built in the walls. Displays that had been dark for centuries flickered to life along the corridor's length, their glow the sickly amber of old power cells pushed past their rated lifespan. The hum resolved into the deeper thrum of systems waking — not the smooth start-up of modern hardware, but the grinding, reluctant return of something that had been holding itself in standby for so long it had forgotten how to be anything else.

"Huh," Desh said, from somewhere behind him. "Guess it likes you."

"It didn't like me. It checked me." Ben was already moving deeper into the corridor, his eyes tracking the displays as they lit. The characters scrolling across them were archaic Basic, the syntax formal in a way that reminded him of the old Jedi Lords-era records his father kept in the Temple archives — the ones nobody read anymore because nobody remembered they were there. "Something in my signature didn't match what it was expecting. But it let me through anyway."

"Reassuring."

"Wasn't trying to be."

The corridor opened into a control center — a broad, low-ceilinged room ringed with consoles, their surfaces filmed with dust that had not been disturbed since before the Ruusan Reformations. The power cells hummed louder here, a deep vibration Ben could feel through the soles of his boots. A central display dominated the far wall, its screen cycling through a sequence of characters that didn't resolve into language — patterns, protocols, a message that had been repeating itself on frequencies nobody had monitored for a thousand years.

Desh drew his sidearm and swept the room's perimeter with the economy of motion that came from decades of doing this for real. His eyes moved across the consoles, the displays, the reinforced bulkheads, and Ben watched him read the architecture the way Ben read the Force — professionally, instinctively, building a threat model from the shape of the space.

"Command and control," Desh said. "Military. Not a temple, not a research station. Someone built this to run an operation." He paused at a console, brushing dust from its surface with the back of his hand. "Lord Gavran. The name's on half these displays. Whoever he was, he built to last."

Ben filed the name without comment. The facility would tell him what Gavran was; he didn't need to fill in the blanks yet. What mattered was the warning cycling through the dead frequencies — the pattern that wouldn't resolve, the message the systems had been trying to send since before his Order existed.

He crossed to the central display and let his fingers rest on the console's edge. The Force was wrong here — not the wrongness of the approach, the ambient pressure that had been building since they entered the system, but something sharper, something that had edges. The weight on his chest had become a pressure that pinned him to the moment, that made each breath a deliberate act.

The characters on the display shifted. The cycling pattern stuttered, found a new alignment, and the archaic protocol resolved into Basic — the syntax old, the letterforms slightly wrong, but the words unmistakable.

SEAL FAILING.

Ben's breath caught.

SEAL FAILING.

The message repeated, cycling through the dead frequency, and the wrongness in the room wasn't ambient anymore. It was the sound of something breaking. A warning that had been broadcasting for centuries, waiting for someone to hear it.

SEAL FAILING.

He read the words. He did not yet name what they protected against. The meaning hadn't reached him — only the weight, the pressure behind his sternum, the certainty that he was standing in front of something the modern Jedi Order did not know existed and the proof was cycling through a dead frequency in a language his Order had forgotten how to read.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet, the operational deadpan stripped back to something rawer. "What does that mean?"

Ben didn't answer. He was still reading the words, still feeling the weight of them settle into his chest like a stone dropped into deep water.

The message repeated.

SEAL FAILING.
