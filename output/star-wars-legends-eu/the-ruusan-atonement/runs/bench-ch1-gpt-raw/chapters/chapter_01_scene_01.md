Rendell’s training blade cracked across Ben’s left shoulder.

Pain flashed hot through the padded tunic. Ben pivoted on instinct, already knowing he was late. Not by much. Half a beat, maybe less.

Half a beat was enough.

He recovered into guard and forced himself not to glance at the spot Rendell had tagged. The salle lights threw a clean white sheen across the floor, and somewhere beyond the open practice lanes another pair of sabers hummed in uneven rhythm. Boots scraped. A wall unit clicked as it adjusted the air a degree cooler.

Rendell lowered his blade a fraction. “Point.”

“Yeah,” Ben said.

He moved first this time. A low cut, quick shift, shoulder turn into a rising feint that should have opened Rendell’s right side. Ben had run this exchange often enough that his body knew where the next opening would be before thought got involved. Usually the Force met him first, not doing the work for him, just arriving at the edge of his motion at the right instant.

Tonight it arrived after.

The beat slipped. His wrists corrected too late. Rendell’s block caught him off line, and the return tap struck Ben’s ribs.

Not hard. Training hard.

Still counted.

Ben stepped back before Rendell could say anything, letting his blade hum between them. Third time this week. Third time the same tiny failure had reached out and tripped him in full view of somebody else.

The first two times he had been alone, which had been considerate of the universe and therefore obviously temporary.

Rendell took a slow breath and reset his stance. He was good enough not to look pleased. “Again?”

Ben considered refusing on the grounds that he was very attached to what remained of his dignity. Instead he nodded.

They circled. Sweat had made his palms slick inside the gloves. He adjusted his grip. The salle’s familiar pressure in the Force should have settled around him by now—the ordinary Temple background, all those meditations and quiet thoughts and old habits layered into stone and air. It was there. Just wrong. The rhythm of it kept landing behind his movements, like accompaniment missing the lead by a fraction.

Rendell came in high. Ben parried, turned, cut low—

Late.

The training blade thumped against his forearm.

Rendell deactivated first. “Ben.”

That was worse than if he had sounded smug.

Ben thumbed his own saber off. The sudden silence where the hum had been left the salle too open. He rolled his shoulder once, because apparently if he moved like a person who had simply had an off round, reality might be polite enough to support the story.

“I’m done,” he said. “Long day.”

Rendell clipped his blade to his belt. “Want to run it tomorrow?”

Ben let out something close enough to a laugh. “So you can enjoy this properly after a night’s reflection?”

A corner of Rendell’s mouth twitched. “I was going to say so you can stop sulking in public.”

“I never sulk in public. I’m a Jedi.”

“Mm.”

Ben spread a hand. “See? Fatigue. My timing’s off. If I stay, you’ll develop confidence.”

“That would be a tragedy.”

It was exactly the right amount of not pushing, and Ben liked Rendell a little too much for it in that moment. “Go. Before I remember I outrank you in devastating remarks.”

“I’m not sure that’s a rank.”

“It should be.”

Rendell gave him a short nod and headed for the door. No lecture, no offer to talk, no careful Jedi concern. Just an exit. His boots faded into the corridor noise beyond the salle, where a lift chimed and someone on the shift laughed at something too far away to make out.

Ben waited until the doorway had emptied before he looked down at his left hand.

A slight tremor. Not much. Enough.

“Great,” he muttered to the empty room. “Very dignified.”

The salle had thinned out while they were sparring. One pair across the room shut down their session and left through the side corridor, voices low. A cleaning droid eased from its wall recess with a soft motor whine and began worrying at a scuff mark near the far practice circle. The place kept going, because of course it did. Temple life did not pause because one Knight had started losing arguments with timing.

Ben unclipped his saber and paced once along the boundary line, then back.

Fatigue was plausible. He’d had worse weeks. The Order had had worse months. Abeloth alone had bought everyone the right to a few bad nights and an uneven meditation cycle. He could have accepted that explanation if his body had not kept refusing to cooperate with it.

He stopped in the center of the floor and held the hilt low at his side.

All right.

No blade. No opponent. No excuses.

He drew a breath and started into the opening sequence of Form Three, slow and precise. Feet first, then hips, then shoulders, the movement built from old repetition until he no longer had to think about where his palm should be or how far to turn. His body knew. That had been the point of all those years in salles exactly like this one, under instructors patient enough to make sure he hated them for it at the time and appreciated them later.

The first pass went clean.

He turned and began again.

On the second rotation, the lag caught him.

Not in his muscles. In the space around them. The familiar pulse he had counted on for years—small, constant, usually a step ahead of him—came in late, a note flattened just enough to pull the whole phrase wrong. Ben’s right hand had already committed to the line of the cut before the Force arrived where it should have been.

He checked the motion too hard. The hilt clipped his forearm.

Ben hissed through his teeth and froze with both hands raised, one foot half turned on the polished floor.

For a second he stayed there, listening.

The distant corridor traffic. The cleaning droid’s brushes. The faint whir of the ventilation. His own breathing, too fast now.

And under it, still, that half-beat drag.

He lowered the hilt very carefully.

It wasn’t exhaustion. Exhaustion was messy. This wasn’t messy. It was maddeningly neat. Same delay. Same stolen fraction. Same wrong answer from a thing that was supposed to be the one answer he didn’t have to second-guess.

Ben tried the opening movement a third time.

Late.

Again.

Late.

His mouth went dry.

He stopped before the sequence could finish and stood in the middle of the empty practice circle with the deactivated saber gripped too tight in one hand. The pressure in his chest had nothing to do with the training hit now. He had been working around this all week—adjusting without thinking about it, blaming sleep, blaming distraction, blaming the aftertaste of too many bad years stacked on top of each other.

That option had just died on the salle floor.

“Fine,” he said under his breath. “Not me.”

The words didn’t feel especially triumphant.

He scrubbed a palm across the back of his neck and paced once more, shorter this time. There were procedures for reporting Force-field disruptions. There were meditations for instability, consultations, calm conversations in offices with people who would ask helpful questions in useful tones while he tried to explain something he could barely get hold of himself. He did not want any part of that. Mostly because the explanation sounded thin even in his own head.

There’s a lag in the Force.

He could hear exactly how that would sound.

Ben snorted once, without humor. Skywalker, congratulations. You have reached the stage where your private problem is beginning to sound hereditary.

The thought landed harder than he liked. He set it aside before it could go anywhere unhelpful.

He turned toward the wall mirrors, then away from them. No point checking his stance. The stance had never been the problem. He knew that now, and that should have been relief.

It wasn’t.

Because if it wasn’t him, then it was real.

And if it was real, someone else was going to notice. Someone else already had.

A presence touched the edge of his awareness—familiar enough that his body recognized it before the rest of him caught up. Ben looked toward the doorway.

Luke stood there with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
