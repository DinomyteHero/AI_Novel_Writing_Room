The training salle smelled of sweat and ozone, the faint tang of recycled air doing what recycled air always did at this hour — carrying the ghosts of a dozen earlier sessions through the vents. Ben had been working with Rendell for twenty minutes, long enough for the rhythm to settle into something his body knew by heart. Strike, parry, pivot. Strike, parry, pivot. The training sabers hummed their low, familiar note.

Rendell feinted high and came in low, a sequence Ben had seen him favor since they were both padawans running the same drills under the same Masters. Ben's guard should have dropped into the block before Rendell's wrist finished its turn. It should have been automatic.

Instead his arms moved a half-second late, and Rendell's blade caught him across the shoulder with a sharp *crack* of contained energy meeting cloth and skin.

The sting was clean and specific — the kind of hit that told you exactly how badly you'd misjudged the timing. Ben stepped back, rolling his shoulder against the ache, and for a moment the wrongness pulled at his attention like a note played slightly flat in a piece he'd heard a hundred times. The ambient Force in the salle arrived behind the beat his body expected. It had been doing that all week.

Rendell deactivated his training saber. "You all right?"

"Fine." Ben killed his own blade and offered a half-smile that cost him nothing. "That's three this week. I think you're owed a rematch."

"Whenever you want it." Rendell was still watching him, the way you watched a sparring partner who'd just taken a hit he shouldn't have taken. "You've been off all session."

"Late nights. You know how it is." Ben kept his tone light, the words landing somewhere between truth and deflection. He was tired. He had been tired since the week started. Fatigue was a reason Rendell would accept, and it had the minor virtue of being almost true.

Rendell hesitated — one beat, two — and then nodded, the way Knights nodded when they'd decided not to press. "Get some sleep, Skywalker."

Ben waited until Rendell's footsteps faded into the corridor, until the turbolift chimed its arrival and departure, until the salle was empty except for the hum of the climate control and a cleaning droid working its way along the far wall. The droid paused at a scuff mark, considered it with the gravity of a Jedi examining an ancient text, and moved on.

He paced the salle floor once, twice, letting his boots find the familiar worn spots in the decking. The wrongness sat at the edge of his perception like a half-beat lag he couldn't shake. He had been telling himself it was fatigue. He had been telling himself it was the week, the accumulated weight of late shifts and poor sleep and the kind of restlessness that came from spending too long inside Temple walls.

He had been telling himself a lot of things.

Ben stopped in the center of the salle and raised his deactivated training saber. The form was an old one — one of the basic sequences they taught initiates in their first year, the kind of thing his body knew well enough to run in his sleep. He moved through it slowly, deliberately, not igniting the blade. Strike. Pivot. Guard. Step. The motions were correct. His feet found their marks. His shoulders turned at the right angles.

But the Force arrived a half-second behind the rhythm his body had known by reflex for years.

He felt it in his chest first — a tightening that had nothing to do with exertion. The lag was there, in the ambient field, the way the salle's Force-presence should have risen to meet his movement and instead arrived late, like a musician coming in a beat after the rest of the ensemble. It wasn't him. It wasn't fatigue. It wasn't the week.

Something was wrong with the Force itself.

Ben didn't articulate the thought. He didn't have the words for it yet, and he wasn't sure he wanted them. The realization landed as a physical sensation — a sharpening of focus, a stillness in his breathing, the specific quiet that came when you stopped pretending a problem was temporary. He lowered the training saber and stood in the empty salle, listening to the wrongness the way he'd been avoiding listening to it all week.

It wasn't getting better. It had been getting worse.

He had been handling it alone because no one else seemed to feel it. Rendell didn't feel it. The other Knights he'd sparred with this week didn't feel it. Ben had mentioned it once, obliquely, to Master Cilghal after a meditation session, and she'd asked if he'd been sleeping. He'd said yes and dropped the subject. It was easier than explaining something he couldn't name.

The cleaning droid finished its circuit and retreated into its charging alcove with a soft click. Somewhere down the corridor, the late-shift foot traffic continued — the occasional tread of boots, the distant chime of a turbolift, the muted voice of someone passing on their way to somewhere else. The Temple at night was never quite silent. It was lived in, the way Coruscant was lived in, the way any place became when enough people moved through it long enough.

Ben stood in the center of the salle and let the wrongness sit at the edge of his perception without fighting it. He didn't have a name for what he was feeling. He didn't have an explanation. What he had was a half-beat lag in the ambient field and a growing certainty that it wasn't going to resolve itself.

The door hissed open behind him.

Ben turned. Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben had learned to read years ago — the particular stillness that meant a mission was coming, and that whatever it was, it wasn't going to be simple.

Ben felt the shift in his chest before he could name it: relief, reluctant and sharp, that he might not be alone with this after all. And underneath it, the cost of what came next. He was going to have to say it aloud.

Luke met his eyes and waited.
