Luke’s office smelled like recycled air and old caf, the same as it always had. Ben stepped through the door, and the Temple’s quiet settled around him in layers — the low wash of Coruscant traffic through the transparisteel, the distant murmur of an admin corridor somewhere below, the steady murmur of a holocomm on standby. Ordinary sounds. The kind that meant work was getting done and the Order was still standing.

Luke was already at the desk. He didn’t look up right away, finishing a line of notation on a second datapad before setting it aside. He waited three seconds, maybe four, before he slid the first one across the desk.

Ben picked it up.

Seventeen flagged reports, clustered along a bearing he recognized before he had fully named why. The Order’s survey network had been logging anomalies for weeks — Force readings that refused to settle into anything the standard frameworks could name. The flags were yellow, not red. Anomaly, not emergency. He scrolled down, and his shoulders drew tight.

“Outer Rim,” he said.

“Along the Corridor, mostly.” Luke folded his hands. “The reports started coming in about six weeks ago. Most of the analysts are calling it residual echo — post-Abeloth background noise that hasn’t settled.”

Ben zoomed in on three readings near the middle of the cluster. The wrongness was there in the numbers, in the way the Force readings drifted instead of holding, in the timing drift logged on two of the sensor arrays. He knew that drift. Not from a report. From his hands in the salle, from the half-beat lag that had been stealing his footing all week.

“You don’t think it’s residual echo,” he said.

Luke didn’t answer at once. The pause said enough.

Ben looked up from the datapad. His father’s expression was the one he used in briefings — level, measured, the Grand Master register that kept a room calm. But his hands stayed on the desk, not reaching for the other datapad, not calling up a holodisplay. Still.

“I’ve felt something,” Luke said. “For a few weeks now. Nothing I could put in a report without sounding like I was chasing shadows.” He paused again. “I didn’t want to put it in a report until I understood it better.”

Ben kept hold of the datapad. The wrongness in those three readings sat in his memory like a note heard through a wall — present, directional, wrong in a way that had nothing to do with volume. He had called it fatigue. He had called it bad sleep, a difficult month, the recycled air in the lower barracks.

“I’ve felt it too,” he said.

Luke looked at him. Not the Grand Master look — the other one, the one with fewer layers. Three seconds of it, maybe four.

Then Luke took the datapad from Ben’s hand, set it on the desk between them, and said, evenly, “I know.”

The two words landed differently than Ben had expected. He’d braced for confirmation, for the clinical version — anomaly detected, bearing established, Knight required. What he got instead was something quieter. His father had been sitting on this for six weeks, logging it the same way Ben had been logging it, both of them letting the days pile up and the readings cluster and the wrongness wait in the back of awareness like a door neither had opened yet.

Dad, he thought. Not the Grand Master. Just Dad.

He didn’t say it.

“The survey route runs directly along the bearing,” Luke continued. “Seventeen sites that need assessment anyway, legitimate Order business, and the anomaly sits underneath all of them.” He tipped his chin toward the datapad. “A Knight and a skilled pilot. Four to six weeks.”

Ben crossed to the window.

Coruscant spread out below — towers and transit lanes and the glittering grid of a city that had no interest in the Force’s private plumbing. A cargo speeder banked around a high-rise three levels down, running lights blinking in patient sequence. Someone’s ordinary day, carrying on.

The wrongness was faint here, in his father’s office, under the Temple’s harmonics — a sour edge at the limit of perception, patient and directional. He had been carrying it for three weeks before he admitted it wasn’t his alone. He had spent another week after that deciding what to do with the admission.

Now it had a bearing. Seventeen flagged reports and a survey route that ran straight along it.

“I’ll need to file a departure manifest,” he said.

“I’ll have the route parameters sent to your comlink by midday.”

Ben kept his eyes on the traffic below. Behind him he heard Luke pick up the second datapad, the one with the notation, and set it down again. Small sounds in a working office.

“The analysts aren’t wrong that it could be echo,” Luke said. “I want to know which it is.”

“And if it isn’t echo.”

“Then I want someone out there who can tell the difference.”

Ben let that settle. His father wasn’t wrong — there were Knights with more experience, more decorations, longer records. There were also Knights who would write up what they found in the language the frameworks could receive, who would describe timing drift and call the wrongness anomaly and let the reports close yellow. He didn’t know if that was what Luke was thinking. He didn’t ask.

“I’ll reach out to a pilot I know,” he said. “He can meet me at Terminus Station in two days.”

“Good.”

That was all. No call to take care, no offer of backup, no opening of the roster to suggest a second Jedi. The silence where that offer didn’t come was specific and weighted, and Ben took it in the same way he had taken the pause before the datapad slid across the desk.

He turned from the window.

Luke was watching him with that same expression — not the briefing face, not performing anything. Just his father, sitting at a desk in a working office on a working planet, with Coruscant traffic running its ordinary cycles outside and the wrongness lying faintly at the edge of both their perceptions.

“Sir,” Ben said.

Luke nodded once.

Ben picked up the datapad from the desk, clipped it under his arm, and walked to the door. He didn’t look back. Neither of them said anything about what the solo assignment meant, or didn’t mean, or cost. The door hissed open and the corridor swallowed him, and behind him Luke’s office returned to its ordinary quiet.

The wrongness came with him. It always did. But now it had a direction.
