The preflight was done. Ben sat in the cockpit with his hands folded in his lap, letting the hangar noise wash around him — a service tech calling clearance codes two bays over, the heavy percussion of a freighter’s landing struts settling somewhere behind the partition, the recycled-air smell of the Temple’s lower levels seeping through the ventilation. Ordinary sounds. He had been sitting with them for forty seconds longer than he needed to.

His comlink was already in his hand.

He brought up the contacts list. The Jedi partner roster sat right there — three names near the top, Knights he’d worked with before, competent and available and exactly what the departure manifest technically required. His thumb slid past them without stopping. He kept scrolling until he found Desh.

He pressed call before he had quite finished deciding to.

The comm crackled through two seconds of long-range static. Then:

“Skywalker.” Desh’s voice, flat and dry. “It’s late.”

“It is,” Ben said. “I need a pilot. Survey run, Outer Rim. Four to six weeks.”

A pause. Not hesitation — Desh sorting logistics. Ben could hear the shift of thought behind the silence, the faint background murmur of whatever ship or station he was on.

“How bad is the Outer Rim part?”

“Unknown.”

“How bad is the survey part?”

“Routine on paper.”

“Mm.” Another pause. “Terminus Station?”

“Two days.”

“I’ll be there.” Desh’s tone didn’t change, didn’t reach for the question hanging between them. “Bring caf. The station stuff is criminal.”

Ben’s mouth twitched. His shoulders loosened before he noticed the tension he’d been carrying there. “Done.”

“Good.” A beat of static. “See you in two days.”

The comm clicked off.

Ben set the comlink on the console. The hangar noise came back in — the service tech still running codes, the freighter’s engines cycling down, someone laughing somewhere near the fuel depot. His breathing had slowed without permission. The relief was immediate and faintly unsettling, the kind that came from a thing going easier than it should have.

He had worked with Desh since before either of them had the titles they wore now. They had worn the same gray uniforms at sixteen, learned the same rules in the same narrow corridors. The Galactic Alliance Guard had been dissolved before either of them had time to decide what they thought of it. What remained was the shorthand — a register that didn’t need setup or explanation, that understood the shape of an operation from three words and a bearing. Desh had heard survey run, Outer Rim and not asked why Ben wasn’t calling a Jedi. He wouldn’t. They both knew the answer, and neither needed to put it into words.

Ben started the ignition sequence.

The ship came alive around him — the low vibration of the repulsors warming, the nav-computer cycling through its pre-jump checklist, the engines building from a hum to a steady push. He ran through the departure protocol by feel, fingers moving through the sequence without pause. The Temple hangar’s ceiling lights slid overhead as the ship lifted, and then the bay dropped away beneath him and he was in the approach corridor, Coruscant’s transit lanes opening up ahead.

He took the orbital vector and climbed.

The wrongness was there as he cleared the Temple’s upper levels. Not louder — it never behaved that way — but closer, somehow, stripped of the building’s larger noise underneath it. A pressure at the edge of perception, the way a sound sits just below hearing. He had carried it for weeks. He knew its texture, the slight sour note in the current of the Force, the half-beat lag that could throw him off in the salle if he let it.

It didn’t diminish as Coruscant shrank behind him.

He marked that and kept moving. No reaching. No pushing back. Just the note, held and filed away.

The nav-computer confirmed the jump coordinates. Ben checked the readout, confirmed the first leg, and set his palms on the controls. Below him, Coruscant was a grid of light so dense it looked solid. Somewhere in that grid, his father was still at his desk, probably, still working. The survey route parameters had arrived on his comlink by midday, exactly as promised. The roster had stayed closed. Neither of them had said a word about it.

Ben pulled the hyperspace lever.

The stars stretched.

The wrongness stretched with them.

He felt the jump take — the ordinary universe dropping away, replaced by the blue-white corridor of hyperspace. The wrongness should have gone with it. That was how it worked. Disturbances bled off at the threshold; the tunnel of lightspeed was its own sealed current, cut off from the Force’s usual texture. He had made enough jumps to know the sensation, the slight clearing, the pressure easing as realspace receded.

This time it didn’t ease.

Ben’s fingers tightened on the controls. The wrongness was still there, and it was doing something it hadn’t done before. Not pressing outward in every direction. Not ambient. It was pulling, with a weight and a vector, steady and patient and aimed.

The instruments flickered — a single sweep across the sensor board, there and gone, the kind of ghost reading that meant nothing if you weren’t paying attention. He was paying attention.

He sat with it for a moment. The blue-white tunnel ran ahead of him toward the first jump point, seventeen survey sites and weeks of travel and a bearing he’d been trying not to name. The wrongness ran with it now, not behind him, not surrounding him. Ahead. Drawing him on.

His breath came out slow.

The Force did not do this. Disturbances scattered; they didn’t follow. They didn’t settle into a direction and hold it like a compass needle finding north. Whatever this was, it wasn’t the residual echo the analysts were logging. It wasn’t background noise.

It knew where it was going.

Ben watched the hyperspace tunnel stream past and kept his grip on the controls and said nothing, because there was nothing to say yet. The wrongness pulled, patient and specific, toward something waiting at the far end of the bearing.

He let it pull.
