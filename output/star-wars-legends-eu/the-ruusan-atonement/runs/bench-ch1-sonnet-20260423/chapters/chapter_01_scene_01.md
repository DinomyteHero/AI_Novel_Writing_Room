The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back hard enough to reset his footing.

The hit itself was nothing. A low-power practice saber, enough to sting through the tunic and leave a line of heat along the muscle. What landed was the fact of it. His guard had been up. He had been tracking Rendell’s feet, the left shoulder tell that always came before the overhand cut, the slight shift in his off-hand. He should have had the strike before it started.

He was a half beat late anyway.

Rendell lowered his blade. To his credit, he didn’t look triumphant, or even surprised. Just annoyed on Ben’s behalf, which somehow made it worse.

“Again?” he said.

Ben’s fingers were already moving toward the deactivation switch on his hilt. “I’m done for tonight.”

Rendell glanced at the chrono on the wall. It was early for done. They both knew it. “You’ve been off all week.”

“I know.” Ben clipped the training saber to his belt and kept his voice level. “Fatigue. I’ve been sleeping badly.”

Not a lie, exactly. He had been sleeping badly. That part was true enough to lean on. He watched Rendell weigh it, saw the other Knight decide it might be plausible, then decide it was also none of his business.

“Tomorrow,” Rendell said at last, and headed for the equipment rack.

Ben stood his ground until he heard the corridor door hiss open and close. A moment later the turbolift chimed out in the hall, distant and metallic. Then the salle settled.

He waited one more beat, then another.

The overhead lights hummed softly. Somewhere down the hall, a cleaning droid whispered across the durasteel floor, its brushes ticking over seams in a steady, patient pass. The Temple at this hour carried its own quiet, the kind built by people doing careful work for so long that the walls seemed to remember how to hold still. Most nights that steadiness wrapped around him without effort. Tonight it kept arriving just after it should have.

Half a beat. Always half a beat.

That was the problem. Not the hit — the hit was embarrassing, but survivable. The problem was the thing underneath it, the one that had been bothering him all week, the one he kept pinning on bad sleep and the aftertaste of a difficult month. On Taungsday he had blamed fatigue. On Zhellday he had blamed distraction. Three different times he had blamed the recycled air in the lower barracks, as if the ventilation system might be responsible for the way his attention kept slipping at exactly the wrong instant.

The Temple’s presence was supposed to be steady. A thousand years of meditation and duty soaked into stone and metal, a current you moved through without thinking, the way you moved through your own pulse. For most of his life it had been that way. Lately, though, something in it came to him just behind the rhythm his body expected.

He unclipped the saber again, held it unlit at his side, and started through the Soresu opening sequence.

Slow. Not a drill — he wasn’t drilling, not really. He moved through the form the way his mother had made him do it when he was twelve and irritated by repetition, slow enough that each position had to mean something, slow enough that he could feel what the form rested on. Soresu wasn’t only footwork and economy. It depended on timing, on a response arriving when the rest of him reached for it.

He flowed into the second position.

There it was. The lag. Like a note gone a shade flat, like the field itself had blinked and reopened a fraction too late. His body had already started the third motion on reflex, and the support it expected wasn’t there when it reached. His balance corrected before it could fail outright, but he felt the catch in his hands and shoulders all the same, a tiny lurch swallowed before it became a stumble.

He stopped.

Stood in the third position with the unlit blade in his hand, the cleaning droid still whispering down the corridor, the Temple’s quiet pressing in from every side.

It wasn’t him.

He had been telling himself it was him — telling himself the month had cost more than he wanted to admit, that three years of carrying things in silence had finally tipped into something his body couldn’t muscle through on stubbornness alone. He had been telling himself that the same way you tell yourself the bridge is fine while you’re still crossing it, because the alternative is to stop in the middle and look down.

But he had just run the sequence slowly enough to feel every step, and the gap showed up in the same place each time. Not his timing. Not his focus. The field. Whatever the Temple was supposed to be giving him, it was arriving late, arriving wrong, like a metronome that had started drifting by an almost invisible margin.

He lowered the training saber.

His chest had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with exertion. He drew a measured breath and let it out through his nose, then another. The sensation stayed put, stubborn and unpleasant, but he let it sit there without giving it a name. Not here. Not in an empty salle at the twenty-third hour with a cleaning droid for company and his own thoughts waiting like bad company.

Third time this week. Third time the wrongness had stolen a beat he should never have missed.

He should tell someone. He knew that the way he knew how to tighten a grip before a blade exchange: instinctive, immediate, and entirely without interest in whether he liked the answer. He also knew exactly why he wasn’t going to do it yet. Because the first explanation anyone else would reach for was the easy one. Fatigue. Stress. Residue from old injuries, old losses, old noise in the head that never quite went away. He could hear the conversation already, and he didn’t want it.

He wanted to be wrong. He wanted to sleep badly for another week and wake up with the sequence landing on time again, so he could tell himself the whole thing had been exhaustion and nothing more. Then he could go back to being the sort of Knight who noticed things and moved on from them.

His hands were steady when he clipped the hilt to his belt.

Footsteps drifted past in the corridor outside — two Knights, maybe three, heading toward the turbolift bay, their voices low enough to stay respectful of the hour. Someone laughed once, quiet and tired. The late shift was still at work; the Temple was still a living place, still full of traffic and cleaning routes and people pretending the night would hold together if they kept moving through it.

Ben looked at the empty wall space in front of him and shut off the last of the impulse to make it someone else’s problem.

The door behind him opened.

He felt the attention in the room before he turned — warm, broad, and unmistakably Luke Skywalker. Ben pivoted anyway.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand, and he wore the expression Ben knew meant a mission.
