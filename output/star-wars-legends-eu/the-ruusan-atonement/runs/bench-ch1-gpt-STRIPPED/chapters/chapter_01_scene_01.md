Ben's guard came up half a beat late, and the training blade cracked across his left shoulder hard enough to sting through the padded tunic.

He hissed and backpedaled on instinct, boot sliding on the salle floor. The green practice blade in his hand wavered, almost twisting loose from his sweat-slick grip.

"Point," the other Knight said, lowering his weapon.

Third time this week.

Ben reset his stance anyway, shoulder burning, pulse knocking in his ears. The training salle's lights threw clean white bars across the polished floor. Beyond the walls, the Temple carried on in its usual late-evening rhythm—distant lifts, a door cycling somewhere down the corridor, the low mechanical breath of circulation systems. Under all of it sat the thing that had wrecked his timing: that faint wrong note pressing at the edge of everything.

It bent the moment where his parry should have landed. Not much. Half a second. Less. Enough.

His partner circled in again, cautious now. Ben gave him a feint to the right, cut low, recovered high. Clean sequence. His shoulder complained. The other Knight caught the high cut and disengaged.

Ben let the exchange die. He thumbed his blade off. The hum vanished, leaving the room flatter than it should have.

"That's enough," he said.

His partner blinked. "You sure? We can go another round."

"Unless your goal tonight is to prove I'm distractible, I think the data set's solid."

That got the edge of a smile. "You look tired."

"Thanks. Always good to get professional feedback."

The Knight clipped his saber to his belt and gave a short bow. "Get some rest, Ben."

Sure. Rest. That solved everything from galactic war to bad footing.

Ben returned the bow because his mother had raised him correctly, even if the universe kept testing the lesson. "Night."

The door sighed open, then shut again behind the other man. Silence settled over the salle in stages. First the absence of the second set of boots. Then the missing blade hum. Then the awareness that the room was not silent at all.

His own breathing.
The whisper of fabric as he rolled his shoulder.
That off-pitch vibration underneath the Force, thin and persistent.

Ben stood still in the middle of the floor and listened to it.

Most people would have called the room calm. Temple calm, even. The place held its usual pressure in the Force: hundreds of lives stacked above and below him, sleeping, studying, healing, arguing in low voices, meditating with varying degrees of success. Familiar presences layered over architecture he'd known since childhood. Home, if a building full of Jedi and opinions counted.

Tonight the whole thing sat a fraction out of true.

Maybe everyone else heard it and had decided to be healthy about it. Maybe they were all doing the mature Jedi thing where you accepted lingering disturbances from recent galactic disasters and moved on with your life. Ben kept trying that. The problem was the wrongness kept reaching up and clipping him behind the knee.

He paced to the far wall and back, thumb dragging along the inactive emitter at his belt. No blade this time. No Force-assisted anticipation. Just movement.

He set his feet and ran through the opening Soresu sequence dry, wrists and elbows tracing the pattern from muscle memory. Guard high. Turn. Deflect. Recover. Shift left. Cut line. Reset.

Again.

On the second pass the hitch came at the same place it always did—not in his arm, not in his stance, but in the instant before action, where intention and response usually met cleanly. Tonight they rubbed together and missed.

Ben stopped. "Perfect."

His voice came back at him off the stone and practice mats.

He started again, faster. Form four transition, then a stripped-down hybrid he'd built years ago because life with Skywalkers had never favored doctrinal purity in combat. Pivot. Drop line. Shoulder turn. Reverse grip correction. His boots thudded harder now. Sweat tickled down his spine.

The hitch was still there.

So it wasn't fatigue. He'd hoped for fatigue. Fatigue was insulting, but at least it was ordinary.

He cut across the floor, grabbed a weighted practice dummy by the collar post, and shoved it out of his path. The base scraped loud enough to bark through the room. Too much force for a piece of equipment that had done nothing except exist where he didn't want it.

"Sorry," he muttered to the dummy.

No answer. Probably wise.

Ben planted himself in the cleared space and closed his eyes for three breaths—not meditation, not even close, just enough to strip away the visual clutter. The Force brushed against him in familiar channels: life nearby, currents moving through the Temple, the faint residue every old place gathered from years of purpose. Underneath all of it, the note dipped again.

Slightly flat.

That was the problem. Not loud. Not dramatic. A tiny failure of alignment that made everything around it work harder.

His hand tightened on the unlit hilt.

He knew this quality.

Not the panic and static of people still recovering from Abeloth's mess. Not the scarred edges left in places that had seen too much death, too much power, too many bad decisions by gifted idiots. This sat colder than that. Cleaner. The absence had shape.

Ben opened his eyes.

"No," he said to the empty room.

The word didn't help. Neither had the last dozen times he'd tried dismissing the thought.

He moved again, slower now, taking the forms apart piece by piece. If the disruption lived in muscle, it would show in the transitions. If it lived in his head, it would track with memory. If it lived in the Force—

His next turn stalled halfway through. Not from pain. Not from uncertainty. The feedback simply wasn't there for a blink, the tiny answering pressure he'd relied on since childhood, the thing that made motion and awareness lock together.

His arm completed the cut an instant later. Too late for a real fight.

Ben let the pose collapse and bent forward, hands braced on his thighs. Sweat dropped from his temple to the mat. His shoulder throbbed where the training blade had tagged him. He was breathing harder than the drill deserved.

That was the part he hated most. Not being wrong. He could survive being wrong. Skywalkers got plenty of practice at that. It was the possibility that he was right for the wrong reason.

Years around Jacen had left marks. Anyone who pretended otherwise was either lying or had suffered some very convenient head trauma. Abeloth had added her own collection. Ben had spent enough time near impossible things to know memory could cheat. You survived by building clean boxes around the past and labeling them useful. You did not go rummaging through them every time the Force made a strange noise.

He straightened and rolled his sore shoulder with care.

But this matched too well.

Around Jacen, in the worst moments, there had been flashes where the moral pull of the Force—the immediate recoil, the living answer that told you a line had been crossed—seemed to fall away before slamming back into place. Around Abeloth, there had been stretches where reality itself felt handled by someone with no respect for load-bearing walls. Different causes. Different scale. Same ugly family resemblance.

An imposed fault. A missing support. Not pain echoing from old scars.

Ben stared at the empty doorway, as if the Temple might take pity and provide a simple explanation on a service cart. Nothing came.

Fine. Of course not.

He clipped the training saber back onto his belt with deliberate care, making himself get the angle right on the first try. Small control. Better than none.

He could report it again. He probably would. Another calm conversation, another measured nod from someone older and wiser and less currently getting hit in practice. Yes, Ben, disturbances linger. Yes, Ben, everyone is carrying a lot after the past few years. Keep centered. Monitor it. Rest when you can.

Useful advice, in the way "apply pressure to wound" was useful while a bridge collapsed on top of you.

If he kept pushing alone, he might be wrong and look foolish.

If he didn't, he might miss the moment when wrong turned dangerous.

His mother would have told him to stop sulking and gather better intelligence. His father would have told him to trust what he heard in the Force without making himself the center of it. Ben, being the best of both worlds, preferred to collect evidence in private and sulk only where no one could grade the performance.

He paced once more, slower now, tracing the edge of the salle. The polished floor cooled through the soles of his boots. Recycled air touched the sweat on his neck. Beyond the wall, a distant chime marked the hour.

The note flattened again.

Ben halted mid-step.

There. Barely there, but distinct. A clean subtraction. The same void-edged quality that had brushed him in places no one should ever have had to stand.

His shoulders eased down a fraction, not from relief but from surrender to the fact. He'd been hoping for trauma, which was a sentence no sane person should ever think. Trauma was familiar. Trauma was his problem. This was not.

And he was still going to carry it alone for one more hour, one more day, until he had enough to bring someone besides a bad feeling and a bruised shoulder.

Because that was how Skywalkers stayed useful.
Because that was how Skywalkers got in trouble.
Because apparently he was still working on the difference.

The door opened.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
