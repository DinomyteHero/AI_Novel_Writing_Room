The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

Not hard — training sabers were calibrated to sting, not injure — but the impact didn't matter. The half-second did. Ben had seen the strike coming. He'd tracked the angle, read the shift in his partner's weight distribution, begun the parry. And then something in the Force had gone briefly, horribly flat, like a string pulled a quarter-tone sharp, and his arm had moved late.

Third time this week.

"Good hit," he said.

His partner — one of the newer Knights, whose name Ben kept forgetting to retain — lowered his training saber and looked at Ben with the particular expression of someone who'd just beaten a Skywalker and wasn't sure how to feel about it. "You want another round? I can dial back the—"

"I'm done for tonight." Ben deactivated his blade. "Early start tomorrow."

It wasn't entirely a lie. He did have an early start. He just didn't have any intention of sleeping through it.

The other Knight shrugged, clipped his saber to his belt, and headed for the door with the easy gait of someone whose evening had gone unexpectedly well. Ben watched him go without moving.

The salle settled into silence around him. Polished floor, high ceiling, the faint residual warmth of two bodies working through forms for the better part of an hour. The Temple's ambient presence pressed in from the walls — dozens of Force signatures distributed through the structure above and below, the low collective weight of Jedi going about their lives. He'd grown up with that weight. It was as familiar as the sound of his own heartbeat.

Underneath it, slightly wrong, the thing that had been there for six weeks.

Not a sound, exactly. Not a color or a shape. More the way a room feels when a piece of furniture has been moved two centimeters off its usual position — nothing visibly different, but the proportions are wrong and your eyes keep sliding back to the gap. Ben had tried to describe it to Master Cilghal three weeks ago. She'd listened carefully, asked the right questions, and told him that post-Abeloth residue was still presenting in sensitive individuals across the Outer Rim corridor, that the Council was monitoring it, that he should log any significant spikes in his daily reports.

He'd logged two. The wrongness had spiked eleven times since then.

He started moving through Shii-Cho, blade unlit, boots scuffing the floor in the familiar geometry of the form. The point wasn't the form. The point was whether the wrongness tracked his body or his Force sense — whether it disrupted muscle memory or timing.

The answer, as it had been every time he'd run this test, was timing.

His footwork was clean. His weight transfers were clean. Every mechanical element of the sequence landed exactly where it should. But the places where the form connected to the Force — the micro-adjustments, the half-conscious reads of space and momentum — those were where the wrongness bled through. A quarter-second of static at the transition between third and fourth position. A gap in the feedback loop at the end of the extension. Small enough that a competent opponent wouldn't notice. Large enough that a good one would score.

Ben stopped at the end of the sequence and stood still.

He'd been around Jacen's experiments long enough to know what Force lattice disruption felt like. He'd been at Abeloth's fractures long enough to know what structural damage felt like. Both had left marks — not trauma, not exactly, but a kind of calibration. The way a pilot who's flown through a bad ion storm learns to recognize the early warning signs of another one.

This wasn't residue. Residue was emotional. Residue had texture and weight and the particular flavor of specific suffering. Residue faded.

This was something underneath the residue. Something in the architecture.

He crossed the salle to the viewport and pressed one palm flat against the transparisteel. The glass was cool and faintly vibrating with the city's distant traffic — Coruscant never stopped, not even at this hour, and the hum of a billion lives going about their business pressed up through the superstructure of the Temple like a tide. Somewhere below, a transport lane was backed up. He could see the lights strung out across the lower atmosphere, patient and orderly.

The wrongness was there too. Faint, but there. Not localized to the salle or the Temple. Distributed.

He'd checked the post-Abeloth monitoring reports. He'd pulled the Council's summary data on Force-sensitive disturbances across the Outer Rim corridor. He'd cross-referenced the spike timing against known anomaly clusters and found nothing that matched the pattern. The reports described emotional resonance, trauma echoes, localized dark-side saturation in specific planetary bodies. None of them described what he was feeling, which was less like a wound and more like a load-bearing wall developing a crack that no one had noticed yet because the ceiling hadn't fallen.

The Order was treating it as background noise from recent crises. Ben understood why. They'd had a lot of crises. They were tired. The wrongness was subtle enough that most people registered it as general unease and moved on, the way you stop noticing a persistent low-grade vibration from the building's ventilation system after the first week.

He'd been noticing it for six weeks. He was still noticing it.

The pragmatic move was to escalate. Write a formal report, request a full Council review, push until someone with more authority than Cilghal took it seriously. His mother would have done exactly that, and she'd have done it in the first week.

He hadn't. He'd logged two entries and run his own tests and kept the rest to himself. Standing here with his palm on the cool glass, he could at least be honest about why: because the alternative was being the Skywalker who cried wolf. Who saw shadows that weren't there because he'd spent too many years around people who cast very large ones. Jacen. Abeloth. His own father's history. The Order was still rebuilding its confidence in Skywalker judgment, and Ben had no interest in spending that capital on something he couldn't prove.

So he was handling it alone. Because that was what you did.

He stayed at the viewport for another minute, watching the traffic lanes. The wrongness pulsed once, faint and off-key, and his hand on the glass registered nothing — just the cool vibration of the city, just the ordinary physics of a world that didn't know anything was wrong.

Maybe nothing was.

He'd considered that too. He considered it regularly, with the same systematic thoroughness he applied to everything, and he kept arriving at the same answer: three missed beats in a week, all at the same type of moment, all correlated with spikes in the same underlying disturbance. Ben was many things, but he wasn't sloppy with data.

The salle door opened.

He turned.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew.
