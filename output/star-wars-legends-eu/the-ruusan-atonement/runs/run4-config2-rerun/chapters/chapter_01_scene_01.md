The Force had been off-key for three months.

Ben Skywalker sat cross-legged on the meditation chamber’s stone floor, palms resting on his knees, and tried to ignore the discord. The room offered everything a sanctuary should: cool air, low amber light, and walls that had absorbed decades of contemplative practice until they hummed with residual calm. He sensed the Temple around him the way one feels a familiar house in the dark—the distant presences of other Knights, the deep structural quiet of the Archives two levels down, and the soft collective murmur of the galaxy moving through the Force like a river over smooth stone.

Except for the note.

It sat beneath the ambient hum and the distant traffic bleeding through the Temple's outer walls. A frequency no one had tuned to, playing constantly. It wasn't loud or dramatic. It was merely persistent and irrevocably wrong—the way a stringed instrument sounds when a peg has slipped a quarter-turn and the player hasn't noticed.

Ben exhaled. He reached for a standard technique: focus on breath, release peripheral awareness, let the Force settle into clarity. He’d done this ten thousand times. He’d done it in the field with blaster fire ricocheting off durasteel. He’d done it in the aftermath of Abeloth, when the Force felt like a lake recovering from a thrown stone, all disrupted surface and disturbed sediment.

This was different. Every time he approached stillness, the note waited for him. The silence only made it louder.

He opened his eyes.

The amber light remained steady. The stone felt cool against his palms when he pressed them flat against the floor. He sat for a moment, teeth gritted, and let himself feel the weight of it—three months of being the only one who couldn't unhear the noise.

He’d mentioned it to Corran Horn six weeks ago, framing it as a careful question: *Have you noticed anything unusual in the deep background, structurally speaking?* Corran had given him a patient, measured look—the expression of a man taking a student seriously without believing a word he said. Abeloth had left echoes. The dark side had been churned up. These things took years to settle. Ben was, Corran had not quite said, perhaps still processing.

Ben had nodded as if he understood. As if that made sense. As if he hadn't been trained since childhood to recognize the difference between emotional static and structural failure.

He’d tried Master Cilghal next, approaching from a clinical angle. He asked about documented cases of Force disturbances with a persistent harmonic signature rather than an emotional one. She’d been interested for ten minutes before the conversation drifted into post-Abeloth neurological studies and the correlation between trauma and heightened sensitivity.

He got the message. He stopped asking.

The Temple's ambient hum pressed in, warm and familiar. Everyone else heard the same river they’d always known. He was the only one with a bad ear—or the only one with a good one. He tried not to frame it the second way; that road led toward a dangerous arrogance.

His father perceived the Force at a depth others couldn't. Luke had seen things coming that no one else had, sensing the shape of events before they crystallized, trusting those perceptions even when the Council demurred.

And Jacen had heard things no one else heard, too. At the end, he’d been very certain about what they meant.

Ben’s hands curled against the stone. He straightened them deliberately, one finger at a time.

The logic was sound. He’d endured a difficult few years. The psyche fractured under sustained pressure. Heightened sensitivity to ambient disturbance was documented, clinical, and explainable. Everyone said so.

The note played on.

He tried to characterize it the way a mechanic might describe an engine knock—not what it felt like, but what it was doing. It wasn't emotional. It lacked the resonance of a dark side presence, which carried a specific, oily heat. It wasn't the fading echo of a wound in the Force, like Ruusan or Malachor. Those had always struck him as scar tissue: present, altered, but static.

This moved. Not with the intent or breath of a living mind, but with the cyclic rhythm of a structure under load. It was the vibration of a beam bearing weight it could no longer support.

He’d felt something like it twice before. Once in the months before Jacen’s final fall, when the Force around his cousin started to carry a particular quality of wrongness—not the dark side itself, but the architecture of something being built. And once, briefly, near the end with Abeloth, when the Force felt as if it were straining at its own edges.

This was quieter. More distant. But it possessed the same character.

Something was failing, and it had been failing for at least three months. It was getting worse.

Ben stopped trying to meditate.

He sat in the amber light and let the conclusion settle. This wasn't a trauma response or an ambient echo. It had a source and a direction, if only he could find the right angle to listen from. The Temple’s collected calm wasn't going to resolve this because the Temple wasn't the problem.

The Order had suffered. Everyone was tired. The Council managed a hundred active concerns and the institutional momentum of recovery. When a Knight mentioned a persistent harmonic disturbance in the deep background, the rational response was to file it under post-crisis sensitivity and move on. He understood that. He’d have done the same.

But he’d spent years learning the specific way the Force felt when something was wrong at a level deeper than individual darkness. He’d learned it in the worst possible classrooms, and he trusted that education even when he didn't want to.

He would have to act alone. Explaining the structural quality of the disturbance to someone who couldn't hear it would take longer than finding the source himself. Besides, the last time he’d trusted someone else’s read on a deep Force disturbance, that someone had been his cousin. Jacen had been very wrong about what it meant.

The thought landed with its usual weight. He sat with it, then pushed it aside.

The chamber door chimed.

Ben turned as the door slid open. His father stood in the frame, backlit by the corridor light, holding a datapad. Luke Skywalker’s expression was one Ben had filed under *mission*—not urgent or alarmed, but settled. It was the look of a man who had made a decision.

Ben stood. The wrongness remained, playing its off-key note beneath the world, but now there was a direction.

Luke’s eyes met his. Ben saw the moment his father registered the tension in his face—a flicker of recognition, the way Luke looked when he read something in the Force that others missed.

"We have a situation," Luke said.

Ben nodded. He could feel the shape of it. Whatever was failing was about to become his problem in a way he could finally touch.

He stepped toward the door.
