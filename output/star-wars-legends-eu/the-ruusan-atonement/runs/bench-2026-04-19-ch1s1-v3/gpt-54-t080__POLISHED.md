Ben's guard came up a half-second late.

The training blade cracked across his right shoulder with a flat, stinging snap. Pain flared through the padding and into the joint. His own saber was still moving toward the parry that should have met the strike cleanly.

Too late.

He killed the blade on reflex and stepped back, rolling the shoulder once as the sting spread. Across from him, the other Knight lowered her training saber and dipped her head in brief acknowledgment of the point.

"Again?" she asked.

Ben let out air through his nose. "You trying to build a reputation off this?"

"One hit isn't a reputation."

"Third one this week says you should start printing cards."

The line landed well enough. Light, easy, almost normal. He was good at almost normal.

The Knight gave him a short smile and reset her stance anyway, practical enough not to take the joke as a refusal. The salle lights threw pale bars across the polished floor. Around them, the late session had thinned to almost nothing; one of the far practice circles stood empty, and beyond the doors the corridor traffic had that end-of-shift rhythm—boots, a lift chime, voices too muffled to make out.

Ben raised his saber.

The familiar current that should have met him there arrived a beat behind his body.

Not gone. Worse than gone. Present, but out of step, the way a song turned wrong when one instrument lagged the rest of the ensemble by a fraction. Just enough to pull at his timing. Just enough to make every move ask a question it had never needed to ask before.

The Knight came in high. Ben met it, slid off, cut low. Their training blades hissed and snapped together. For three exchanges he held the rhythm by force of habit and plain old muscle memory, which was a sentence no Jedi wanted attached to his name if there were alternatives.

Then the lag tugged again.

His partner saw the opening and checked her strike before it landed full force, tapping his ribs instead.

Ben shut his blade down and stepped out of range. "That's enough."

She frowned. "You sure?"

"Unless the Council's issuing replacement shoulders now."

That got another small smile, but she kept studying him. Ben hated that part. Not concern itself—concern was fine, healthy, very Order-approved. It was the moment right before concern became a question he didn't want to answer.

"You're off tonight," she said.

"Tonight?"

Her brow lifted.

"Fair," Ben said. He hooked the deactivated saber to his belt. "Late week."

"We can run it back tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

The Knight hesitated, then nodded and headed for the exit. The doors sighed open and shut behind her, and the salle changed shape with her absence. The space felt bigger at once, the empty rings wider, the ceiling higher. The training blade hum was gone. What remained was the whisper of the Temple's ventilation, the distant pulse of traffic through the corridor walls, and the soft, methodical whir of a cleaning droid beginning its evening crawl somewhere outside.

Ben stood still until her footsteps faded.

Late week. Good answer. Clean answer. Maybe next he could blame gravity and see if anyone bought that.

He flexed his right hand. Sweat had dried tacky across his palm beneath the glove. The shoulder still burned where the blade had caught him, not enough to matter, just enough to mark the miss.

Three times this week.

No. More than that. Three times it had cost him cleanly enough to count.

He started pacing the edge of the nearest practice circle, boots brushing faintly over the salle floor. The Temple had a particular feel at this hour, not quiet exactly, but stripped down to essentials. The rush was over. The people still moving through it had places to be and reasons to go there. Knights coming off duty. Padawans squeezing in one last session before sleep. Service droids with routes to complete. The whole building carried on around him, solid and busy and certain.

Ben unclipped the saber again but didn't ignite it.

If it was fatigue, he'd know. If his body was slipping, he'd know that too. There were only so many ways to embarrass yourself in a training salle, and most of them were familiar.

He took a breath, settled his feet, and began the first form.

No blade. No opponent. Just line, turn, cut, recover.

The motions lived in his muscles from years of repetition. His shoulders found the angles. His wrists rolled through the transitions. His weight shifted where it should. The first sequence went clean. So did the second.

Then he let the Force in a little more, the way he always did without thinking, and the room answered a fraction late.

His next step landed wrong.

Not a dramatic stumble. Nothing worth a holovid replay. But his heel clipped the floor, his balance hitched, and the invisible blade in his hands drifted off line before he corrected it.

Ben stopped.

A cleaning droid rolled past the open strip of corridor outside, brushes ticking softly over stone. Somewhere farther off, a pair of voices traded a tired joke and vanished around a corner.

He reset and tried again.

First sequence. Fine.

Second. Fine.

Third—

There. That drag again. Not in his legs. Not in his shoulder. In the handoff between intent and motion, where the Force should have been threaded through both so cleanly he never had to notice the seam. Tonight the seam scraped.

He cut across, pivoted, brought the blade hand back—

The half-beat slipped.

His empty saber hilt knocked against his own forearm with a hard plastic tap.

Ben lowered it and stared at the floor.

That was not fatigue.

He knew fatigue. Fatigue was heavy limbs, slower recovery, the ugly little bargain where you gave up precision to keep moving. This was precision itself going crooked for an instant, as if the room kept answering from one step farther away than it should.

He tried to laugh at himself and got nothing out of it.

Great. Excellent. Son of Luke Skywalker, defeated by bad timing and whatever this was. Family legacy was apparently less a shining banner and more an endless invitation to compare your worst day to everyone else's best stories.

He scrubbed his free hand over the back of his neck and resumed pacing. Faster this time. The sting in his shoulder had faded, replaced by the cooler, meaner discomfort of not trusting his own reactions. That bothered him more than the hit. A lost sparring point was embarrassing. Unreliable instincts were dangerous.

He had mentioned it. Sort of.

Comments during meditation. A question in passing after drills. Had anyone else noticed the drag in the Temple, the slight lag, the way the Force in some rooms seemed to arrive a touch behind the moment? He'd gotten shrugs, thoughtful frowns, one suggestion to sleep more, and the general background answer of the last few years: after everything the galaxy had been through, after Abeloth and the rest of it, everyone was still a little raw. Disturbances lingered. Sensitives overread things. Give it time.

Maybe they were right.

Except his shoulder still hurt where a training blade had just proved they weren't.

He moved back to the center of the ring and took his stance once more. Sweat cooled under his tunic. His fingers settled around the saber hilt. He listened without closing his eyes, because he didn't need meditation posture for this and because if he sat down right now he might stay there out of spite.

The salle answered him.

Late.

That tiny delay brushed along his awareness again, too small to name cleanly and too steady to dismiss. It sat in the room's pulse, in the familiar background presence of the Temple, in the current every Jedi learned to trust before they had words for trust. A beat missing where no beat should be missing.

Ben drew the hilt through another slow cut.

The lag stole the return.

His weight shifted to compensate. His left foot skidded half an inch. He caught himself before he could make a full idiot of it, but the break was there, plain as the sting on his shoulder.

He went still.

For a few seconds he stayed in guard, saber dark in his hand, chest rising and falling. Then he let the stance drain out of him and backed to the wall. The stone at his shoulder blades felt cool through the damp fabric of his tunic.

So that was done.

No more pretending this was a rough week. No more stuffing it into the same box as lack of sleep and too much Temple caf and the usual post-crisis static everybody joked about because the alternative was admitting none of them had fully caught up yet. Whatever was off, it was real. It had been taking pieces out of his timing for days, and now it had done it in front of another Knight.

He looked toward the doors.

For one sharp, uncharitable second, he wanted someone else to walk in, watch one pass, and say it first. There. That. You're not imagining it. He wanted not to have to package it, defend it, or hear himself sounding like he was bringing a ghost story to the Order.

The second passed.

Ben pushed off the wall and straightened, thumb brushing the ridges of his saber hilt. Fine. If nobody else heard the missed beat yet, he would. He'd keep listening. Keep testing. Keep his mouth shut until he had something better than I know what I'm hearing.

The doors slid open.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
