The cockpit lights had gone from amber to green three minutes ago. Preflight sat complete across the console in neat columns. Fuel load, nav solution, traffic clearance pending, hyperdrive status clean. Everything ready except the part Luke had left unsaid and Ben had volunteered not to hear.

His datapad rested against his thigh, blue screen open to the Temple roster. Three names waited at the top of the recommended partner list. Good Knights. Competent. Available on short notice.

His thumb slid past the first.

Past the second.

It slowed at the third, hovered a beat, then kept going until the roster tab vanished and his personal contacts opened in its place.

The cockpit held the sounds of an idling ship and the Temple hangar beyond it: distant repulsor whine, a clank of cargo gear, voices too far off to make out as words. The bay was never silent. Right now it felt empty anyway.

Practical, he told himself, tapping the comm icon beside Desh Rolan's name. Desh knew fieldwork. Desh knew how to keep his head when things got strange. Desh didn't need Force impressions translated into something useful because he'd never expected the galaxy to sort itself into useful categories in the first place.

Also, Desh wasn't a Jedi.

Ben kept his face still and keyed the long-range burst.

The connection took a moment to claw its way through relay traffic. Static brushed the speaker. Then Desh's voice came through, flattened by distance and old comm hardware.

"If this is a social call, Skywalker, your timing's offensive."

Ben leaned back in the pilot's seat. "Good. I was aiming for consistency."

"That narrows it down to you or one of my former creditors."

"Relax. I'm not asking for money."

A scrape sounded over the line, as if Desh had shifted a chair with his boot. "Pity. That would've been easier."

Ben looked once at the dark canopy above the console, catching his own reflection in it. "You working?"

"I am now."

That got a short breath out of him, close enough to a laugh to count. "I need a second set of hands on a survey run. Outer Rim route, uncertain duration, low chance of comfort. Moderate chance of somebody shooting at us."

"Only moderate?"

"I'm trying to be respectful of your age."

Desh made a sound that might have been a snort. "Ship?"

"Shadow-class patrol craft. Temple loaner."

"That's your polite way of saying the bunk is terrible."

"It builds character."

"I already have enough character."

Ben brought up the launch checklist and sent the final ping to hangar control. Clearance code scrolled across the board a second later. Ahead of him, the bay doors began to cycle, durasteel plates separating with a slow hydraulic groan that rolled through the hull.

On the speaker, Desh said, "When do you leave?"

"Now."

"Coordinates?"

"I'll transmit once I'm clear of Coruscant traffic."

A brief pause. Not surprise. Not irritation. Just the space where another person might have asked why a Jedi Knight was calling a private security contractor for a mission that had started in the Temple.

Desh didn't.

Of course he didn't.

Ben had known him in rooms where nobody asked the useful question because asking it put the wrong thing on record. GAG briefing rooms with recycled air and stained caf dispensers, officers moving mission markers around a tactical display while words like containment and deniability did work they had no business doing. Desh had sat through those with the same dry face and steady hands, and afterward he'd gone out and kept people alive inside orders that deserved better language than they got.

That history settled in over the comm line without either of them touching it.

Desh broke the pause first. "You want me armed for survey trouble or Jedi trouble?"

Ben's hand stayed on the throttle. "Pack for both."

"That's the worst answer, so I assume it's honest."

"It usually is."

"Then send the pickup point. I'll meet you there."

No questions. No polite concern. No offer to bring anyone else. Desh accepted jobs the way some people checked the weather: glance, assess, move on.

Ben said, "Thanks."

"Don't make it sentimental." A beat. "And Ben?"

"Yeah?"

"If this crate has one of those refresher units that sprays cold water in moral protest, I'm billing extra."

The line clicked dead before Ben could answer.

He sat for half a second with the speaker hissing softly at him. The call had a familiar ease to it, quick and clean. Desh took the angle off things. Made decisions feel like work instead of confession.

Ben locked the datapad to the console and brought the repulsors up.

The ship lifted from the deck on a low, steady hum. Hangar floodlights slid across the canopy as he guided the patrol craft out of its berth and into the traffic lane. Temple walls fell away behind him in pale stone and steel, all severe lines and old confidence. Ahead, the doors finished opening on Coruscant night.

He eased the ship through the gap.

The city hit him at once through the hull and controls: traffic beacon chatter, lane markers flashing, the distant vibration of heavy transport moving under the smaller currents. Coruscant spread in layers of light, towers stacked against towers, skylanes braided in red and white. Even sealed inside the cockpit, he could taste the dry metallic edge of the life-support system working harder as the engines climbed.

Under it all, the wrongness rode with him.

Thin. Sour. Not pressure, not malice. More like a missing tooth his tongue kept finding.

He merged into the Temple departure corridor, rolled the craft starboard around a stream of civilian speeders, and climbed through authorized ascent vectors. The console painted clean lines. Altitude. Velocity. Orbital slot assignment. Normal numbers. Reliable numbers. He liked reliable numbers.

His thumb brushed the edge of the datapad where the Temple roster still sat one tab away.

Three available Knights.

Any of them would have made sense. Better than sense, probably. Another Jedi would have understood what he was looking for without translation. Another Jedi would have felt the same drag under the Force and helped pin down where it changed. Another Jedi would also have seen him hesitate, second-guessed his calls, watched for the places where old memories bent his judgment.

Practical, he told himself again, and heard how thin it sounded now.

Desh wouldn't crowd the mission with Force theory. Desh would watch doors, read people, handle the solid parts. A clean division of labor. Efficient. Safe.

Safe from what stayed unspoken.

Coruscant's upper atmosphere thinned to black. Stars burned into view beyond the traffic haze. Ben cleared orbital control, angled the nose toward his outbound vector, and fed the astrogation computer the first leg. The route would take him out along one of the major corridors before he cut spinward toward the flagged reports. Fast enough. Ordinary enough not to draw notice.

He checked the coordinates, checked them again, then dropped his hand over the hyperdrive lever.

For a moment he let himself lean into the Force, just enough to test the shape of that wrong note before the jump. Usually hyperspace blunted local impressions. Distance and transit noise smeared things out. If this was tied to Coruscant, or to his own nerves, it should fade the second realspace peeled away.

Ben pulled the lever.

Stars snapped into lines. The ship gave one hard lurch as the motivator engaged. Instruments flickered across the board, nav display dimming and returning in a quick pulse that made his hand tighten on the controls.

Then the tunnel of hyperspace steadied around him, blue-white and endless.

The wrongness stayed.

Ben went still.

Not memory. Not the hangar. Not bruised pride from the salle, or too little sleep, or his head stitching old disasters onto a new problem because that was what scar tissue did when it got bored.

The drag under the Force remained through the jump, and now that local clutter had dropped away, it changed.

It turned.

Not stronger. Cleaner. The vague pressure that had been spread across everything drew itself into a line. His attention followed before he wanted it to, angling off his plotted route by a fraction so slight the nav computer didn't care and his own body did. Westward on the display, spinward in real terms, somewhere beyond the first leg he had entered.

His fingers moved over the board, not changing course yet, only marking the heading. The numbers sat there, patient and cold.

Ben stared into hyperspace and felt the wrongness settle with quiet certainty, like a compass needle finding north.
