The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Third time this week.

He held the position a beat longer than necessary — weight on his back foot, blade hand low, the training saber's practice hum cycling down in his grip. His sparring partner lowered her blade and said nothing, which was professional of her. She had the decency to look like she was checking her own stance rather than watching him absorb the hit.

Ben rolled his shoulder. No real damage. The cortosis-weave practice blade had stung through his tunic, nothing more. The problem wasn't physical.

"Good session," he said.

She nodded, and he could see her recalibrating — wondering whether to push for a rematch, wondering if something was off. He gave her his best impression of tired-but-satisfied, the expression that said *long day, not your fault, let's call it*. She bought it. Most people did.

"Early start tomorrow," he added. "You go ahead."

The salle door sealed behind her with a soft hydraulic hiss.

Ben stood in the middle of the floor and exhaled.

The practice blade had found him because he'd been half a beat behind his own read on the engagement. He'd seen the angle of her shoulder drop, processed the feint correctly, started the counter — and then the Force had slipped. Not like losing his grip on something. More like pressing a key on an instrument and hearing the note come back wrong. Not absent. Wrong. The pitch off by enough to make his hand hesitate where it should have moved.

That hesitation was all she'd needed.

He deactivated the training saber and set it on the equipment rack. The salle stretched empty around him — high ceilings, pale stone, the faint amber glow of Coruscant's perpetual sky-haze filtering through the upper windows. Late enough that the other training rooms had gone quiet. He could feel the rest of the Temple as a background presence, dozens of Force signatures settling into evening routines, none of them close.

None of them feeling what he was feeling.

Ben pulled his tunic straight and walked the perimeter of the room. Not pacing — he was clear with himself that it wasn't pacing. Perimeter assessment. He did it twice, letting his body settle, then stopped at the center line and ran through the opening sequence of Shien without igniting the blade. Muscle memory only. No Force assist.

Clean. Every transition precise, every weight shift where it should be. His body knew the forms the way he knew his own name.

He moved into Djem So. Still clean. Faster than the Shien sequence, the footwork more aggressive, and his body handled it without complaint. He pushed the pace until he was moving at half-speed combat tempo, blade hand tracking phantom opponents through the empty air.

Fine. He was physically fine.

He stopped and stood still.

The wrongness was still there. Low and persistent, that flat undertone beneath everything — as though the foundational note the rest of the Force was built on had dropped just slightly out of true. His muscle memory worked because muscle memory didn't need it. His Force-assisted timing — the half-second of prescience that turned a good fighter into a Jedi Knight — drew on something deeper, and that deeper thing was off.

Ben had spent enough time around Jacen to know what Force damage felt like. He'd been at Centerpoint, at Fondor, on Coruscant during the coup. He knew the particular texture of large-scale Force disruption — the way it registered as wrongness in the gut before the mind caught up. And he'd been through Abeloth, which had been something else entirely, something that had left every Jedi in the Order scrubbing the residue out of their meditations for two years.

This wasn't either of those things. Not localized damage, not Abeloth's specific corruption. It was more like a crack in a load-bearing section — invisible from outside, not yet catastrophic, but present. The kind of thing that didn't show up until you tried to put weight on it.

He ran the opening of Shien again. And again. Each time, the forms held. Each time, the wrongness sat underneath them like a floor that wasn't quite level.

The Order's consensus was that post-Abeloth disturbances were fading. Master Cilghal had published analysis suggesting the Force was stabilizing. Ben had read it. He'd nodded in the right places during the briefing. He hadn't argued, because what was he going to say — *I think you're all missing something, and I think it's big, and I know how that sounds coming from me specifically*?

He knew how it sounded. He was a Skywalker. Skywalkers had a documented history of perceiving galaxy-ending threats that everyone else thought were manageable. He'd grown up watching his father get that look — the one that meant Luke had felt something no one else had felt yet, and was trying to decide how much alarm to express without triggering the kind of institutional response that made things worse.

Ben did not want to be that person. He'd spent a significant portion of his adult life actively not being that person.

He stopped moving.

The wrongness pulsed once — not stronger, just more distinct, the way a sound clarifies when the background noise drops. He'd noticed it getting clearer over the past few weeks. Not louder. Clearer. The way a bad signal resolved into recognizable static right before the transmission died.

His Jacen comparison had been accurate, and he hadn't wanted it to be.

With Jacen, the wrongness had been personal — a darkness threading through his cousin's presence, intimate and corrosive. With Abeloth, it had been vast and hungry, an appetite without a center. This was neither. This was structural. Something wrong with the underlying arrangement of things, not with any person or entity moving through them.

He'd felt this before. Not this specific wrongness — but the *category* of it. The sense of a wound in the Force that existed independent of anyone's intentions, that predated the current crisis, that was going to require more than a lightsaber and good instincts to address.

Ben picked up the training saber and set it on the rack with its companions, very carefully, because he needed something physical to do with his hands.

He was not going to bring this to the Council. Not yet. Not without something concrete — a location, a reading, an artifact, anything that turned *I feel something wrong and it's getting more distinct* into a briefing rather than a confession. He'd been wrong before. He'd been right before too, but he'd also been wrong, and the Order had enough to manage without him raising flags based on Force intuition he couldn't fully characterize.

He'd run more forms tomorrow. Document the pattern. Find a way to describe it that didn't sound like trauma speaking.

The salle door opened.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben knew the way he knew his own face in a mirror.

It meant a mission.
