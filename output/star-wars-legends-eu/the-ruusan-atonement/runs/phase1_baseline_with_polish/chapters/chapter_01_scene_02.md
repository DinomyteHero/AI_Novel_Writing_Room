Luke was already holding the datapad when Ben walked in.

That was the tell. His father didn't hold datapads during normal briefings — he set them on the desk, pushed them across, let the data speak. Holding it meant he'd been reviewing it before Ben arrived, which meant he'd been thinking about how to present this, which meant whatever was on it required presentation.

Ben stepped into the office and his father's presence settled over him like warm air after a cold corridor. Vast. Familiar. Deep and unhurried, like a fire that had been burning long enough to stop needing attention — it was something Ben had spent his childhood orienting by without knowing he was doing it. He knew it the way he knew north.

Underneath it, faint but distinct, was the sourness.

Here too. Even here, under the Temple's harmonics, threaded through the clean stone smell and recycled air and the faint electrical hum of the holocomm on the desk. He'd half hoped it would be absent in the inner rooms. It wasn't.

"Ben." Luke set the datapad on the desk and slid it across. "Seventeen flagged reports. Take a look."

The blue glow caught the desk's surface as Ben picked it up. Survey data, each entry tagged with coordinates along the Outer Rim Corridor — not a cluster, more like a dotted line drawn by someone who didn't know they were drawing it. Anomalous Force readings. Instrument uncertainty flags. Three separate survey teams noting that their Jedi attaché had reported perceptual disturbance that didn't correlate with any catalogued phenomenon.

Ben scrolled to the first of those three. Then the second. Then the third.

The reports described it differently — one Jedi had written *interference pattern*, another *tonal displacement*, the third had simply written *wrong* and then apparently thought better of it and added *anomalous resonance, cause undetermined*. But the underlying data was the same shape. The same bearing. The same faint sourness that had been riding under his own perceptions for weeks, sharpening as it drew closer to whatever it actually was.

"This one," Ben said, zooming on the third report's coordinates. "And this one. The bearing's consistent. It's not a scatter — it's a direction."

"I noticed that." Luke leaned back in his chair. "What does it remind you of?"

Ben set the datapad down. "Jacen. Early, before anyone else had named what was wrong with him. That low register that didn't fit." He paused. "Not the same thing. But the same category."

Luke was quiet for a moment. "I've been monitoring something similar for the past year. I initially attributed it to post-Abeloth residue."

"Cilghal published that analysis," Ben said. "I read it."

"So did I." A beat. "I wrote the foreword."

Ben waited.

"But it isn't fading," Luke said. "Abeloth's effects have a decay signature — they dissipate as the Force reintegrates the disrupted areas. This doesn't decay. It clarifies." He folded his hands on the desk. "My read is that we're looking at a historical artifact. Something old enough that the modern survey instruments weren't calibrated to find it. The Outer Rim has pockets of Force disturbance going back to the Sith Wars — Ruusan, Malachor, the Corridor itself. Whatever this is, it may have been present for a long time."

"And now it's louder," Ben said.

"Clearer," Luke corrected.

"That's not a distinction that makes me feel better."

The corner of Luke's mouth moved. Not quite a smile, but close. "No. I don't suppose it is."

Ben crossed to the window. Coruscant's perpetual amber glow pressed against the transparisteel, the city-planet's light-scatter turning the night sky into something warm and featureless. He could see the upper spires of the Senate District in the middle distance, the traffic lanes moving in their endless organized streams.

"You're reading it as archaeological," Ben said. "Old damage, newly audible."

"It's a reasonable working hypothesis."

"Abeloth was old damage too." He turned. "So was Centerpoint. So was the Maw. Old doesn't mean stable."

Luke held his gaze. "No. It doesn't."

The thing about his father — the thing Ben had spent years learning to work around rather than against — was that Luke didn't dismiss. He listened, he weighed, he gave you the gravity of his full attention. It should have felt like support. Sometimes it did. But sometimes it felt like being assessed by someone who had seen so many crises that each new one got filed into a category before the filing was warranted, sorted into *manageable* or *requires intervention* based on pattern-matching that predated the current problem.

Ben had done the same thing once. He'd pattern-matched Vestara into *redeemable* and been right, and he'd pattern-matched his own perception of Jacen into *I'm too close to trust my read* and been wrong in both directions simultaneously. He'd learned, slowly and at some cost, that his instincts were worth trusting even when they were inconvenient.

The wrongness was not archaeological. He didn't know what it was. But it was alive in a way that old damage wasn't — it had a direction, and it was getting louder, and its texture reminded him less of Malachor's dead scar and more of the weeks before Centerpoint fired, when the Force had been pressing something at him and he hadn't had the vocabulary yet.

"The survey route," Ben said. "It runs the bearing."

"Yes."

"You want me to follow it."

Luke picked up the datapad and held it for a moment without looking at it. "I want someone I trust to follow it. Someone who can read what instruments can't." He set it back down. "You felt this before any of the survey teams filed reports. Your read is earlier and cleaner than anyone else's. That's not coincidence."

Ben heard what his father wasn't saying. *You're the right person for this.* He also heard what his father was carefully not saying: that sending one person into an unknown anomaly along the Outer Rim Corridor was a choice with implications, and that the person being sent was his son, and that neither of them was going to name that.

"Departure window?" Ben asked.

"Forty-eight hours. I'll have Artoo pull the nav data and survey coordinates to your ship."

"I'll need updated charts for the Corridor past Ruusan."

"Already flagged." Luke stood. "Ben."

Ben looked at him.

His father's presence in the Force was warm, unhurried, that deep familiar fire. The sourness hummed underneath both of them, patient and persistent, waiting for no one.

"Trust what you feel out there," Luke said. "Not just the instruments."

"Always do," Ben said, and picked up the datapad.

He didn't suggest a partner. Luke didn't ask why. The datapad's blue glow reflected off the window as Ben tucked it under his arm, and neither of them said anything else, and that silence had a shape to it that Ben chose not to examine.
