Sera didn’t wait for permission. She crossed to the archive console with the certainty of someone who had walked this room a thousand times, and her fingers found the activation plate before Ben had even finished the sentence he’d started to offer.

The console answered at once. Not the hesitant flicker Ben had gotten from the stasis-ring displays — this was immediate, solid, as if the system had been waiting for her hand. Holographic light washed over her face, and for a heartbeat Ben saw something catch there before her expression smoothed out again. A thousand years later, and the machine still knew her touch.

Desh shifted his weight behind Ben’s left shoulder. “That was fast.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was watching Sera’s hands move over the interface — not the tentative probing of someone learning an unfamiliar system, but the exact, efficient motions of a user whose muscle memory predated the language on the display. She wasn’t reading the archive. She was pulling it open from inside herself.

His chest tightened. The unease he’d carried since they entered the fortress was still there — the missing-handrail sensation, the bad footing under him — but it had changed shape. Before, it had felt like the room. Now it felt aimed. The archive was about to tell him something, and his body knew it before his mind caught up.

Torin moved to Sera’s right. He didn’t crowd her; there was a practiced space between them, the sort that came from years of working in rooms where a bad step could get you killed. His hand brushed the console’s edge with the same familiarity Sera had shown, and Ben watched his fingers settle on the old metal the way a pilot’s hand settled on a throttle. No hesitation. Just home.

“Index, please,” Torin said, and his voice had shifted. It was still warm, but the warmth rode over something older — the tone of a man who had been telling stories in briefing rooms before most briefing rooms had a name. “Reformations-era institutional record. Unredacted.”

The holographic display changed. A timeline bloomed above the console — dates, names, structural reorganizations, the sort of institutional mapping Ben had studied in Knight trials. He knew the outline of it. The Ruusan Reformations as every modern Jedi learned them: the Army of Light dissolved, the Order decentralized, the Jedi Lords’ era ended. A thousand years of peace built on the lessons of a war that had nearly torn the galaxy apart.

Except the timeline kept going.

Ben’s attention narrowed. The names were the same — Gavran, the council signatories, the charter revisions — but the cause-and-effect chain was wrong. Decisions his history texts had framed as philosophical corrections were tagged here as containment measures. Doctrinal reforms he’d been taught to admire as ethical refinements appeared under a header he didn’t recognize, a phrase in Old Galactic Standard the console’s translation matrix rendered as penance-structure.

Torin gestured at the display, and a second index slid forward — personal logs, council minutes, facility-status reports. “You know what I love about modern Jedi?” he said, and the dry note in his voice was so easy Ben almost missed the weight underneath it. “You still think the Reformations were about philosophy.”

“They were about philosophy,” Ben said. The words were out before he’d chosen them.

Torin looked at him. Not unkindly. “They were about what Gavran couldn’t bring himself to write into the public charter.” He tapped the display. “This is the private version. The one the Order decided not to pass down.”

Ben’s breathing steadied. His mind was doing what it always did under pressure — cataloguing, comparing, hunting for the break he could fix. But this wasn’t a broken console or a mistranslated glyph. It was the foundation of the institution he’d been raised inside, laid out in front of him in a holographic index he couldn’t fully read because the language was a thousand years dead.

Kael moved.

Ben caught it from the corner of his eye — the man who had been standing against the wall with his back to the stone, silent since he’d given his name in the stasis ring. He stepped forward now, not fast, not hesitant, simply there. His shoulder passed within a hand’s breadth of Sera’s, and she didn’t flinch. Didn’t acknowledge him, either. The sort of silence that came from long familiarity with someone whose presence you had already learned to make room for.

Kael reached past her and touched the display.

One word. He highlighted a single entry in the index — a date marker, Old Galactic Standard — and replaced it with another. The correction was so small Ben almost missed it. A digit. The gap between the year the public Reformations charter was signed and the year the private penance protocols took effect.

Kael didn’t explain. He stepped back, his hand dropping to his side, his eyes already going where they had been before — exits, threats, angles, everything but the room itself.

Ben stared at the corrected date. His vertigo sharpened. The survivors weren’t a single voice. They were three separate people with three separate ties to the truth, and the fact that Kael had chosen this moment — this detail — to break his silence meant something Ben couldn’t yet name.

Sera didn’t look at Kael. She didn’t thank him. She accepted the correction the way she’d accepted the console’s activation — as a fact already folded into her next move.

And then she spoke.

“The Ruusan Reformations were dual-purpose.” Her voice was rough, worn by a millennium of disuse, but the sentence itself was precise — formal, complete, the cadence of someone delivering a finding she had waited a very long time to say aloud. “The public restructuring the Order remembers. And a private penance for what the Jedi Lords did at Veranthos.”

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t soften it with context or apology. She gave it the way she’d given her name in the stasis ring — complete, composed, finished.

Ben felt the words hit his body before his mind caught up.

It was a sickening drop in his center of gravity, the sudden clarity of a door opening onto a room he hadn’t known was there. The Reformations weren’t a philosophical correction. They were damage control. The Order he’d been raised inside, the institution whose identity he had never questioned, had been built on what Gavran chose not to say.

He didn’t ask what the penance was for. He didn’t need to. Sera’s face told him the answer wasn’t in this room — and that she had carried it anyway, for a thousand years, and would keep carrying it until the moment she decided he was ready to hear it.

The archive hummed around them. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case, unopened, its faint light steady against the dark stone. Desh still hadn’t moved from Ben’s shoulder, and Ben could feel the question he wasn’t asking — the operational assessment, the threat calculus, the silence of a man who had learned to let Force-users finish their revelations before asking what it meant for the mission.

Ben looked at Sera — the instrument of precision, the edge honed against something harder than stone. At Torin — the keeper of narrative warmth, the steady center that had been holding a weight Ben was only beginning to grasp. At Kael — the quiet correction, the monk’s brevity that had just broken silence to speak about a date.

Three witnesses to the Order’s hidden history. Three people who had waited a thousand years to say what Sera had just said in one composed sentence.

The weight of it settled into Ben’s shoulders, not as despair but as a new clarity about what he was responsible for. He set himself for whatever came next.
