The freighter's contrail had faded to a white scar against the atmosphere by the time Ben and Desh cleared the ridge. Below them, the ruins pushed up through wind-scoured rock like bones working free of a shallow grave.

Desh stopped at the crest, hands on his hips, and surveyed the valley with the flat assessment of a man who had spent too many years in intelligence to be impressed by anything on first sight. "Sixty years of surveys and nobody wrote this down."

"Nobody saw it," Ben said. He was already moving, boots finding the descent path without conscious direction. The terrain here was wrong in ways that had nothing to do with the Force — erosion patterns that didn't match the prevailing wind, tool-marks on exposed stone that caught the light at angles his training couldn't name. "The last team was geological. They weren't looking for structures."

"They weren't looking for anything buried under three meters of silt." Desh followed, loosening a scatter of gravel with each step. "Which raises the question of what else they missed."

Ben didn't answer. The question was the right one, but it was also the one he'd been turning over since the briefing — the gap between what the chart said and what his instincts kept flagging. The survey catalog for this sector was thin. Thin enough to be wrong, or thin enough to be convenient.

A discarded supply crate lay half-crushed against an outcrop near the valley floor, its stenciled markings bleached past legibility. Civilian-grade. Not recent, but not ancient either. Someone had been through here in the last decade — a prospector, maybe, or a smuggler using the system as a waypoint. The sector was flown through. It just wasn't *known*.

The ruins themselves were larger than the ridge had suggested. What Ben had taken for a single collapsed structure resolved into a complex — three distinct buildings, maybe four, their foundations cut directly into the bedrock. The exposed stone was dark, fine-grained, worked with a precision that had survived wind and time and whatever had buried the site in the first place.

Desh circled left, giving Ben space to work, his boots crunching on gravel at a careful distance. He didn't ask what Ben was looking for. He knew the rhythm by now.

Ben crouched beside the nearest wall. The surface was smooth where the wind had polished it, rough where the original tooling remained. He ran his fingers above the stone without touching it, mapping the cuts in the air before committing to contact.

The markings were precise. That was the first thing that registered — not crude, not weathered past recognition, but deliberate. Straight lines intersecting at angles that suggested a system. Not decoration. Not language, or not any language he knew. Something closer to notation.

He reached out.

His fingers brushed the cut.

The wrongness arrived before the touch was complete — a low vibration that traveled up through his hand and into the bones of his forearm, a sensation that had no business being there because the stone was *still*. It wasn't heat. It wasn't a hum in the audible range. It was a fractional error, the way a step you expect to be there isn't, the way a handrail gives a quarter-inch when you're already leaning on it.

His body recoiled before his mind caught up. He was standing. He didn't remember standing.

"Ben."

Desh's voice came from fifteen meters out, flat and careful. Not a question. An assessment.

"Give me a minute." Ben's own voice sounded steady. That was something. He flexed his hand, feeling the absence of the vibration more than he'd felt the vibration itself. The wrongness was gone now. The stone was just stone.

He didn't reach for the Force. Not yet. The Force was the problem.

He crouched again, slower this time, and pulled the datapad from his belt. The scanner activated with a soft chime, its display casting pale light across the carved surface. Ben began his catalog: angle of the primary cut, depth, tool-width, evidence of secondary working. He photographed the markings from three angles. He noted the erosion pattern and ran the age-estimate algorithm, watching the numbers resolve on the screen.

The datapad gave him a range. The lower bound put the ruins older than the Republic.

He recorded it. He did not record what his hand had felt.

Desh had moved closer while Ben worked, close enough to read the tension in his shoulders without crowding him. "What did you find?"

"Markings. Pre-Republic construction style, or something that looks like it. The tool-work doesn't match any tradition I can place." Ben stood, tucking the datapad against his hip. "I need to send this to my father."

"That's not what I asked."

Ben met his eyes. Desh's expression was neutral in the way that meant he was reading everything and filing it for later. They'd known each other long enough that the silence between them had its own vocabulary.

"I know," Ben said.

He walked to a flat shelf of rock a few meters from the ruins, where the transmission signal would clear the ridge. The datapad's interface was familiar — standard survey template, fields for geological context, structural description, age estimates, photographic attachments. He filled each one with the precision his training demanded.

The Force impression did not appear in any field.

He read the report twice before sending it. The first time to confirm the data was accurate. The second time to confirm the omission was complete.

The transmission went through. The acknowledgment pulse returned in under three seconds — Luke's personal receiver, routing through the relay network Ben had set up before they left Coruscant. His father would read the report within the hour. He would see the markings, the age estimate, the geological context. He would ask the questions Ben was not prepared to answer truthfully.

Ben stood at the edge of the ridge, datapad dark in his hand, and felt the quiet alarm settle into his chest like a stone dropped into still water. The decision had been made before he'd consciously framed it — somewhere between the recoil and the catalog, between the wrongness in his arm and the blank field on the template. He hadn't theorized. He'd simply done it.

The freighter's contrail was gone now. The sky was empty except for the pale disc of the system's primary, and the wind had picked up, carrying mineral grit that stung his cheeks.

Desh appeared at his shoulder, close enough to speak without raising his voice. "You're going to have to tell him eventually."

"I know."

"But not today."

Ben turned the datapad over in his hands. "Not today."

Desh nodded once, the way he nodded at mission parameters he didn't like but couldn't argue with. "Then we should move. We've got three more sites on the grid and daylight's not getting any longer."

Ben followed him back toward the ridge path, leaving the ruins behind them. The wrongness stayed with him — not as a sensation now, but as a question he'd chosen not to send. The first fracture in a transparency that had defined his relationship with his father since he was old enough to understand what honesty cost.

He'd trusted his Force-sense over the official record. And then he'd hidden that choice from the one person he'd never hidden from before.

The weight of it sat in his chest as they climbed, heavier than the datapad, heavier than the question of what else the surveys had missed. He was braced for the moment Luke would read the report and ask the thing Ben couldn't answer.

He wasn't afraid of being caught.

He was afraid of what the withholding meant about his own judgment — and of how easy it had been to do.
