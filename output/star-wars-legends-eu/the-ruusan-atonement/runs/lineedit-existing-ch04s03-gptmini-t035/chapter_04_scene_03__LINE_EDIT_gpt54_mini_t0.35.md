Level 3 opened into a ring.

The stairwell spilled into a circular chamber built around a central shaft that dropped into darkness. Twelve durasteel panels ringed the void at even intervals — Ben counted them once, then again to be sure — and most were dead. Pale blue emergency strips glowed along their bases, but the panels themselves sat dull and unlit, their indicators dark. The air was warmer than it had been above, and the hum in the walls had deepened until it vibrated under his skin.

Ben stopped at the threshold.

The Force pressed at him, the same wrongness he’d been tracking since the plateau, but here it came sharper, narrowed as if the fortress had funneled it straight into this room. Beneath it, something else moved with a clarity that made his chest tighten.

Three signatures. Alive. Distinct.

One was disciplined, steady as a metronome. One was heavy, slow and deep, carrying weight more than shape. One was clipped, cut short in a way that felt like restraint, not injury — someone who had learned to hold still and was still holding.

“Twelve chambers,” Desh said from behind him. “Most of them are dark.”

Ben didn’t answer. He was already walking the curve of the ring, counting as he went.

One. Dark.

Two. Dark.

Three. Dark.

Four. Dark.

Five. Dark.

Each viewport showed a body inside, blue-lit and preserved, faces still visible through the transparisteel. Human. Near-Human. One Twi’lek, lekku pale with age and stasis. All of them sealed in chambers that had failed one after another over the centuries, their Force-signatures gone — not hidden, not muffled, just absent.

Ben’s fingers closed around the railing along the inner edge of the ring. The metal was warm.

Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Nine dark chambers. Nine dead anchors — the thought came unbidden, with no explanation attached, only the shape of what he was looking at. Then the tenth chamber, and this one still held.

Not bright. Flickering, unstable, the blue field dimmed to a weak wash over the viewport. But active. Amber indicators blinked along the panel’s edge. The hum was stronger here, thrumming up through the railing into his palm, and the Force inside the chamber was alive — disciplined, steady, unchanged. A woman’s silhouette stood within the glow, features blurred by the flicker, posture upright, hands at her sides. Waiting.

Eleven. Active too. Heavy. Slow. The presence inside pressed outward with a weight Ben felt in his teeth. A broader male shape hung in the stuttering blue, head bowed. The field around him flickered harder than the tenth chamber’s, its light breaking and returning in a pattern his instincts read as strain.

Twelve. Active. Clipped, restrained, quick and sharp — another man, smaller than the one in eleven, coiled rather than still. The field held, but barely. Amber lights winked along the panel, and one flashed in the same urgent rhythm Ben had seen upstairs on the network display: BREACH translated into a chamber alarm.

And the twelfth chamber was empty.

Not dark. Not dead. Empty — its stasis field gone, its transparisteel viewport shattered from the inside, the interior scored with gouges that radiated outward as if something had clawed its way free. The damage was systematic. Deliberate. Lines converged on the center of the chamber, then burst back out, and the durasteel around the viewport was bent in a way that said pressure had been applied from within for a long time.

Desh had already moved to it. Ben saw him step past the eleventh chamber, glow rod trained on the empty cell, free hand skimming the edge of the broken viewport without touching it.

“Someone broke out,” Desh said. Flat. Immediate. “Recently.”

“How recently?”

Desh put his fingers on the monitoring panel beside the chamber. The display still ran — ancient, flickering, the readouts in the same unknown notation Ben had seen upstairs — but the timestamps were clear enough. Desh read them, jaw tightening.

“Weeks. Maybe a month. The flags are still logging.”

Ben’s attention sharpened. The awe at the three living signatures — the disciplined woman, the heavy man, the clipped presence in the flickering field — tightened into a hard focus. These three were still here. Still sealed. Still depending on systems that were failing, on a fortress whose power had been dying for ages, on a network that had been breaking down one piece at a time. And whatever had been in the twelfth chamber had had weeks to move.

Weeks to act.

Weeks to tear at whatever it had found.

“The damage,” Ben said. “From inside.”

“Systematic scoring. Outward from the center.” Desh’s voice had gone quiet, harder around the edges. “Whatever was in there didn’t panic. It worked at it.”

Ben’s hand moved toward the monitoring panel before he’d decided to move it. The display flickered under his touch — amber indicators, the flashing BREACH warning, timestamps counting back from the moment the chamber field failed. He didn’t need the notation to read the meaning. The breach was old. The escapee was loose. And the three people still sealed above had no idea.

“We need to see what’s below,” he said.

“Ben.”

“I know.”

“You don’t.”

Ben turned from the empty chamber. Desh was watching him, glow rod still aimed at the broken viewport, his weight set back in a stance Ben had learned to read as *we should leave*. But he hadn’t moved. He was waiting.

“Whatever was in there,” Ben said, “had weeks. If it wanted us dead, we’d already be dead. It wanted out. It got out. The immediate problem is what happens next — to them.” He nodded toward the three active chambers. “And to whatever’s below us that Gavran was watching when he died.”

Desh held his gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded once, not in agreement, but in acceptance — the nod of a man who didn’t like the path ahead and was choosing it anyway.

“The stairwell continues,” he said. “After you.”

—

Level 4 was smaller than the ring above.

The stairwell ended in a single chamber built around the base of the central shaft, its walls lined with conduits that ran into a control nexus at the center. Most of the conduits were dark — the power through them gone long ago — but a few still glowed with the same amber light that had flickered through the monitoring stations upstairs, energy cycling through systems built to outlast their builders. The hum was strongest here, rising through the floor, the walls, and the air itself, a vibration Ben felt in his chest, his bones, the base of his skull.

Gavran sat at the nexus.

His body had been preserved by the fortress’s dry air, skin drawn tight over the bones of his face, robes faded but intact. His hands still rested on the control surfaces, fingers laid across the interface with the precision of a man who had been working when he stopped. His posture wasn’t peaceful. It was settled — the way a body settled after the will holding it upright finally gave out.

Ben stopped at the edge of the nexus. The Force around Gavran was quiet. Not gone — the pressure of the fortress remained, still straining against whatever it had been built to contain — but quiet, the way a room goes after a note finishes ringing. The life that had once been here was gone, and what remained was only its outline.

The monitoring log still ran on the console beside Gavran’s right hand. Amber. Unstable. The text was in the same unfamiliar notation Ben had seen on every console in the fortress, but the final entry was legible enough. The sentence broke off mid-word, the characters dissolving into a trail of incomplete glyphs, as if the writer had stopped typing before he could finish the thought.

He held this together until he couldn’t.

Desh said nothing behind him. Ben didn’t turn. He stood at the edge of the nexus, looking at Gavran’s hands on the controls, at the conduits converging on the center of the room and rising toward the ring of chambers above, at the man who had built this system, sealed himself inside it, and held it together for a thousand years while the anchors failed one by one.

The grief hit like weight, not tears. Gavran had stayed. Had held. Had kept the seal intact even as his people died around him, even as the power thinned, even as the network bled itself into silence. He had held until he couldn’t. And now Ben was standing where he’d died, looking up at the three survivors still sealed above him, with no one else to take up the burden.

“Ben.” Desh’s voice was low. “The chambers upstairs. The three still active.”

“I know.”

“What do we do with them?”

Ben’s jaw tightened. It was the right question — practical, immediate, the sort Desh always asked when the situation stopped being theory and became a problem that needed solving. But the answer wasn’t his to give. Not yet. The three signatures were still pulsing above him — disciplined, heavy, clipped — each one a person who had been sealed for a thousand years and had no idea the galaxy had moved on. Waking them would change everything. Waking them without knowing who they were, what they believed, what they would do when they understood what had been done to them—

“Not yet,” Ben said.

“Ben.”

“I know what you’re asking. But I need to know what we’re dealing with before I wake three people who’ve been in stasis since the Ruusan Reformations and tell them the galaxy forgot they existed.”

He turned from the nexus. Gavran’s hands stayed on the controls. The log stayed frozen mid-sentence. The conduits hummed. The pressure in the walls remained. And the three signatures waited above.

“Come on,” Ben said. “I need to see something.”

—

They climbed back to Level 3 in silence. Emergency lighting flickered along the risers. The hum followed them up.

Ben stopped in front of the tenth chamber — the disciplined signature, the woman’s silhouette visible through the flickering blue field. Amber indicators blinked along the panel’s edge. The stasis field thrummed under his palm.

He touched the control surface.

Not hard. Not the sharp, directed nudge he’d used on the monitoring station upstairs. This was gentler — the Force equivalent of knocking on a door and waiting to see who answered. The field answered first. Its blue light steadied for a moment, then slipped back into its flicker, and the signature inside pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat answering a tap.

The other two answered with it. The heavy one in eleven, pressing outward. The clipped one in twelve, quick and sharp. Three signatures, distinct and alive, acknowledging the touch without waking.

Ben left his hand there. The hum carried a question he wasn’t ready to answer. The three signatures were people, not infrastructure, not anchors, not components in a system built to fail. People. Sealed here by an Order that had decided their lives were an acceptable cost. Forgotten by the galaxy built on top of that sacrifice.

“We’ll wake them,” he said. “When we’re ready.”

Desh didn’t answer. He was still in front of the empty chamber, glow rod aimed at the gouges radiating from the shattered viewport, jaw tight.

“Someone broke out,” he said again. “Recently. And they had weeks.”

“I know.”

“Do you know what they’re going to do?”

Ben looked at the three active chambers — the disciplined woman, the heavy man, the clipped presence in the flickering field — then at the empty one, its interior ripped and scored, its warning lights still blinking, its breach still unanswered.

“No,” he said. “But I know where to start looking.”

Below them, in Level 4, Gavran sat at the nexus of the conduit network. His hands were on the control surfaces. His last monitoring log entry trailed off mid-sentence. He held this together until he couldn’t.
