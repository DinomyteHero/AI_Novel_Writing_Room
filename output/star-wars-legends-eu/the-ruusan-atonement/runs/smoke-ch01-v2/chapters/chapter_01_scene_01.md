The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben's first thought was *third time this week.*

Not the hit itself — the hit was clean, his partner had earned it — but the half-beat that had opened up before it, the gap in his guard that should not have existed. He'd seen the strike coming. The Force had given him the read, same as always. And then, for the span of a single wrong note, the timing had simply *slipped*, and his arm had been half a second too slow.

His partner stepped back, blade still humming. Waiting.

"Good." Ben deactivated his own saber, the blue light dying with a soft click. He rolled his shoulder once — the training blade had left a dull throb that would fade in an hour — and waved off the other Knight's unspoken offer of a rematch. "I'm done for tonight. Fatigue."

The other Jedi accepted this without comment, which was either professional courtesy or genuine disinterest. Ben didn't particularly care which. He watched the man gather his gear and leave, the salle door hissing shut behind him, and stood there in the sudden quiet.

The Temple breathed around him. He could feel it — the low, collective presence of forty-odd Jedi going about their evening routines, meditating and eating and sleeping in their quarters above. It was a familiar weight, comfortable the way old furniture is comfortable. He'd grown up inside it.

Underneath it, the note played flat.

That was the closest he'd come to describing it, even to himself: a note, slightly off pitch, persistent beneath everything else. Not painful. Not dramatic. Just *wrong*, the way a door that doesn't quite latch is wrong — you notice it every time the wind moves through.

He'd first caught it six weeks ago. He'd assumed it was residual disturbance from the Abeloth crisis, the Force still settling after what had happened to the Maw. Except the Abeloth disturbances had been loud, violent things, emotional wreckage he could track and identify. This was different. Quieter. It didn't feel like damage to anything in particular. It felt like something was missing.

Ben set his deactivated saber on the equipment rack and walked the length of the salle.

His boots were quiet on the durasteel floor. The room smelled of recycled air and old sweat, the honest smell of a space that got used. He moved through the opening sequence of Shii-Cho without thinking about it, hands empty, letting muscle memory carry him. No blade, no Force-extension, just the physical forms his body had been doing since he was nine years old.

Perfect. Every transition, every weight shift. His body had no complaints.

He pushed deeper into Djem So, the aggressive sequences he'd drilled ten thousand times, and his limbs responded exactly as they should. He was not tired. He had told his partner fatigue because it was a reason that required no follow-up questions.

The note was still there.

He stopped in the middle of the salle, hands loose at his sides.

He'd spent six weeks assuming the problem was his perception — that he was reading the Force through some filter he hadn't identified yet, some post-crisis distortion in his own sensitivity. He'd run the diagnostics any Jedi Knight would run. He'd meditated. He'd checked his emotional baseline, looked for attachment or fear or any of the other things that could cloud Force-reading. He'd found nothing useful.

But his forms were clean. His body was fine. The wrongness wasn't in his reactions — it was in what the Force was giving him to react *to*. The timing slipped because the signal itself slipped, that flat note intruding at the worst possible moment, and his arm had followed the Force rather than overriding it, which was exactly what training was supposed to produce.

He was doing everything right. The problem was somewhere else.

The back of his neck prickled.

He'd felt something like this before. Not identical — nothing was ever identical — but structurally familiar in the way that certain problems had a shape you recognized before you understood the specifics. During the Jacen years, there had been moments when the Force felt *used*, bent toward purposes that left marks. During the Abeloth crisis, the Force had felt *infected*, contaminated by something that operated outside normal parameters. Both had been noisy. Both had been obvious once you knew what to look for.

This wasn't noise. It was the opposite.

Ben walked to the equipment rack and picked up his saber, not igniting it, just holding it. The familiar weight of it. His father had given him the components for this one; he'd built it himself at seventeen, in a meditation chamber while Luke watched from the doorway with that expression he had when he was trying not to offer help.

An absent signal rather than a corrupted one. He'd read that phrase in a survey report last week — a Knight named Pellan logging a Force-reading anomaly from the Outer Rim, classified as routine background noise by the Temple archivist who'd processed it. Ben had flagged it, added it to the three other similar reports he'd collected over the past month, and said nothing to anyone because he didn't yet have anything coherent to say.

Skywalkers cried catastrophe. Everyone knew this. It was practically a cultural tradition.

He was aware of how it would sound: *the Force feels wrong* was not a report that inspired confidence, particularly coming from someone with his bloodline and his history. He'd need more than a flat note and a slow guard to walk into his father's office with that. He'd need something he could point to, something external and verifiable, something that didn't rely entirely on his own perception being trustworthy.

Which was the problem, wasn't it. He wasn't sure it was.

The Abeloth crisis had left marks on everyone who'd been close to it. He knew that. He'd watched other Knights come back from that period changed in ways they didn't always recognize in themselves. He was not so arrogant as to assume he was exempt. Maybe the flat note was him. Maybe he was the instrument that needed tuning.

He stood in the empty salle and ran through that possibility honestly, the way his father had taught him — not dismissing it, not clinging to it, just holding it up and looking at it.

His forms were clean. His body was fine. The signal slipped in the Force, not in him.

He didn't know if he believed that. He knew he couldn't prove it either way, not alone, not from inside his own skull.

The salle door opened.

Ben turned. His father stood in the doorway holding a datapad, still in his working robes, the amber light from the corridor catching the silver in his hair. Luke's expression was the one Ben had learned to read years before he'd learned to read most people — measured calm with something purposeful underneath it.

Ben had seen that expression before missions.

He hadn't realized, until this moment, how much he'd wanted one.
