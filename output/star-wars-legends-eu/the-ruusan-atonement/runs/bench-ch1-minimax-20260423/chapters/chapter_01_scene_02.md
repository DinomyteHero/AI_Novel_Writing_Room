Ben followed his father down the corridor without asking why. There were a dozen reasons to be summoned to Luke’s office at the end of a late shift—administrative matters, a Knight’s report that needed signing, the usual accumulation of Temple life. None of them required a personal escort.

He counted the possibilities anyway. It was what he did.

Luke’s office sat on the fourth floor of the administrative wing, a functional space that had never quite been renovated to match the rest of the Temple’s austere grandeur. The table was the same durasteel-and-transparisteel standard issue as every other office in the building, but the surfaces were cluttered in a way that felt lived-in rather than disordered—a used caf cup, three stacked datapads, a small bronze compass that had belonged to someone’s grandmother. The window behind it looked out over the mid-level landing platforms, and beyond them the slow churn of Coruscant traffic, a river of lights that never quite went dark.

The sour note was here too. Faint, muffled by the Temple’s accumulated presence, but present all the same, sitting under the room’s familiar harmonics like wine left too long in the glass. Ben noticed it the instant he crossed the threshold.

He gave no sign. He had gotten good at that.

Luke gestured to the chair across from him and lowered himself into his own with the careful economy of movement Ben had inherited without meaning to. The chair was standard-issue too, but Luke sat in it like it owed him nothing and he owed it nothing—a man who had spent enough years in institutional furniture to know it would outlast him.

“Close the door,” Luke said.

Ben did. The soft hiss of the seal engaging cut off the corridor noise, leaving only the low wash of traffic from outside and the faint hum of the Temple’s environmental systems. A cleaning droid moved somewhere in the hall beyond, its repulsors whispering.

Luke set the datapad on the table between them. The blue glow of the screen caught the edge of his hands and threw a faint light across the surface.

“Seventeen reports in the last three weeks,” Luke said. “All flagged by Knights on Outer Rim runs. Same anomaly, different sectors.”

Ben didn’t reach for the datapad yet. He watched his father’s face instead—the same careful stillness, the same warmth held under restraint. Luke had always been like that: vast in the Force, quiet in the room. Ben had spent years trying to decide whether the restraint came from discipline or loss, and he still didn’t have an answer.

“Which anomaly?” Ben asked.

“The readings don’t match anything in the database. Force sensors are showing inconsistencies that aren’t resolving as instrument error.” Luke slid the datapad toward him. “The pattern clusters along a bearing. Take a look.”

Ben picked it up. The reports were laid out in standard survey format—date, sector, Knight name, sensor readout—but the anomaly column had been flagged in orange, seventeen times over. He scrolled through the first three. The readings were consistent: a low-grade distortion in the local field, not dark side, not residual contamination, not anything the classification system had a name for.

He knew what it was.

The knowledge sat in his chest like a stone. He had known since the week began, since the first time it surfaced during morning forms and refused to leave. But knowing it and admitting it were different things, and admitting it to Luke Skywalker was a third thing entirely.

He kept scrolling. The pattern held: different Knights, different sectors, the same signature. Then he saw the bearing.

His thumb stilled on the screen.

Three of the seventeen reports clustered along a route that ran deep into uncharted space—not Outer Rim mapped territory, not anywhere a survey mission would take a Knight without specific reason. The navcomputer would flag it as insufficient data. The sour note would tug at the edges of perception the closer you got, like a sound just below hearing.

“Ben,” Luke said.

He looked up. Luke was watching him with the particular stillness that meant he was reading something in the Force—not the way Sera or Kael read it, with precision and classification, but the way a man watched a weather front approach. Patient. Measured.

“I have felt something too,” Luke said.

The words landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Ben’s breath caught—not visibly, he wasn’t that far gone—but somewhere behind his sternum, in the place where the sour note had been sitting for days. The confirmation was immediate. Total. The thing he had been carrying alone was not alone anymore.

But it was also not shared. Not really.

He looked back at the datapad. Luke’s reading was there in the reports—not the anomaly data itself, which had come from the Knights, but the flagging, the clustering, the decision to bring Ben in rather than send a senior Knight. Luke had been sitting on this for weeks. Luke had chosen this morning, after the sparring session, after the loss Rendell had blamed on bad sleep.

Luke knew.

“How long?” Ben asked.

“The reports started coming in six weeks ago. I held them pending assessment.” Luke’s voice stayed even, Grand Master even, which meant everything and nothing depending on what Ben was willing to hear in it. “Your report from two days ago pushed me to a decision point.”

Two days ago. Ben tried to recall exactly what he’d written. Routine mission completion, no anomalies, navigation nominal. He had left out the hour spent in the pilot’s seat with that sour pull at the edge of the hyperlane, like something reaching through water.

He had left it out because he didn’t know how to write it down. He still didn’t.

“This route,” Ben said, tapping the screen. “The navcomputer will flag it as unmapped.”

“I have a survey route cleared for you. Departure tomorrow, mid-morning. Desh Rolan is your pilot.”

Desh. Ben felt a small loosening in his chest—the practical relief of someone who could fly and wouldn’t ask questions he couldn’t answer. Desh was good. Desh was steady. Desh had been GAG, which meant he’d seen enough institutional machinery to know when not to look at the gears.

“And the assignment is a survey,” Ben said. “Not a response.”

Luke met his eyes. The warmth was there in his father’s presence, the familiar weight of it, but the words that followed were Grand Master words: precise, careful, institutionally correct.

“A Knight and a skilled pilot,” Luke said. “Not a war.”

The sentence closed a door on exactly the conversation they were not going to have. Ben heard it clearly: not a war meant not an emergency, which meant not something I’m sending you into with support, which meant this is yours to carry and mine to know.

He could have pushed. He could have asked for a partner, another Knight, something that would spread the weight and the risk. He could have said, I’ve been feeling this for a week and I don’t know what it is and I’d like someone else to feel it with me so I know I’m not losing my mind.

He didn’t say any of those things.

He set the datapad down on the table.

Luke watched him—watched the hand that moved, the shoulders that settled, the breath Ben did not let show as anything other than deliberate. The brief connection was still there in the room, the shared knowledge of the sour note, the shared silence around what it meant. It was real. It was not enough.

“I’ll have the route loaded tonight,” Ben said.

Luke nodded once. The gesture held nothing and everything—the same stillness, the same restraint, the same warmth kept at exactly the distance that let it survive.

“Ben,” Luke said.

He stopped at the door. The sour note hovered at the edge of his perception, patient and slightly off-key, pulling at a route he already knew he would follow. The traffic hummed outside. Somewhere below, a turbolift chimed its arrival.

“Safe travels,” Luke said.

The words were ordinary. The weight behind them was not.

Ben opened the door and stepped into the corridor, leaving the sour note in the room with his father, where it had always been, where it would wait until he came back or did not.
