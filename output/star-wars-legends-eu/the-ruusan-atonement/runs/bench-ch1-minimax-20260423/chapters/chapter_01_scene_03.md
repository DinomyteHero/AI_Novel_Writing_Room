The cockpit’s low hum filled the gap where thought should have been. Ben’s thumb rested on the datapad’s edge, three Jedi Knight contacts visible in his list—Rendell, Salle, one other—each of them a reasonable choice for a survey mission into uncharted territory.

His thumb moved past them.

The Jedi partner roster sat one tap away. Ben knew it the way he knew the location of every emergency exit in the Temple—instinctive, built in. The protocol existed for good reason. A Knight running solo into the Outer Rim with nothing but a non-Force-sensitive pilot was exactly the sort of operational choice that earned an after-action report and a quiet conversation with the Council.

His thumb found Desh’s contact instead.

The comm link crackled twice before connecting—a good sign. The pause meant Desh was awake and functional rather than groggy and disoriented. The delay between signal and response stretched longer than local comm range would allow, which meant Desh was off-planet or in transit, bouncing the signal through relay stations. Long-range comm made the voice thin, stripped of the lower frequencies, but under the tinny quality Ben could still hear the cadence that belonged to Desh Rolan: flat, dry, the vocal equivalent of a man who’d stopped being surprised by anything around the third year of the Galactic Alliance Guard.

“Skywalker.” No hello. No preamble. “You’re up late.”

“Or you’re awake early.” Ben’s thumb moved across the datapad, pulling up the mission parameters. “I need a pilot.”

“I know how comms work.” The pause was deliberate—Desh gathering information from context, from the hour, from the fact that Ben had called him personally instead of routing through his charter’s dispatch. “Define need.”

“Outer Rim survey run. Luke’s assignment.” Ben scrolled to the bearing data. “I need someone who can keep his mouth shut about what he sees.”

The silence that followed was different from the first one. Not the comfortable pause of old habit, but the careful silence of a man deciding how much to say. Ben felt that sour note at the edge of his awareness again, the half-beat lag between what should happen and what did. He breathed through it.

“Someone who won’t file a report with the Council,” Desh said. Not a question.

“That’s the assignment.”

“You’re going to explain that one eventually.”

“I know.”

Another pause. Then Desh’s voice lost some of its flatness, warmed by something Ben couldn’t name. “Terminus Station in two days. I can make that work.”

The knot in Ben’s chest eased. He hadn’t realized how tight it was until the pressure loosened. “I’ll send coordinates.”

“Do that.” The comm clicked dead.

Ben set the datapad aside and stared at the navcomputer. The route was plotted: Coruscant to the Outer Rim, deep territory, coordinates that shouldn’t exist on any official chart. Tomorrow he’d be gone—a solo mission with no Jedi oversight, no partner from the Order, just a former intelligence operative and whatever waited at the end of that bearing.

He started the preflight sequence.

The hangar’s recycled air hit him as he walked the ship out of its berth, the familiar tang of lubricant and ozone cutting through the broader smell of a starport that never slept. Service droids hummed through their routines in the adjacent bays. Beyond the open bay doors, the traffic Control maintained for Temple approach vectors created a constant low roar, the heartbeat of a city that had forgotten how to be quiet.

Ben completed the walkaround with the mechanical precision his mother had drilled into him—check every seal, every connection, every panel that might work loose in atmosphere. The sour note sat at the edge of perception, faint and persistent, like a sound just below hearing. He registered it and moved on. He’d gotten good at that.

The boarding ramp retracted with a hydraulic hiss. The cockpit received him with its familiar weight: the slight give of the pilot’s chair, the tactile spread of controls worn smooth by years of his hands. He ran the ignition sequence and felt the engines come alive—that particular vibration through the seat, the controls, the body—that meant his ship knew him and he knew his ship.

The disturbance was here too. Faint, muffled by the hull and his own concentration, but present. It had been present for weeks now, growing from a whisper to something he couldn’t ignore. He hadn’t told Luke about it yet. He wasn’t sure how.

Air Traffic Control cleared him for departure with the crisp professionalism of someone who’d done this ten thousand times. Ben acknowledged and lifted free of the hangar floor, feeling the repulsorlifts take the ship’s weight as he guided it through the approach vector. The Temple towers slid past the cockpit in a slow tilt, their lights bright against the perpetual glow of the lower levels.

Luke would be awake now, probably in his office, probably reviewing the same seventeen reports that had landed on Ben’s desk yesterday. The thought sat wrong in Ben’s chest—not quite guilt, not quite dread. Something beside both.

He cleared the approach vector and oriented toward open territory. The navcomputer calculated the hyperspace jump: three days minimum to the bearing, longer if the anomalies along the route forced evasive routing. Ben entered the coordinates and initiated the jump sequence, feeling the familiar pressure as the stars stretched and the hyperdrive engaged.

The disturbance remained.

Ben’s hands paused on the controls. Hyperspace resolved into coherent streaks outside the cockpit, the visual shorthand for traveling at impossible speed through impossible distance. The navcomputer logged the bearing. The instruments confirmed the jump. And at the edge of his awareness, that sense sat exactly where it had been before—not fading, not dimming, not growing distant as it should in hyperspace, where the Force grew thin and strange.

Instead, it tightened.

The sensation was difficult to describe without reaching for the wrong words. It felt like a line drawn taut, or the held beat before release. A bearing now. Not just the absence of something—the disturbance had always felt like something missing, a load-bearing element removed from a structure—but a bearing. The navcomputer had logged the coordinates, and now the disturbance sat in the same quadrant of territory, pulling from the same point.

His hands moved on the controls without conscious direction. Ben watched them go—adjusting, confirming, doing the things his body knew how to do while his mind tried to catch up to what was happening. The Force did not work this way. Force perception in hyperspace was unreliable at best, filtered through the massive speeds and the strange physics of lightspeed travel. Jedi texts described it as a dimming, a recession, the Force becoming a whisper rather than a voice. Disturbances dissipated. They did not persist.

They did not pull.

But this one did.

Ben gripped the armrests of the pilot’s chair and watched the hyperspace streaks blur past. The instruments were steady. The navcomputer had locked onto a bearing that would take him to the Outer Rim, and from there to wherever the disturbance was coming from, and from there to whatever was waiting. He didn’t know what he’d find. He didn’t know if he’d come back.

The sour note had become a bearing, and the bearing was a destination, and the destination was three days away.

He settled back in the chair and let it pull.

The disturbance in the Force shifted as Ben left Coruscant—not diminishing but acquiring direction, like a compass needle settling.
