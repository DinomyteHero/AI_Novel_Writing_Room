The third time it happened, Rendell’s training blade snapped across Ben’s shoulder hard enough to sting through the padding.

Ben caught the impact with his guard a beat too late, and the failure landed before the pain did. His body had answered the feint the way it always did in this drill, except tonight it answered late, as if the order had to travel through water to reach his muscles. The field around him came in behind the rhythm he knew by heart, a fraction of a breath off, like a musician joining a piece after the bar line had already moved on. He reset his stance and the wrongness tugged at the edge of his attention like someone speaking his name from the next room.

“You’re slipping,” Rendell said. No gloat in it, no satisfaction. Just the sort of flat report you might make about a misfiring recycler.

Rendell had been a Knight for six years, and Ben had beaten him in six of their last eight spars. The training session had been his idea.

“Long week,” Ben said. He deactivated his saber and straightened. The blade’s hum died in a clean hiss. “Same time Thursday?”

Rendell studied him a moment, and Ben felt his chest draw tight before the question even came. Instead, Rendell only gave a brief nod.

“Thursday. Get some sleep, Skywalker.”

He headed for the side corridor, boots striking the floor in a steady retreat, and Ben was left under the overhead lights with the distant murmur of the Temple’s late shift moving through the east wing. A cleaning droid rolled past the open doorway with a soft mechanical whirr. Farther down the hall, a turbolift chimed its arrival.

Ben clipped his saber to his belt and paced the perimeter of the practice space, letting his hands move through the opening positions of Form III without igniting the blade. Soresu. The defensive discipline his mother had drilled into him when he was still young enough to resent the lectures and not yet wise enough to appreciate them. He slowed the sequence on purpose, testing each transition.

The wrongness stayed with him.

It showed itself the way a loose stair shows itself underfoot: not enough to make him fall, just enough to make him doubt the next step. The Force had always been a rhythm he lived inside without thinking. Now it came in behind the beat, late by a heartbeat that should not have been there. Again he ran the form. Diagonal parry, pivot, high guard. His body moved with the precision of a Knight who had trained since childhood, but the support he expected arrived just after the motion had already happened. Not enough to stumble. Enough to make the mistake visible. Enough that, if he had been sparring, he would have lost the exchange.

Which, of course, he had.

Ben stopped in the center of the room, arms hanging loose, and let out a long breath through his nose. He wasn’t tired. He hadn’t been tired all week, which was almost worse. He had been sleeping badly, waking before his alarm with his pulse already up and the wrongness sitting at the edge of perception like a splinter he could not dig out, but his limbs hadn’t gone heavy and his concentration hadn’t blurred. His body was doing its job. His awareness was doing its job. Something else was not.

He pressed his palms together at his chest and shut his eyes, reaching for the calm that should have settled over him by habit. The Temple’s presence gathered around him in layers, the accumulated weight of meditation, duty, and all the quiet hours spent in rooms like this one. It was supposed to sit behind him like a steady hand.

Tonight it came in late.

Ben opened his eyes. The practice room was empty except for him and the cooling space where Rendell had stood moments ago. Overhead lights hummed softly. The faint smell of sweat lingered where the fighters had moved. Beyond the open doorway, the Temple kept living around him: distant footfalls in the corridors, the low pulse of the city below, the tired machinery of a place that never quite stopped.

A week. The wrongness had been pulling at him since the week began. He had told himself it was residue, the usual static left behind after crisis, the sort of thing that settled once everyone stopped looking for it. The Order had been speaking that way too, treating the whole business as a lingering echo from older wounds in the galaxy. No one else was reporting anything. No one else was losing exchanges by a beat.

Ben walked to the water station at the far wall and drank without stopping to think about it. The recycled water had a cool, metallic edge on his tongue. He’d spent the last several days telling himself it was fatigue, because fatigue was respectable and fixable. He had slept through two nights because the wrongness sat on the edge of thought like a question he couldn’t finish, and he had woken annoyed at himself for being restless over nothing. Monday’s loss had been distraction. Wednesday’s had been bad timing. Tonight, apparently, was more of the same.

Now he stood alone with no audience and no useful lie left to lean on.

He could still pretend this was exhaustion if he wanted to be absurd about it. He could even do it convincingly, for a while. But the truth sat there in the room with him, hard to ignore: his body had not failed first. The timing had. Something in the field was arriving late, and his reflexes were tripping over the gap before he could hide it.

A tightness gathered in his chest. Not pain. Not fear. Something cleaner and sharper than both, the recognition of a problem that could not be explained away without making him sound unstable. As if the issue were not out there at all, but in him.

Ben set the cup down and stood with his hands at his sides, staring at nothing in particular. The Temple hummed around him, full of Knights who were not flinching at empty space, full of Masters who had spent longer in the Force than Ben had been alive and had not mentioned a disturbance. If they felt anything, they were not saying so. If they didn’t, then he was the only one left hearing it.

He was going to have to say something.

Not tonight. Tonight he was still letting himself believe he could put it off one more hour, one more training session, one more wave-off and half-truth. But soon. Eventually. The private version was already wearing thin.

Footsteps sounded in the corridor.

Not the soft shuffle of the late shift. These were heavier, purposeful. Someone moving with a destination in mind.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and mission-ready posture in the set of his shoulders. The look on his face was one Ben knew too well: not a lecture, not a family check-in, not the expression he wore when Ben had done something stupid. This was the face that meant the Order had work, and Ben was going to be part of it.

“Son,” Luke said. “We’ve got a survey assignment I need you to take.”
