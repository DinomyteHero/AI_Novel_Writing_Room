The training blade caught him across the shoulder — a clean, sharp report that snapped through the salle like a door slamming shut. Harder than a sparring tap should land, the kind of contact that meant Rendell had already committed to the strike before Ben's block failed to arrive.

Ben stepped back, rolling his shoulder to shake out the sting. Even now the wrongness pulled at his attention — not a sound exactly, more like the salle's ambient Force presence arriving a beat behind the rhythm his body had known by reflex for years. A note played slightly flat, and late.

Third time this week.

Rendell deactivated his training saber and pushed sweat-damp hair off his forehead. "You all right?"

"Fine." Ben's voice came out light, almost easy. He deactivated his own blade and clipped it to his belt in one motion, practiced enough to read as casual. "Call it fatigue. Long week."

Not quite a lie. The week had been long. He had been tired since Tuesday, or maybe since the wrongness started tugging at the edges of his perception like a half-heard question he could not quite frame. But fatigue was something another Knight understood — something that did not require explanation, or vulnerability, or the discomfort of saying aloud what he could not yet name.

Rendell hesitated, training saber still in hand. Weighing whether to press.

Ben waited him out.

"Another round?"

"Tomorrow." Ben offered a half-smile that did not reach his eyes. "Let me get some sleep first."

Rendell nodded — accepting the out, or at least accepting that Ben was not going to give him anything else — and crossed the salle to rack his training saber. His boots made soft sounds on the padded floor. A cleaning droid hummed past in the corridor outside, its motivator whining faintly, and somewhere deeper in the Temple a turbolift chimed its arrival at another level. The late shift still moved through the building, Knights and Masters and support staff going about their business in the quiet hour between evening and night.

Ben stood alone in the center of the salle until Rendell's footsteps faded.

Then he let the half-smile drop.

His shoulder throbbed where the training blade had landed. Not pain — the weapons were set to sting, not injure — but the specific, clean failure of a mistake his reflexes should not have allowed. He had been blocking that strike since he was fourteen. The form was muscle memory, the timing something his body knew before his mind caught up.

Except tonight his body had arrived a half-second behind the moment, and the wrongness had been there in the gap.

He paced the salle once, twice, measuring the space with his footsteps. Empty now — no witnesses, no sparring partners waiting for a rematch, no one to offer explanations to. Just Ben and the wrongness and the quiet hum of the Temple's recycled air.

He stopped in the center of the training circle and lifted his training saber without igniting it.

The form was an old one — something his father had taught him years ago, a slow sequence built for precision rather than speed. Ben moved through the first stance, then the second, his body finding the positions from memory while his attention stayed fixed on the ambient field. The salle's Force presence was steady, familiar, the same quiet current he had felt since he was old enough to recognize it.

He reached the third stance and felt the lag.

Not dramatic. A half-second delay between the movement of his arm and the arrival of the Force's presence around it — like a musician hearing the accompaniment come in late, the notes right but the timing wrong. He completed the form anyway, moving through each position with deliberate slowness, and the lag followed him through every transition.

The wrongness was not in him.

The realization landed as a physical sensation — a tightening in his chest, a sharpening of focus that felt almost like the moment before a fight. He had been carrying it all week as a private failure, something frayed in his own perception, some lingering echo of crises past that the other Knights had already shaken off. But his body knew the forms. His body had been doing this since before he could read. And his body was arriving on time.

The Force was not.

Ben stood still in the center of the training circle, training saber unlit in his hand, and did not name what he was feeling. *Dread* was too composed, too clinical — the kind of word that belonged in a mission debrief, not in the quiet of an empty salle while a cleaning droid hummed past in the corridor. He was not ready to name it. He was not sure he wanted to.

But he stopped pretending it was fatigue.

The wrongness was external. Something was wrong with the Force itself, something that had been tugging at his attention since the week started and was now costing him beats he could not spare. He did not know what it was or why no one else seemed to feel it. He did not know whether the problem was the galaxy or his own head — whether he was perceiving something real or whether the years since Abeloth had left a crack in his perception that was only now starting to show.

The uncertainty was worse than the wrongness itself.

He ran the form again, faster this time, and the lag was still there — a half-beat behind every transition, the Force arriving late to its own rhythm. His body compensated automatically, adjusting the timing to match what was actually there instead of what should have been, and the adjustment felt like failure even though it was not.

He stopped mid-stance and lowered the training saber.

The salle was quiet. The cleaning droid had moved on. The turbolifts had stopped chiming, the late-shift foot traffic thinning as the hour grew later. Ben stood alone in the empty training circle, breathing evenly, and let the wrongness sit in his chest without trying to explain it away.

He did not know what it was. He did not know why he was the only one who felt it. He did not know whether saying it aloud would make it real or just make him sound like a Knight who could not trust his own senses.

But it was not fatigue. It was not him.

The door hissed open behind him.

Ben turned. His father stood in the doorway, datapad in one hand, the other resting loose at his side. Luke Skywalker's expression was calm — the calm that meant he had already processed whatever was on the datapad and was now thinking about what came next. His presence in the Force was steady and warm, vast in the way it had always been vast, and for a moment Ben felt the wrongness recede slightly, as if the sheer weight of his father's attention had pushed it back.

Then Luke met his eyes, and Ben saw the expression shift — not much, just enough to register. The expression Ben knew meant a mission was coming.

The wrongness sat in his chest like a half-heard note, waiting for him to speak it aloud.

Ben clipped the training saber to his belt and turned to face the door.
