The wrongness had weight here.

Ben had tracked the sensation for months—a pressure at the back of his skull, the Force’s structural complaint made manifest. As he walked the perimeter of the facility, the air pressed against his sternum. The thin place felt dense, his proprioception misfiring with each step as if the ground sat a half-second further down than expected.

"Power signature," Desh noted. He didn't frame it as a question. His scanner was already up, angled toward the structure.

"I know."

The facility had no business looking this way. The jungle had reclaimed everything else on this world, swallowing survey markers and digesting the shuttle’s landing pad into a mossy suggestion of flat ground. Yet the walls ahead remained clean. Not cleared or maintained, but avoided. It was as if the vegetation had considered the stonework and recoiled. Durasteel reinforcing rods pierced the outer facade at regular intervals, exposed at the corners where the stone had settled. No erosion pitted the surface. The metal bore a gray-brown oxidation from a millennium of atmospheric exposure, but the structural integrity held. Whoever had built this intended to outlast time itself.

The architecture was ill-suited for meditation. Where a collapsed section on the eastern face offered a cross-section, the walls measured three meters thick. The lines were military—load-bearing over aesthetic, sightlines over beauty. Ben had seen Jedi construction on Ossus and Coruscant; this predated both by philosophy. The Jedi Lords had built for war. They had built expecting an assault.

"Chrono," Ben said.

Desh checked his wrist. "Running fast." He paused, frowning at the readout. "Two seconds now."

"It was synchronized at the shuttle."

"Yes."

Ben filed the data and kept moving. Chronometers didn't drift in two hours. They didn't drift near power sources unless those sources generated a specific kind of field—the kind that warped space, suggesting something massive churning beneath the soil.

The main portal was set into the hillside. The facility extended deep underground, leaving the visible structure as a mere entry point. The door, three meters of solid stone fitted into a durasteel frame, lacked any visible mechanism. No handle, no keypad, no release lever. Angular script covered the surface, the markings cut deep enough to hold shadow even in the flat midday light filtering through the canopy.

He had photographed those markings on other worlds. Here, they weren't decorative. They were functional.

Ben reached for the Force.

Resistance met him instantly. It wasn't hostile or active, but felt like pressing his hand into cold clay. The mechanism registered his presence—he could feel the internal gears of the Force-lock acknowledge him—but it sat idle. His technique was refined, a product of Temple training that emphasized precision. He was reaching with a scalpel when the lock demanded a fist.

He pushed harder. The clay pushed back.

"Problem?" Desh moved to cover the approach, his back to the door as his scanner tracked the tree line.

"The lock is old." Ben kept his eyes closed. "It expects something I'm not doing."

He stopped trying to force the mechanism and tried to understand it. The lock had a signature—not Force-sensitive itself, but shaped by the hands that had keyed it. He felt the impression of those creators the way one feels the grip worn into an old tool. Broad. Heavy. Unrefined by modern standards, or perhaps refined toward a different purpose. Power over precision. Intention over technique.

The realization brought a spike of recognition. Or a warning.

He stopped filtering his output. He abandoned the careful management of his connection and let the Force move without correction, the way he had before he’d learned to be cautious. The mechanism shuddered. The clay gave way.

The door opened inward with a grinding of stone that sent birds scattering from the canopy. Cold air spilled out—not the damp chill of a cave, but the dry, preserved air of a sealed tomb.

Desh slipped through the threshold, sidearm drawn. "Clear."

Ben followed, and the wrongness followed him, settling into the space like a guest that had been waiting.

The interior was a single vaulted room, designed for long-term occupation. Banks of consoles lined three walls. These weren't modern stations; the housings were stone and durasteel, the displays dark, yet indicator lights on several units glowed a steady amber. Dormant, not dead.

Desh’s scanner chirped. "Getting power readings from below. Geothermal tap, I think. This thing has been running on its own for a thousand years."

Ben moved to the central dais. It wasn't a computer station; it lacked an input surface or holographic projector housing. It was a focus point, a raised platform with channels cut into its surface in a radial pattern. The grooves were deep enough to hold liquid, though they were dry now. This was where Force-sensitive hands had channeled energy through the stone. He could feel the residue of that work like the scent of a fire lingering in the walls of a room years after the flames died.

The wrongness was most present here. Structured. Waiting.

"Look at this." Desh stood at the eastern console, his voice hushed.

Ben crossed to him.

The console surface featured a chart etched directly into the stone beneath the housing—a backup meant to outlast any power failure. The Darpa Sector was rendered in the angular style of the Old Republic, with twelve points marked in a circle. Each point was labeled in the archaic Basic Ben had spent months parsing.

Not ruins. Not observation posts. Anchors.

At the base of the console, letters had been cut into the durasteel with more violence than the surrounding script, as if the engraver had pressed harder when they reached the name:

*Lord Gavran, Commanding.*

The name meant nothing to the modern Order. But the etching revealed the man—the depth of the strokes, the placement, the fact that it was carved into the bones of the facility rather than a replaceable display. Gavran had wanted to be remembered by the stone.

Ben’s hand came to rest on the primary lattice of the central dais.

The room woke up.

It happened in sequence. Power cycled through the consoles from the geothermal feed, amber indicators shifting to a cold white. Displays stuttered alive in fragments of static. A deep carrier frequency hummed through speakers that hadn't moved air in centuries, a vibration Ben felt in his teeth. Desh shifted into a ready stance, hand hovering over his pistol.

The holographic display above the dais flickered into existence. It was a jagged, strobing projection, one corner dark where a node had failed. It resolved and collapsed, cycling through data streams that moved too fast to read.

Ben kept his hand on the lattice. He held the connection steady, not with Temple precision, but with raw intention. Control was his instinct, but this mechanism demanded surrender.

The data streams slowed. They collapsed into a single signal.

The projection steadied on two words in archaic Basic, the blocky letters casting a cold blue light across the floor.

*SEAL FAILING.*

Desh’s scanner went silent. In the quiet, the geothermal hum sounded like a machine that had been waiting a millennium for someone to receive its message.

Ben’s breath hitched. His hand remained on the lattice, channeling the system’s remaining power. He felt the message cycling, waiting for acknowledgment.

The wrongness he’d felt for months wasn't a localized anomaly or residual echo. It wasn't a minor tremor the Order had flagged for later study.

It was a collapse in progress, and the only people who had known to build a warning system were a thousand years dead.

The blue light held steady. Patient. Urgent.

"What does it mean?" Desh asked.

Ben found his voice. "It means something is failing. Something they thought was important enough to broadcast a warning about for ten centuries."

"And they built this place to contain it?"

"To mark it." Ben pulled his hand from the lattice, but the message remained. "To make sure someone eventually knew where to find it."
