The stairs ended in a ring.

Ben stopped two steps from the bottom, waiting for his eyes to adjust. Emergency lighting ran in strips along the floor—cold, blue-white bands that threw the curved walls into sharp relief. The chamber spanned thirty meters, a perfect circle with twelve alcoves cut into the stone at regular intervals. Each held a transparent panel. Behind each panel sat a chamber.

Most were dark.

He descended the final steps. The air felt different here—drier, colder, carrying a faint mechanical thrum from ventilation that had cycled for longer than he had been alive. His footsteps rang too loud against the floor.

Desh followed him down, silent.

Ben walked the perimeter.

The Force felt wrong in a specific way he had never encountered. On the levels above, the distortion had been distant and structural, a mere hum in the masonry. Here, it was rigid. The lattice of the Force pressed from all sides like a cage wound too tight and left to rust. It vibrated against his perception, straining rather than flowing. Held fast.

He stopped at the third alcove.

The panel was frosted with age. When he brushed the lower edge with his palm, the grime came away in flakes. He leaned in.

A face stared back from the gloom. Human, female, her features slack in the stillness of deep suspension. She wore armor that matched nothing in the Jedi archives—heavy plates over a padded undersuit, with a rank sigil at the shoulder that had been violently scratched away.

The stasis field was dead. The chamber held only the ordinary chill of failed machinery.

He moved to the next. Dark. Another face waited inside, male and younger than the last. The frost was thick enough that Ben had to wipe a wide section clear to see. The man’s hands were pressed flat against the inside of the panel. He had died reaching out.

Ben’s breath hitched. He forced his lungs to steady and kept moving.

He counted the rest through the Force instead. Each dark chamber manifested as a void, a place where a life had been before the Force closed over the vacancy. Nine voids, nine silences, arranged in a ring like a circuit with its nodes burned out.

Then he found the active ones.

Three alcoves pulsed with faint blue light, their stasis fields clinging to whatever reserve power remained. The Force signatures bled through the containment like signals through heavy interference—muted, but present. The first felt precise. It was disciplined, a blade kept sharp through centuries of maintenance, the presence held close even in sleep. The second felt heavy, weighted with something old and unresolved. The third was clipped, truncated—the signature of someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible.

Three people. Alive. A millennium in the dark.

"Ben."

Desh stood at the twelfth alcove. It was the one Ben had been drifting toward without realizing it.

The panel was gone. Only the frame remained—an empty rectangle cut into the stone, mounting brackets still bolted in place. Shards of transparent material littered the floor in overlapping curves, as if something had pushed from within until the barrier shattered. The edges were jagged, radiating from a point at chest height.

Desh crouched to retrieve a large fragment. He turned it over, checked the edge, and set it down with deliberate care.

"The material failed from the inside," he said. "Pressure, not age. The gasket around the frame is still intact." He stood, running two fingers along the stone housing. "No cracks here. This wasn't a malfunction."

Ben stepped into the alcove and extended his perception. The Force here felt different than the rest of the chamber. It wasn't the rigid vibration of the containment system. Something had been here that left an absence in its wake—not a void like the dead chambers, but a hole, like a tooth pulled from bone. The lattice was frayed at the edges, individual threads of Force-structure unraveling into nothing. He could feel where it ended. Where it had been severed.

His hands curled into fists.

"Pull the monitoring log," he said.

Desh was already at the panel. The interface was archaic—physical switches instead of haptic sensors, paired with a small display that flickered under his touch. He worked methodically through menus that had been untouched for decades.

The screen populated.

Ben read over Desh’s shoulder. Most entries were automated: integrity checks, field fluctuations, and power readings spanning centuries. The logs grew sparse as time passed. Then came a gap of eighty-three years, followed by a cluster of rapid-fire alerts.

The final entry was dated six weeks ago.

*Anchor Twelve: Containment breach. Field collapse. Occupant status: absent. Cause: unknown.*

Six weeks. Ben had been on Coruscant then. He had been training with Kyp Durron, complaining about the cafeteria hours, while somewhere in the Outer Rim a thousand-year-old Sith had walked out of a broken box.

"Someone broke out," Desh said. His voice remained flat, but his gaze didn't leave the screen. "Recently."

Ben nodded. The reality landed with a physical weight. Not a failure of ancient machinery. An escape.

"The cascade didn't cause the breach," Desh continued.

"No." Ben looked at the frayed edges of the Force inside the empty alcove. Whatever had been sealed there had functioned as infrastructure. Their very presence had been wired into the containment system to hold the structure together. When Anchor Twelve vanished, the system began to fail. Three anchors left. One empty. Nine dead.

"The breach caused the cascade," Desh finished.

Ben turned away before Desh could see his hands shaking. The stairwell to Level 4 was cut into the central floor—a shaft with a ladder running down one wall, the rungs worn smooth by use that had ended long ago. The air rising from below was even colder than the ring.

He descended without a word.

The nexus was smaller than he expected. It was a circular room, ten meters across, with conduits climbing the walls in organized bundles. This was the hardware beneath the Force-work. Twelve conduits. Three still carried power. The other nine fed into nothing.

A chair sat at the center, facing a curved bank of controls. The boards were still lit. Every indicator continued to report and log the slow death of a system losing its nodes.

The man in the chair was dead.

He was old—or he had been when he died. His hair was white and cropped close. His hands rested on the control surfaces, fingers poised over switches as if he had simply stopped mid-task. His face was turned toward the main display.

Ben had expected a jailer. He found something else. Someone who had stayed when he could have fled. Someone who understood the seal needed a keeper and chose to be that anchor until the choice no longer mattered.

Gavran’s last log entry remained on the display. *Three remain viable. Field integrity—*

The sentence stopped there.

Desh climbed down the ladder. He looked at the body, the controls, and the three amber lights on the board. He exhaled slowly.

"The active fields are drawing from the same reserve," Desh said. "When it hits zero—"

"I know." Ben watched the three active indicators. They flickered. The power was failing. Not in years or months, but perhaps days.

The question was simple. It was also crushing.

Three people alive in the dark. One man dead at the controls. One person loose in the galaxy with a six-week head start. Ben stood in the cold, realizing the only way to save the three was to wake them into a world that had moved on—a world that had already let something out that should have stayed buried.

He looked at Gavran’s hands on the switches. The old man had been trying to hold on. To keep the lights burning long enough for someone to arrive. For someone to decide what happened next.

Ben had arrived. The decision was his.
