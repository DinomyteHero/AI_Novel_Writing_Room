The *Steady Returns* held five people and smelled like it.

Recycled air, hydraulic fluid, and the chemical ghost of emergency rations Desh had cracked open between the fortress and the landing pad. Ben kept his eyes on the nav-display. The ship was rated for a crew of two. Torin’s shoulder pressed against the co-pilot’s headrest while Sera stood at parade rest behind the console, her fingers hovering two centimeters above a holographic display she hadn't touched. Kael had claimed a corner near the aft bulkhead, occupying it with the efficiency of someone who had spent a century learning to take up no space at all.

Desh leaned over the tactical overlay. "Fourteen percent seal integrity. Three nodes failed since we landed. At the current degradation rate—" He pulled up a projection. "—the Corridor has weeks. Not months."

"We knew that."

"I'm saying it for the record." Desh's voice was flat, a tone he used to manage his own reactions. "Veraine has sixty-three days on us. She downloaded the local cache, and she knows the architecture from the inside. We have a ship."

"And four people who were part of that architecture," Ben said.

The survivors remained silent. Sera’s fingers stayed motionless. Torin turned toward the star-map, his expression fixed in the same careful blankness he’d worn since the archive. Kael watched the display refresh, his face unreadable.

Ben pulled up the Corridor overlay. Twelve nodes glowed amber and red. The wrongness he’d felt for months had always been directional—a pressure below the galactic disk, an itch in the Force that never resolved into a name. Now it had coordinates. The seal’s failure wasn't uniform. It was sequential, like a bridge losing its pins from one end. Veraine would know exactly which node was next.

So did Ben.

The first anchor still emitted a faint structural signal. Degraded, but present. He set the nav-computer to the coordinates and watched the confirmation light blink.

The comms panel sat three centimeters to his left. He’d avoided looking at it for ten minutes.

His hands moved through the encryption protocols before he’d fully decided to act. The cursor blinked in the message buffer. Ben had composed this transmission a dozen times in his head during the walk from the archive. It had been detailed. Precise. It had contained everything: the seal, the survivors, the timeline, and the lineage of techniques running from Gavran’s work at Ruusan to a Brotherhood alchemist with a two-month head start.

He thought about the last time the Order mobilized for a galactic threat. He remembered the last time a Skywalker said *trust me* and the Council agreed.

If twelve Jedi Masters descended on the Corridor with warships and zero understanding of what they were touching, Veraine would win.

His fingers typed: *Investigating anomalous Force readings along the Ruusan Corridor. Survey expanding. Will report when I have something concrete. Don't worry.*

He sent it before his rational mind could argue. The message icon pulsed once and went dark.

Behind him, the silence shifted. It was no longer the quiet of a group moving toward a shared objective. It was sharper. Waiting.

"First node," Ben said. He turned to face the group. "The seal has a weak point there. Veraine knows it. We get there first and understand the infrastructure before she can dismantle it." He kept his voice level, mimicking the tone his mother used when committing to a path she couldn't retract. "That's the mission."

Sera gave a single nod. It wasn't agreement, but acknowledgment. Her expression suggested she was reserving judgment on the plan while accepting its existence. 

Kael watched to see whether the stated mission matched the real one. His fingers drummed a rhythmic pattern against his thigh, then stopped.

"The technique she's using," Torin said, still facing the star map. "It isn't hers."

Ben waited. The pressure in his chest remained.

"The Brotherhood refined it, but the original derivation—the mechanism Gavran used to construct the seal—runs the same direction. Remove rather than destroy. Pull the structure out." Torin turned from the map. He chose precision over warmth. "Every application in the historical record descends from the same source. Nathema. Malachor. Ruusan." He paused. "Veraine is doing what Gavran did. She's just doing it in reverse."

The room absorbed the weight of it.

Ben’s throat tightened. The Jedi Lords who built the seal had used the same architecture as the wound they were containing. The implications spiraled: the modern Order didn't understand its own foundations. Veraine could reverse-engineer a thousand-year-old working. Ben was now the only person in the galaxy who understood both the lock and the key.

He filed it away. Process later. Control now.

"Then we understand the technique better than she thinks," Ben said. "Because you were inside it."

Torin’s expression shifted. Something warmer bloomed, though a heavy recognition settled beneath it. "Yes."

The pre-jump sequence ran its checklist. The fortress appeared as a gray shape on the exterior camera, shrinking as Desh finished the final calculations. Ben watched it vanish: Gavran’s monument to institutional control, built on the certainty that one man’s decision could hold a wound closed for a millennium.

It almost had.

"How does the modern Order maintain the lattice?" Torin asked. The question was quiet, the kind asked when the speaker knows the answer will be unsatisfying. "The old rituals required anchors. Living ones. What replaced them?"

"Training," Ben said. "Meditation. The Code."

Torin looked at him. "Gavran thought discipline was enough, too." He said it gently. "Before."

Ben held his gaze. The words landed in a place he didn't have time to examine. He didn't ask what *before* meant, or what had changed Gavran’s mind. He didn't ask if the same change was happening to Torin now.

He let the silence close over the question.

The *Steady Returns* punched into hyperspace.

Blue light flooded the cockpit glass, stretching the stars into lines and contracting the galaxy to a single point of motion. Ben kept his eyes forward. Behind him, he heard Sera’s measured breathing and Torin settling into the co-pilot seat.

Sixty-three days. A partial report to his father. A destination.

The wrongness hadn't disappeared. It had simply become navigable.

In the tunnel of light, Ben felt a new pressure at the edge of his senses. It wasn't the seal’s degradation. It was something else, tracking the vector they had just cut through the stars. He didn't name it.

Someone else already knew where they were going.
