The console felt cold with a chill that transcended the room’s temperature. Ben’s fingers hovered over the release panel. He hesitated.

Desh stood three meters back. He understood when a moment required space.

Three amber lights pulsed. Three people had spent a millennium breathing recycled air and dreaming of nothing, waiting for a specific bloodline to trigger the locks. Ben pressed the button.

A long, pressurized exhale hissed through the chamber as the fortress relinquished its hold. Frost sublimated off the first pod in pale curtains. The figure inside emerged in stages: armored greaves, a torso plate etched with archaic sigils, and finally a face. She was sharp-featured, perhaps in her mid-thirties, possessed of a stillness that signaled imminent motion.

She did not wake up confused.

The woman surged from stasis into a combat crouch. Her hand clamped onto her hip where a lightsaber hilt belonged. Her Force presence detonated, striking the room like a blade drawn from a sheath. Ben’s instincts took over. He planted his feet, absorbing the impact rather than resisting. This signature bore no resemblance to a modern Jedi’s. It lacked fluidity. It was rigid, martial, and ancient—structured like weathered stone and heavy with the weight of centuries.

Behind him, Desh’s blaster cleared its holster with a metallic click.

"Hold," Ben said.

"Knight Varik." Her voice sliced through the frost-heavy air. She spoke at him, not to him, her eyes cataloging threats with mechanical efficiency. "Who commands this installation? State your unit designation and sector command."

The words were Basic, but the syntax made Ben’s mind hitch. It was formal and institutional, stripped of any personal inflection.

"Ben Skywalker," he replied. "Jedi Knight. There is no unit designation. I came alone."

Her eyes locked onto his. She assessed him the way Kyp Durron might judge a sparring partner—quickly, entirely, and without warmth. Her hand drifted from her empty hip to her side, fingers curling into a white-knuckled fist.

"Skywalker is not a name from any House I recognize." She glanced at Desh, her gaze snagging on the weapon. "And you travel with an armed civilian."

Desh didn't lower the blaster, though he kept the muzzle averted. He watched her like a problem he was already solving.

Ben moved to the second chamber and triggered the release. The same hiss. The same frost. This occupant was broad-shouldered and massive. The Force presence that accompanied him differed entirely from the woman’s; where hers was cold and contracted, his felt expansive. Torin Hal opened his eyes and stood with the ease of a man who had enjoyed a full night's rest. He smiled.

"Well," Torin said. His Basic followed the same formal construction, but the edges were worn smooth. "The fields are down." He looked at Ben with a disarming, genuine heat. "You're young to be a Jedi Lord."

"Jedi Knight," Ben corrected. "The Order doesn't use that title anymore."

Torin’s smile remained, but a calculation flickered behind his eyes. He turned to the woman, his expression shifting into something measured. The warmth became a tool rather than an impulse. When he looked back at Ben, the grin had settled into a practiced mask.

"Then we've missed quite a bit," Torin said.

Ben released the third chamber.

This cycle took longer. The figure inside was slighter, a young man with close-cropped hair and a posture that betrayed nothing. His Force presence was the strangest of the three—clipped and contained. It wasn't suppressed in the way a trained Jedi hides their signature; it felt like something that had learned to occupy the smallest possible space. He opened his eyes, looked at Ben, then at the room. He remained silent.

"Kael Drenn," the woman said. An identification, not a greeting.

Kael nodded once and stepped out. He retreated to the wall, watching the others with wary silence. His eyes tracked the woman, then Torin, before settling on Ben to catalog the exits.

The three stood in the emergency-lit ring, testing their limbs with small, careful movements. The woman’s hand kept returning to her empty hip. Torin rolled his shoulders. Kael stayed motionless against the stone.

"How long were we under?" the woman asked. Her voice stayed steady, but the repetitive twitch of her hand grew more insistent.

"I need context," Ben said. "What calendar do you use?"

"Ruusan. What other—" She stopped. Her Force presence sharpened, the rigid structure contracting. "What year is it by the Ruusan calendar?"

Ben performed the mental math he’d practiced on the way down the ladder. "I can give you the Galactic Standard year, but—"

"Convert it."

He told her.

The silence that followed was the heavy, hollow quiet of minds failing to process a void. The woman’s hand fell limp. Her Force presence fractured. Through that crack, a sensation hit Ben like a physical blow: the weight of a thousand years of lost faces, compressed into a single second of realization.

Torin’s shoulders slumped. The warmth in his presence guttered like a dying flame. He pressed a hand to his chest, checking for a heartbeat.

Kael closed his eyes and leaned his head against the wall. His presence flickered and then shrank, as if he were trying to disappear from the information itself.

Ben braced himself. For these three, he was the only bridge to a galaxy that had forgotten they existed.

"A thousand years," Torin whispered. His voice was even, but his presence was not. Underneath the warmth, something pulsed with a missed beat.

"More than a thousand," Ben said. "Closer to eleven hundred."

Kael opened his eyes. He didn't speak, but his posture shifted—a subtle bracing, as if the wall behind him had turned to glass.

The woman turned away from Ben to face the nine dark chambers along the far arc. Nine alcoves without amber lights. No hum. No life. She walked toward them, her hand trailing along the edge of each dead pod. At the end of the row, she turned back. Her expression was a mask of iron, but her hand shook.

"The others," she said.

"The field logs show degradation over centuries," Ben explained. "The systems failed one by one. I'm sorry."

She nodded once. The fracture in her presence sealed. She looked at Ben with that same assessing distance, but the quality had changed; she was measuring him against a need, not a threat.

"You are Jedi-trained," she said. "Your form is modern, your terminology is foreign, and you stand in a sealed installation that responds to bloodline verification." She paused. "What is the Order's current strength?"

"That conversation will take a while," Ben said.

"I have time." She spoke without irony. The precision of it—the refusal to acknowledge her loss—sounded like a threat.

Movement at the far end of the ring caught Ben’s eye. Torin had drifted away. He moved with unhurried certainty, pausing at each dark chamber. He didn't touch them. He didn't need to. He counted the inventory with his eyes, his flickering presence pulsing in rhythm with his steps.

He stopped at the twelfth alcove—the one with the shattered housing and jagged edges, where the threads of Force-structure unraveled into nothing. He stood there for a long moment with his back to the group. His fingers traced the fracture line without touching the metal.

Then he turned.

His expression was calm. The warmth had returned to his presence, and the glitch underneath was invisible once more. He looked at Ben across the ring of empty tombs and asked a question in the tone one might use to discuss the weather.

"Who got out?"

Ben held his gaze. "We don't have a name. We only know she left six weeks ago."

Torin looked back at the broken alcove. His thumb pressed against a jagged edge, testing the angle of the break.

"Six weeks," he said. "She's been moving."

He didn't ask it. He stated it like a man who knew exactly what disaster had been unleashed and was already calculating the cost of the fire.
