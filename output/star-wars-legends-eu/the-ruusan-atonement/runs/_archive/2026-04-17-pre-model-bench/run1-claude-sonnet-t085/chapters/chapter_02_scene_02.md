The ruins were not supposed to exist.

Ben stood at the base of the archway and checked the datapad again. The device insisted he was standing on empty ground. Survey 13-B, filed sixty-one years ago by a Republic geological team, was explicit: *WR-7741, rocky terrain, no artificial structures, no Force-sensitive readings, no indigenous life signs, no further survey recommended.* It was signed, stamped, and filed by a lead whose name had long since faded into obscurity.

The archway was three meters of solid basalt. It had been here for centuries.

"Lazy scouts," Desh said. He was ten meters back, crouched over the portable sampler. He fed a core into the analyzer with the patience of a man who had worked in far worse conditions than a dry wind and heavy silence. "Survey team probably hit the sensor package, saw no atmosphere worth breathing, and logged 'dead rock' without landing."

"The erosion patterns don't match that theory." Ben crouched, running his thumb along the base of the arch's left pillar. The stone was wind-carved on its exposed upper faces, rounded at every edge facing the prevailing gusts. But the joints between pillar and lintel remained sharp. The archway had spent most of its existence buried. "The lower sections were underground until recently. This has been exposed for decades, maybe a century. The survey team would have seen it if they’d actually touched down."

Desh looked up from the sampler. "So they stayed in orbit."

"So they stayed in orbit."

"And we're here because your Force thing is making you twitch."

Ben didn't answer immediately. The wind moved through the arch, lifting a thin scrim of reddish dust across the threshold. He could feel Desh watching him—not with suspicion, but with the practiced attention of a partner reading the small tells of a bad feeling. Ben turned toward the courtyard beyond. "The Force is a precision instrument."

"That's currently twitching."

"It's sensing something the survey missed." Ben moved through the arch before Desh could press the point.

The masonry was unsettling in the way that competent things are unsettling. It wasn't crude, but the geometry sat fractionally outside what human eyes expected from right angles and parallel lines. No mortar held it together. The blocks fitted with a precision that implied either extraordinary tools or obsessive patience. He had seen Sith construction on Ziost and Dromund Kaas. He had studied Jedi ruins on Ossus and Tython. Both traditions left recognizable fingerprints. Sith architecture favored the theatrical, using weight and shadow to intimidate; Jedi stonework was utilitarian, built to endure without fanfare.

This was neither.

He moved along the courtyard wall, scanning with the holorecorder. Luke had taught him to catalog before hypothesizing—to see what was there before deciding what it meant. The recorder hummed. Bare stone. No inscriptions. Then, at the wall's corner, he found the markings.

They hadn't been carved so much as poured. The grooves spiraled in paths that followed the natural grain of the basalt, widening and narrowing, branching and converging with the organic logic of water finding its way downhill. They looked like someone had traced the path of a liquid across the stone and then frozen it in place. No chisel marks. No tool-edge fractures. Just smooth, continuous channels worn to a polish the wind hadn't managed to dull.

Desh's boots crunched on gravel as he followed. "Anything?"

"Markings." Ben framed the holorecorder on the largest spiral. "Nothing in the Republic archive. Nothing that matches Sith or Jedi aesthetics. Pre-Republic, maybe, but the records at Ossus go back to the dawn of the Order and I don't recognize the pattern."

"The pattern."

"The way the marks are organized. Every tradition has a signature—shapes that repeat, structures that carry meaning. This looks like—" He stopped, his thumb hovering over the capture control.

"Jedi intuition failing you?"

"I was going to say it looks like a diagram. Not language. A map of something that flows." Ben followed the groove system toward its termination. The spirals converged as they traveled, narrowing and feeding into each other until they reached a single mark near the wall's base: a tight spiral that ended in a sharp, unnatural angle. It looked like a river cut off mid-current. The groove simply stopped, clean-edged, as if something had interrupted the flow and the stone had recorded the break.

"Irrigation?" Desh moved closer, tracking the pattern.

"Maybe." Ben felt a cold pressure behind his eyes. The spirals didn't move like water. They moved like something *being* moved. Like a current seized. He reached out into the Force.

The response was skewed. The Force was present—he could feel the ambient hum he had carried his whole life—but when he tried to read the site, the signal slipped. He reached and found nothing to grip. He tried again and got the same result: presence without structure, like trying to read text through frosted glass. He knew something was there. He couldn't resolve it into meaning.

His breathing turned shallow. He forced a slow, deep inhale.

His palm touched the spiral mark before he had consciously decided to move.

The disorientation hit instantly. Not pain. Not dark side pressure. His spatial orientation simply vanished for one heartbeat. Up and down exchanged places in his inner ear and then snapped back, his body catching the imbalance before his mind processed the cause. His hand jerked away from the stone. He was still standing. The courtyard remained, Desh was still at the wall's edge, and the wind continued its dry whistle.

The Force was still slipping.

"Ben?" Desh had moved. Ben hadn't heard the footsteps.

He looked at the mark—the spiral terminating in its jagged angle. He thought about the monitoring hub on Ossus, the anomaly readings that had sent him here, the vector Luke had identified as directional. Luke thought it was an echo. A residual.

The readings weren't coming from here. They were happening here. The distinction mattered in a way he couldn't yet articulate, but while his hands were steady, his heart was racing.

"We're done," he said.

"You found something." Desh wasn't asking.

"We're done. Get the sampler. I want to be off-surface in twenty."

He didn't wait for an argument. He framed the full wall with the holorecorder, capturing the groove system end-to-end and the spiral's termination from four angles. He was professional and thorough. He had what the Council needed. His movements were efficient, the actions of a man who was exactly as calm as he appeared.

His hands shook as he lowered the recorder.

Aboard the *Steady Returns*, he composed the transmission while Desh ran preflight. He used the burst-packet format: encrypted, compressed, and tagged for his father's personal queue. He wrote the site designation, the survey discrepancy, and the architectural assessment. *Pre-Republic construction, unidentified tradition, no Sith or Jedi markers, markings of unknown origin, holorecords attached.* He noted the anomaly correlation: the site location fell within the mapped disturbance zone. He recommended further archaeological survey.

His cursor blinked at the end of the last sentence.

He had written nothing about the moment his hand touched the stone. Nothing about the lost orientation, the slipping lattice, or the difference between an echo and an event. He told himself it wasn't enough data to report—one subjective impression without corroboration. He told himself Luke would contextualize it poorly without the full picture, and he didn't have the full picture yet.

But he knew, with the clarity of his mother's pragmatism and his father's instinct, that he was choosing silence.

He hit send.

The transmission pinged success. The sound was clean and definitive. Usually, that chime meant a job was finished.

Ben stared at the board for a moment longer than necessary. Then he closed the channel and went to help Desh with the engines.
