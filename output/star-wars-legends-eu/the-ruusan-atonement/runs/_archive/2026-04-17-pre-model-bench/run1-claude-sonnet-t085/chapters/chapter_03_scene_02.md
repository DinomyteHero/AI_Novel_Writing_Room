The cold hit first—not the wet chill of a cave, but something drier and more deliberate. Preserved. It felt like opening a storage container sealed since before the Clone Wars, finding the air inside still held its shape.

Ben stood at the edge of the data core room. He watched Desh work.

Desh’s hands moved across the console housing with a characteristic economy. No wasted motion. No hesitation for effect. He had already pulled the access panel and was running a diagnostic lead along the edge of the primary storage medium. The crystalline data lattice, roughly the size of Ben’s forearm, sat in a cradle that predated the Republic’s standard interface protocols by four centuries.

"Tell me something useful," Ben said.

"Give me thirty seconds." Desh didn't look up. His breath misted in the emergency lighting. "The storage architecture is intact, but the read mechanism is the problem. It wants a handshake from a system that doesn't exist anymore."

"What kind of handshake?"

Desh paused, tilting his head at the housing. "Spatial. The interface expects the operator to have positional awareness beyond normal input. Every time I try to initialize the read sequence, the drive spins up and then stops. It’s waiting for something I can't give it."

Ben moved to his side. The console was a hybrid construction—durasteel framing over carved stone, the engineering language of a people who built for permanence rather than efficiency. The data lattice sat in a cradle with four alignment pins. Three were mechanically engaged. The fourth had drifted a fraction of a millimeter out of true, barely visible, certainly not enough to register on Desh’s scanner.

Ben didn't touch it. He moved it.

The pin seated. The drive spun.

"That," Desh said, "is annoying."

"Sorry."

"Don't be. Just means I can't do this part without you." Desh keyed the initialization again. The display above them flickered, filled with data streams in archaic Basic, and began sorting itself into something legible.

Ben let the Force settle around him. He wasn't reaching; he was listening. The room held a quality he’d noticed the moment they entered, distinct from the outer chamber. The wrongness remained, threading through the facility’s bones, but underneath it lay something older and quieter. These walls carried the impression of sustained, deliberate attention. It lacked the echoes of violence found on Malachor or the grief of the Temple ruins. This was the residue of people who had come here every day for years to focus, and then simply stopped coming. The Force recorded it the way stone recorded footsteps—not as memory, but as pressure worn into the grain.

"Ben." Desh’s voice shifted register. "Look at this."

The display resolved into a floor plan. It wasn't a map of this facility, but of a region. The Darpa Sector appeared in the Old Republic’s angular cartographic style, showing clean lines where modern charts showed only sensor noise and disputed boundaries. Twelve points marked a circle. The label at the top read *PERIMETER COMMAND: DARPA SECTOR* and below it, in a different hand: *Ruusan Corridor—Integrity Monitor.*

Desh pulled up the modern overlay on his datapad and held it beside the ancient display. The stars matched, mostly. Three of the twelve points were in systems that now had names. The others sat in voids—dead zones, nebulae, and regions the Galactic Alliance’s charts marked as navigational hazards.

"This isn't a research installation," Desh said.

Ben’s hand stilled on the console. "No."

"This is a command hub." Desh pointed to the architectural details in the floor plan, his finger tracing the sparse layout. "No quarters beyond basic bunking. No mess hall, no recreation space. Minimal medical bay. This was designed for a rotating garrison, not a permanent research team. Whoever built this wasn't here to study something. They were here to watch it."

The display cycled. A new set of data resolved—a system status log, automated and formatted like a maintenance report. The timestamp at the header used the Old Republic’s reckoning, but the entries below were sequential.

Ben read them.

*Anchor 3: Offline—seal date plus 142 standard years.*

*Anchor 7: Offline—seal date plus 389 standard years.*

*Anchor 9: Critical—seal date plus 401 standard years.*

The list continued. Ben counted. Of twelve anchors, seven showed as offline. Two showed critical status. Only three remained active.

At the bottom of the log, a single aggregate line:

*Perimeter Integrity: 14%.*

Desh read it twice. He stepped back from the console. "That's a dam report."

"Yes."

"Whatever they sealed in here, they expected the seal to hold indefinitely. This isn't a scheduled decommission. These anchors are failing." Desh turned to face him fully. "How old is this log?"

"The last entry is recent. Within the past standard year."

Desh set his datapad down carefully on the console edge. The gesture was deliberate—the precision of someone about to say something they know won't be received well. "That means the system has been running, logging, and degrading on its own for a thousand years. Right now, whatever it's containing is fourteen percent contained. We should report this. We need to get a transmission out to your father."

Ben didn't answer immediately. He turned back to the display as it cycled again, the status log giving way to navigation data. Star charts from the facility’s original survey database loaded sector by sector. His fingers hovered near the console, not quite touching.

He had expected coordinates. One destination—the source, the center, whatever the warning pointed toward.

Instead, the nav-computer populated the display with a pattern. Red indicators bloomed across the Outer Rim in a configuration that made Ben’s breath hitch. Not a scatter. Not a random distribution. The twelve anchor points formed the bars. The Ruusan Corridor—that string of dead systems and navigational hazards that modern pilots filed around without asking why—was the frame. And at the center, in a region where the star charts simply stopped, where no survey had mapped and no hyperspace lane reached, was a void.

A deliberate absence.

Ben had felt the Force go brittle and thin when they approached from the outer edge of the sector. He understood now what he’d been tracking for months. The corridor wasn't a navigational hazard. It was the perimeter of something built to contain. The thinness, the wrongness threading through the facility’s bones, was the sensation of a structure under terminal stress.

He could feel Desh watching him. Waiting.

"This is beyond a survey mission," Desh said. "This is a GA Navy operational matter. We file the report, we transmit the data, we let the Council and Command assess the resource requirement."

Ben’s hands curled at his sides. "And in the time it takes the Council to schedule an assessment, the fourteen percent becomes zero."

"That's not our call to make."

"It is." Ben turned to face him. There was something sharp in his voice—not anger, but the edge of something harder. "I found it. I understand it faster than any committee will. By the time they finish arguing about jurisdiction, whatever's in that void will have already—"

He stopped himself. Forced the rest back down. He thought of the map he had been maintaining for his father, the curated version of the truth he’d been sending back to the Temple. The survivors he’d found. If he reported this now, the curators would take over. The mystery would be categorized, and the people he had found would become assets or liabilities in a Jedi Council ledger.

Desh watched him. Not judging. Calculating. He read the decision by noting what wasn't being said.

"You're going to follow the coordinates," Desh said.

"Yes."

"Without backup."

"With you, if you're willing."

Desh was quiet for a long moment. The geothermal hum filled the cold air between them. Then he picked up his datapad and downloaded the navigation data without being asked, sliding it into his jacket with methodical precision.

"I want it on record," Desh said, "that I recommended the report."

"Noted."

"And I want it on record that if we die in uncharted space because you decided to skip the bureaucracy, I told you so."

"Also noted."

The nav-computer finished its render. At the center of the cage, the Prime Anchor’s coordinates hung in the air—a set of numbers pointing into a region of space so thoroughly unmapped it read as absence. The blue light of the display settled across the room, across the floor plan and the anchor log and the fourteen percent.

Ben’s hand moved to the console. "Prep the ship."

Desh was already moving.
