The dust came off in a sheet.

Ben's gloved hand dragged across the console, peeling back a millennium of accumulation in one grey mass. It held its shape for a heartbeat before collapsing. The air smelled of nothing. That was the error—ancient places usually carried the scent of stone, decay, or the slow chemistry of time. This was a sealed system. The atmosphere remained exactly as it had been the day the doors locked, compressed and sterile.

The backup cell kicked in.

It didn't hum. Instead, a rhythmic, grinding thrum vibrated through the console, into the floor, and up through Ben’s boots until his teeth ached. He stepped back. The overhead strips stayed dark, but three monitoring stations along the far wall woke to a dim, amber glow.

Desh rested a hand on his sidearm. He didn't draw, but his fingers remained curled around the grip.

"Power cell," Ben said.

"I can hear it." Desh scanned the room—left corner, right corner, the two corridor branches—and his hand finally dropped. He hadn't relaxed; he had simply categorized the threat as manageable.

Ben turned back to the room.

The architecture was unmistakably Jedi. He recognized the proportions, the way interior walls curved to dampen echoes, and the placement of doorways at the midpoints of long walls. He had grown up in halls like these. But the resemblance ended at the masonry. The furnishings were wrong. Monitoring stations were stacked in overlapping fields of view, positioned so no single console had a blind angle. There were no meditation alcoves or spaces for contemplation. The chairs were bolted to the floor. Storage racks lined the walls, built for rapid access under load. 

The New Jedi Order had outposts. This was a command post.

"Choke point here," Desh noted from the left corridor. He catalogued tactical vulnerabilities as naturally as breathing. "And here. Defenders would hold the intersection and let attackers funnel in."

"They were planning for an assault."

"Or they were planning to stop people from leaving." Desh returned to the center of the room. "Depends on which direction you think the threat was coming from."

Ben didn't answer. He turned to the amber glow of the monitoring stations, running his fingers just above the nearest console. Desh’s assessment—that this place was a cage as much as a fortress—settled in his chest like a stone. 

***

A ramp led to the second level, broad enough to move heavy equipment. The transition announced itself before Ben reached the bottom. The air changed texture, growing drier and sharper, carrying the faint static charge of vacuum-sealed storage. When the amber light from above failed to reach the floor, Ben ignited his lightsaber at half-draw. The blue blade cast the room into high contrast.

Shelving units covered every wall. Physical media. There were vacuum-sealed cylinders, data tablets in preservation cases, and scrolls in tubes so old the material had fused to the containers. And holocrons—dozens of them, stacked in padded brackets with a haste that suggested they had been the last items saved.

He pulled one free. The surface felt warm. It was standard Jedi construction, faceted and keyed to Force-sensitivity. He held it in both hands and reached out, presenting the basic access signature he’d used on a hundred training devices.

It stayed dark.

The frequency was wrong. Not locked, but mismatched, like a door fitting a frame from a different era. He could feel the internal mechanism straining to respond, but the register it required was sharper and more rigid than anything in his training. His grip tightened. He forced his fingers to relax and set the device back on the shelf.

The data tablets were more cooperative. The script was archaic Basic, full of grammatical structures he had to parse slowly. He moved from tablet to tablet as fragments accumulated. *Anchor Point Seven, integrity nominal. Lattice Inversion protocols, see Appendix—* The appendix was missing. *The Wound requires—* The sentence ended there. 

He kept seeing those two words: *The Wound.* It was used as a proper noun. A specific, known location. He read the phrase three times, as if repetition might clarify the meaning. It didn't.

"Ben." Desh stood at the far wall, where a narrow cache had been built into the stone behind a cracked panel. He was already pulling items out. "Power cells. Old design, but the chemistry is stable. I can retrofit these to the ship. Mapping beacons. Emergency rations." He paused, holding a ration pack, his gaze moving across the cache. "Whoever packed this was planning to stay a long time."

"Or they were planning for someone else to find it and need to leave fast."

Desh considered this. He began packing the gear into his satchel, his movements turning sharper and more economical. 

Ben turned to the central terminal. It was larger than the others, positioned on a slight elevation. A Force-sensitive plate was set into the base. He steadied himself, reached deeper, and tried to find the frequency through instinct rather than technique.

The lock resisted for seven seconds. Then it clicked.

***

The holographic projector threw light upward. A map assembled itself—the galactic disk rendered in silver, the Outer Rim marked in amber. Twelve points of light appeared across the Rim, connected by filaments so fine they were barely visible. Golden threads ran between the nodes like a web.

Nine of the twelve nodes were grey. Dark. The display coded them as *gone*.

Three nodes pulsed amber. Their current location was one of them.

One node—at coordinates unmarked on any modern chart—flashed a violent crimson. A single line of text sat beneath it.

*Containment failure. Breach from interior. Anchor released.*

The network was infrastructure. It was a dam built to hold something in place. Nine anchors were dead. One had been broken from the inside. Three remained, pulsing like a warning light that had been screaming into the void for centuries.

Ben pressed his hand against the terminal. The metal was cold. 

He had learned the Ruusan Reformations as a recalibration—the Order choosing peace, disbanding the Army of Light, and turning toward diplomacy. It was the story every initiate absorbed. The story that defined them.

He looked at the twelve nodes and the nine grey marks.

The Reformations were the story told afterward by people who needed a reason that wasn't this. By people who needed to forget what they had sealed away.

***

"Stairwell’s clear to the next level," Desh called from the ramp. "Blue emergency lights. Air circulation is still running down there."

Ben crossed the archive. At the top of the stairs, the air thickened. A static charge lifted the hair on his arms. It wasn't the vague wrongness he’d felt since orbit; it was something structured. Something rhythmic.

He put a hand against Desh’s chest, stopping him at the threshold.

"Wait."

Desh went still, his hand hovering over his sidearm.

Ben stood at the precipice and let the Force wash over him. What came back were three distinct pulses. Slow. Regular. 

"There are people down there," Ben said. "Alive."

The blue light from below reflected off the walls, steady and cold. Ben realized then that every choice he’d made in this fortress—every step down, every door opened—had been leading here. Toward something that had been waiting a thousand years to be found.

Desh’s grip on his weapon tightened. "How long have they been down there?"

Ben didn't answer. He couldn't. The question opened onto depths he wasn't ready to measure.
