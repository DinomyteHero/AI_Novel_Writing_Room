The hangar reeked of fuel and molten metal, the institutional hush of a place where people come and go without looking back.

Ben settled into the pilot’s seat of his survey vessel—a Sienar Systems courier, unremarkable by design—and let the pre-flight checklist run while he stared at the datapad. The screen cast a pale glow in the dim cockpit. Outside the viewport, maintenance droids rolled past on their assigned routes, oblivious to his presence.

The Jedi partner roster listed forty-three names.

He swiped past Alden Corso first. Corso was meticulous, cautious, thorough—the kind who’d insist on a secondary report before stepping into the Unknown Regions. Ben’s thumb kept moving.

Yela Marsh. Three years his junior, quick reflexes, lethal in a skirmish. Also the most networked Knight in the Temple, which meant anything she saw would be dissected and debated by people who’d already made up their minds.

He kept scrolling.

Venn Okara. Someone he genuinely liked. Which was exactly the problem. Trusting someone you liked was how you ended up—

He closed the roster.

The encrypted personal contacts list loaded. Ben dialed the frequency from memory, keyed in the sequence, and flipped the auxiliary coolant switch as the signal bounced between two relay stations before reaching its destination in the Colonies.

Static. Then a voice, dry as cracked durasteel.

"You’ve reached Desh Rolan, security consultant. If this is about the Merson job, I already said no twice. If you’re a Jedi, I’m busy."

"Busy doing what?"

A pause. "Eating."

"Breakfast?"

"Second breakfast." The clink of ceramic on a surface. "What do you need, Skywalker?"

Ben tapped the navicomputer’s initialization sequence with his free hand. "Survey run. Outer Rim, maybe beyond. Six weeks minimum, probably longer. I need someone who can handle security without asking why we’re going where we’re going."

"Outer Rim beyond the charts." Desh’s tone didn’t shift. "Survey for what?"

"Force anomaly. I’m following a bearing."

"Your kind of anomaly or the Order’s?"

"Mine."

Another pause. Ben heard the gears turning—not the Force kind, just the human kind, the cold math of risk and owed favors.

"Where’s the meet?" Desh asked.

"Terminus Station. Three days."

"Fine." The comm crackled. "Send ship specs and a packing list. Outer Rim beyond the charts means I’m bringing the serious gear, not the polite version."

"Bring whatever you want."

"I always do." A beat. "You eat before you left?"

Ben checked the checklist. Seventy percent complete. "I’ll eat in hyperspace."

"That’s a no." Desh’s voice carried the weight of a man who’d made this observation before, to no avail. "Fine. I’ll have food at Terminus. Don’t wreck your ship getting there."

The comm clicked off.

Ben sat with the quiet. The hangar hummed around him—the distant clang of a loading crane, the hiss of a fuel line disconnecting three berths over, the low pulse of the Temple’s power grid in the walls. He’d been here countless times. It had never felt like a departure before.

He hadn’t asked why Desh hadn’t asked. He didn’t need to.

Desh had been GAG. Not proudly—no one wore that badge with pride—but he’d been there in the years when the line between security and complicity dissolved entirely. He understood, in the way only those who’d stood on the wrong side of a justified order could, why you wouldn’t want a Force-user at your back when heading into the dark.

No words needed. No words exchanged. That was the point.

The repulssorlifts engaged with a low shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into Ben’s boots. The courier lifted from the hangar floor, steady and controlled, and he fed power to the lateral thrusters, guiding it toward the bay exit. Maintenance droids scattered. A hangar tech in an orange vest waved him through without looking up from her datapad.

He cleared the Temple’s flight envelope and banked into Coruscant’s traffic lanes.

Below, the planet sprawled—a metropolis so vast it had forgotten it was a city, just layer upon layer of light and steel and the compressed weight of ten trillion lives. He’d grown up here. Spent enough years in these lanes that the navicomputer almost ran itself.

He climbed without a backward glance.

The upper atmosphere thinned. Traffic thinned with it. The endless amber glow of the city-planet shrank to a curve, then a disc, then a point among points of light. Ben was in open space, the navicomputer running its final calculations, the roster already closed.

He’d chosen Desh because Desh was practical. Non-Force-sensitive. Trained for fieldwork, comfortable with uncertainty, capable of acting on his own. All true.

He’d also chosen Desh because Desh couldn’t be turned. Couldn’t be bought. Couldn’t look at him one day with Jacen’s eyes, or Vestara’s, or any other pair he’d trusted and watched turn into something unrecognizable.

The navicomputer chimed.

Ben studied the calculated route. The bearing tracked southwest, past the Outer Rim’s last reliable survey markers, into the region the charts labeled with the cartographic cowardice of *sparse data* and *navigational hazards*. Six waypoints. The last one at the edge of nothing.

He pulled the hyperdrive lever.

Starlines stretched—white threads pulled thin—before the mottled blue of hyperspace swallowed everything. The viewport filled with it. The courier’s instruments settled into steady travel readings.

The wrongness remained.

Ben’s hand lingered on the lever. In hyperspace, the Force went quiet in a specific way—present but muffled, like sound through a thick door. The disturbance he’d tracked since the salle should have blurred into the background.

It hadn’t.

Worse: it had sharpened. Not louder. Not closer. Just clearer, like a star snapping into focus when the telescope finally adjusted. He could sense its bearing from here, through the blue tunnel of hyperspace, through the laws of physics that claimed the Force didn’t work that way.

It pulled.

Not a push. Not a pressure. A pull. Directional. Patient. The way a compass needle points north and waits for you to catch up.

Ben released the lever and settled back.

Three days to Terminus. Then Desh. Then supplies. Then whatever came next.

The wrongness held its bearing and stayed silent.
