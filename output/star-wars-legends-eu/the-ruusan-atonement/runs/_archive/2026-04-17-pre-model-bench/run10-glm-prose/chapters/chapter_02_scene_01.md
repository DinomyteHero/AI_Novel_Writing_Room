The *Steady Returns* lurched out of hyperspace like a drunk stumbling off a speeder.

Ben’s caf mug launched in a brown arc. It splashed across the copilot’s console, pooled in the grooves of the jury-rigged sensor display, and dripped onto his boots. The ship shuddered once, twice, then steadied as the stars outside snapped from streaking blue lines to fixed points.

Desh’s hands moved over the controls with the unhurried precision of a man defusing a bomb he’d built himself. "Nav recalibration. We drifted three degrees off bearing."

"Three degrees." Ben yanked a rag from the seat pocket and dabbed. The caf had already seeped into the seam around the primary scanner. "You couldn’t have warned me?"

"Where’s the fun in that?" Desh pulled off his headset and hung it on the hook welded to his chair—another non-standard modification. The *Steady Returns* was a patchwork of aftermarket parts and stripped-down originals, held together by spite and Desh’s maintenance schedule. "Besides, you were sleeping."

"I was meditating."

"You had your eyes closed and your feet up. On my ship, that’s sleeping."

Ben gave up on the rag and used his sleeve instead. The caf was lukewarm and bitter—Desh’s brew, strong enough to strip hull plating. The sensor display flickered, then stabilized, liquid finding paths of least resistance around the buttons. "Your sensors are covered in caf."

"Your caf. Your sleeve. Your problem." Desh leaned back, arms crossed. The overhead lighting caught the grey at his temples—newer than Ben remembered from their last run. "Also, you owe me a nav-computer. That thing’s been finicky since Borleias."

"You bought the ship with a finicky nav-computer."

"I bought the ship with character. The finicky nav-computer came free." Desh reached across the aisle and tapped a sequence into the recalibration interface. His fingers were steady, callused—a mechanic’s hands, not a soldier’s, though they’d been both. "Hand me the small hydrospanner. The one from the toolkit."

Ben found it in the compartment beneath his seat and passed it over. Their hands didn’t touch. They never did.

The cockpit settled into working silence. Desh made adjustments to the nav-computer’s housing, cursing under his breath at stripped bolts and corroded contacts. Ben finished mopping caf and monitored the recalibration progress. The recycled air cycled through its filters with a faint wheeze, carrying the ghost of Desh’s morning brew and the metallic undertone of a ship that had seen too many hard years.

This was the part Ben never mentioned in reports. The quiet. The absence of pressure.

Around Desh, the Force simply… stopped. Not like a void pulling at attention, hungry and wrong. Desh was a warm absence—space where the constant emotional noise of other beings didn’t exist. No stray thoughts bleeding through. No unguarded frustrations pressing against Ben’s awareness. Just a man, a hydrospanner, and the patient work of keeping a freighter alive.

Ben hadn’t understood how much he needed that until he’d found it.

Desh grunted in satisfaction and resealed the nav-computer housing. "That’ll hold until next week. Maybe." He wiped his hands on his trousers—standard Desh, treating his clothes like shop rags—and turned to the sensor array. His brow furrowed.

"What?"

"Nothing." Desh tapped the primary scanner. The display flickered. "Probably."

Ben leaned over. The sensor readout showed their route ahead—a clean line through realspace toward the first survey coordinate. But along the bearing, scattered pockets of static dotted the display. Gravimetric distortions. Spatial inconsistencies. Sensor ghosts.

"Those weren’t there yesterday," Ben said.

"Ship’s getting old."

"The sensors are getting old, or the space is getting weird?"

Desh gave him a look—the flat, assessing squint that meant he was deciding how much truth to dispense. "Both, probably." He tapped the display again. The static didn’t clear. "I’ve been logging anomalies since Terminus. Sensor glitches, chrono drift, nav-computer hesitation. All along the same bearing."

The same bearing.

The words landed in Ben’s chest and settled there, cold and heavy.

He reached for the Force without thinking—and the wrongness was there. Closer now. The directional pull he’d been tracking for weeks had sharpened into something with teeth. A patient absence that watched from the edge of perception, waiting for him to look directly at it.

His stomach dropped.

"Coincidence," Desh said. He didn’t sound convinced. "Cosmic radiation. Bad hardware. Instrument error." He tapped the sensor panel one more time, annoyed, and the static pulsed brighter for a moment before fading. "I’ll run a diagnostic when we dock."

Instrument error. The practical explanation from the practical man.

Ben watched Desh’s fingers hover over the malfunctioning display, and a different kind of cold settled through him.

The wrongness was so dense it was bleeding into physical space. Desh’s sensors weren’t malfunctioning—they were accurate. They were reading something real, something the equipment had no calibration for. The ship’s instruments were registering what Ben felt in the Force, and Desh was dismissing it as bad wiring.

The comfortable atmosphere of the cockpit evaporated.

Ben didn’t correct him. Didn’t explain. The words sat in his throat, heavy and unwelcome, because saying them out loud would make it real in a way that Jedi briefings and encrypted reports hadn’t managed.

The nav-computer chimed. Recalibration complete.

Through the viewport, a planet hung against the stars—dun-colored, unremarkable, exactly where the survey coordinates said it would be. The first stop on a route that led somewhere the maps didn’t go.

The wrongness spiked.

The pressure hit Ben like a hand pressing against his sternum—not pain, but weight. The directional pull sharpened to a point aimed directly at the world outside. Every nerve in his body registered the absence, the hole in the Force where something should have been and wasn’t.

His hands tightened on the copilot’s armrest.
