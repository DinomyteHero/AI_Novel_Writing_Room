The wind carried copper and old stone.

Desh’s boots crunched over loose gravel as he circled the perimeter of what the Galactic Republic Survey Corps had logged as uninhabited sixty-three years ago. He held the datapad at arm’s length, squinting at the screen in the flat afternoon light.

"Surveyed in 28 ABY," he read aloud. "Terrain: arid. Vegetation: minimal. Artifacts: none. Structural remains: none." He lowered the pad. "Either the survey team was blind, or someone built this yesterday and aged it a thousand years."

Ben crouched beside the wall’s exposed corner, running his fingers over the construction joints. The stone was local—volcanic basalt, pitted by wind—but the cut was too clean for primitive tools. The angles were precise. Whoever had shaped these blocks understood load distribution and mortarless joinery.

"Not yesterday." He measured the gap by eye. "The erosion patterns suggest at least eight centuries of exposure. Maybe more."

"Eight centuries of a building the survey corps missed." Desh tapped the datapad screen. "These scanners aren’t great, but they’re not *that* bad. Something this size should’ve shown up on orbital imaging."

"It should have."

Dry wind gusted, grit pinging off Ben’s jacket. He stood, brushing dust from his knees, and studied the rocky basin. More structures protruded from the amber soil—low walls, collapsed archways, the corner of something that might have been a tower. All half-swallowed by wind-scoured earth.

From orbit, the ruins would barely register. The stone had weathered to match the terrain, dun-colored as the hills. A standard sweep might have missed these entirely.

Might have.

Ben reached for the Force.

The wrongness pressed against his awareness like a hand on the other side of a door. Not pushing. Waiting. Spatial distortion hit first—his sense of where the ruins ended and open ground began slid three meters left of where his eyes placed it. His inner compass spun, recalibrated, settled off-kilter.

A sub-audible hum followed. A pressure just below hearing. The kind of vibration that made teeth ache without explanation.

Desh had stopped walking. He stood with weight shifted to one hip, head tilted, scanning the basin with the flat expression he wore when he didn’t like what he wasn’t seeing.

"Something wrong with the sensors." He tapped the datapad. "Range keeps fluctuating. One second it reads the far ridge at two kilometers, next it says four."

"Hardware or environment?"

"Both." Desh’s jaw worked. "Mostly environment. The air’s not doing what air should do."

He didn’t elaborate. Didn’t need to. Desh couldn’t feel the Force, couldn’t name the sourceless unease crawling up his spine, but he’d spent enough years in bad places to recognize hostile ground. His hand drifted toward the blaster at his hip—not drawing, just confirming it was there.

Ben pulled his awareness back from the Force. The relief was immediate and unwelcome. Without it, the world snapped into proper focus—distances matched, the ground held still—but the absence of that wrongness felt like stepping out of a storm into a sealed room. Quieter. Smaller.

"Standard survey protocol," Ben said. "We catalog, we record, we move to the next coordinate."

"Standard survey protocol doesn’t cover this."

"Standard survey protocol doesn’t cover a lot of things." Ben moved toward the nearest standing structure—a squat pillar of volcanic basalt, sheared at the top where something had broken away. "That’s why they send Jedi."

Desh’s snort carried across the gravel. "Is that what they tell you?"

Ben didn’t answer. He crouched beside the pillar, pulling a brush from his kit. Wind had piled fine sand against the stone’s leeward face. He swept the grit away in short, efficient strokes.

The markings emerged like wounds.

Interlocking circles, carved deep into the stone. Three of them, nested, with precise geometric intersections at each point of overlap. Through all three, a single jagged line—stabbing from upper left to lower right, deliberate as a blade.

Ben’s breath caught.

The tool marks were immaculate. No chisel drag, no rough edges. Whoever had cut these had done so with a precision that bordered on mechanical. But the style matched nothing in his training. Not Jedi construction script. Not Sith angularity. Not any pre-Republic glyph system he’d studied at the Praxeum.

He leaned closer, tilting his head to catch the carving’s angle in the fading light. The circles weren’t decorative. The intersections were too deliberate, the line too purposeful. This was language. Or mathematics. Or both.

His hand moved without conscious decision.

His bare fingertips touched the carved line.

The jolt hit like a Force pike strike to the sternum. Ben’s arm jerked back, his boots skidding on gravel as he scrambled away from the pillar. The wrongness that had been humming at the edge of hearing surged into a roar—a deafening silence that filled his skull with pressure and absence and the screaming sense of *wrong wrong wrong*—

His back hit stone. He was on the ground, shoulder blades pressed against a collapsed wall, chest heaving. The world tilted, spatial distances screaming disagreement with his eyes, and for three seconds he couldn’t tell where he ended and the ruins began.

Then it receded.

The pressure collapsed back to the sub-audible hum. The spatial distortion settled into a low-grade wrongness instead of a scream. Ben’s hands shook against the stone beneath him.

Desh appeared at the edge of his vision, blaster drawn, scanning the basin. "Ben."

"I’m here." His voice came out rough. He swallowed. "I’m good. Fine."

"What happened?"

Ben looked at his fingertips. The skin was unmarked. No burn, no cut, no visible damage. But the phantom pressure still echoed in the bones of his hand, like gripping a live wire.

"I touched the carving."

Desh’s eyes tracked to the pillar, then back to Ben. His expression said everything about Jedi who touched strange things on uninhabitable worlds. He holstered the blaster, but his hand stayed near the grip.

"Find anything useful?"

Ben pushed himself upright. His legs held. Barely. The ruins were connected—not tangentially, not by coincidence—directly, intentionally, woven into the fabric of whatever was causing the disturbance he’d been tracking for weeks. The carving wasn’t decoration. It was infrastructure.

He pulled his datapad from his belt and began scanning.

The work steadied him. Methodical. Professional. Sweep the pillar, record the dimensions, capture the marking patterns in high resolution. Move to the next structure. Document the architectural taxonomy—the mortarless joints, the load-bearing angles, the erosion estimates. The datapad hummed as it compiled the data, cross-referencing against the Republic survey archives and coming up empty.

Empty. Of course.

Desh watched him work for a while, then turned to a perimeter sweep. His boots crunched over gravel in a slow, deliberate circuit. Ben heard him mutter something about sensor calibrations and coreward engineers who’d never seen a rock in their lives.

The banter helped. Gave the scene a shape that wasn’t *wrong wrong wrong*.

When the scan was complete, Ben opened a new message window. The transmission address glowed in the corner: Luke Skywalker, Grand Master, Jedi Order.

He attached the visual data. The coordinates. The architectural discrepancies. The notation that the ruins didn’t match any known construction style in the Jedi archives.

His thumb hovered over the text field.

The Force impression—the spike, the spatial distortion, the roaring absence that had nearly taken him to his knees—sat heavy in his chest. He could describe it. He should describe it. The whole point of the survey was to document anomalies, and this was the most significant anomaly he’d encountered in months.

He deleted the empty text line.

His thumb moved to the send command.

The transmission chirped out—coordinates, visual scans, structural analysis. Clean data. Archaeological survey results. Nothing about the wrongness. Nothing about the carving that had knocked him flat.

Ben stared at the datapad until the screen dimmed.

Then he pocketed it and stood, brushing dust from his jacket with hands that had stopped shaking.
