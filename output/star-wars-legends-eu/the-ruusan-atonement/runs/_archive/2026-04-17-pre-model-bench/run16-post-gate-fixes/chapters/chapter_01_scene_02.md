The datapad clicked against the desk, its blue light pooling like spilled coolant between them.

Ben lifted it.

Seventeen flagged surveys, each stamped *post-Abeloth residual disturbance, non-urgent, monitor*. He skimmed the first four, then froze. Different teams. Different sectors. Same shrug—the Force’s version of a medic jotting *patient complains of pain* and moving on.

“They line up,” he said.

“Keep scrolling.”

He found the three that mattered. Not the strongest pulses, but the ones with directional data. He spread them in his head, turned the pad so Luke could see.

“Plot these. The line runs past the Outer Rim into blank space.”

Luke studied the display. His presence filled the office the way it always did—vast, warm, the closest Ben had come to standing in another person’s sunlight. Familiar enough to vanish. Ben had calibrated his own sense of the Force by learning to separate himself from that gravity.

“I’ve had teams reclassify these since your second report,” Luke said.

“I know.”

“They were wrong.”

Ben set the pad down. His jaw throbbed from clenching.

“The teams aren’t inept,” Luke went on. “Post-Abeloth signatures overlap on standard instruments. Without a vector, the label sticks.”

“Noise doesn’t organize itself into a straight line.” Ben’s voice sharpened. “This isn’t noise.”

Luke raised an eyebrow. “Then what is it?”

“A hole.” Ben stayed on his feet; the point felt like it needed legs. “Disturbance pushes, itches, weighs. This doesn’t touch. I’ve brushed against two things like it: Jacen the year before Centerpoint, and the space around Abeloth when the veil tore. Both times the Force hadn’t gone sour—it had stepped back, left a vacuum.”

He heard how stupid it sounded: the Order’s resident expert on galactic-level nightmares because he’d lived through two of them. Mara Jade’s boy, credentialed by catastrophe.

Luke let the quiet settle. Then: “I feel it too.”

The words struck like a fist to the sternum. Not the confession—he’d guessed Luke had noticed—but the steadiness behind it. The Grand Master voice dropped away; only a father spoke.

Ben kept his hands still, refusing to rub the ache in his chest.

“Three months.” Luke’s fingers laced together. “Longer, maybe. I kept waiting for it to fade the way post-Abeloth ripples do. It hasn’t.”

“What’s it feel like to you?”

“Echo. Like standing where something ancient snapped and never healed.” His gaze turned inward. “Dark side leaves scars—Malachor, Nathema. You still feel the fracture centuries later.”

Ben exhaled through his nose. “An echo is still sound. I’m getting dead air.”

Luke weighed that. He didn’t dismiss it. He didn’t budge.

“That’s why I want you to take a look.”

He tapped the desk. The holocomm threw a hyperspace lane across the room—pre-plotted, Ben’s designation already attached, bearing southwest along the vector he’d just drawn. He’d had it loaded. Maybe since the second report. Maybe the first.

Ben studied the line. Past the Outer Rim, into fringe territory, then off the edge where charts turned speculative.

“Standard survey brief.” Luke’s voice stayed level. “Record what you find. If it matches your read, we escalate.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then we file it with the other Abeloth noise and move on.” A beat. “I don’t think that’s what you’ll find.”

Ben stepped to the window.

Coruscant layered itself below—towers jammed against towers, traffic threading glowing canyons, the planet-city doing what it had always done, indifferent to any private night. The Temple murmured at his back. Beneath that, small as a splinter, the wrongness tugged from the southwest—patient, aimed.

He’d felt it on the training floor. He felt it here. He’d probably feel it in hyperspace.

The route on the holocomm followed the tug exactly.

“I’ll ship out in a week,” Ben said.

“I was thinking three days.”

“Three days, then.”

Silence settled—not awkward, but loaded. Luke didn’t ask who he’d take. A dozen Knights would make sense; his father knew their names. The question was obvious and absent.

Ben supplied no answer.

He turned. Luke watched him with the careful attention Ben had grown up decoding—*I see more than I’m saying*—which was almost always true.

“Route dies past the Perann Nebula,” Ben said. “I’ll need fresh charts.”

“They’re in the packet.”

Of course.

“One more thing.” Luke reclaimed the pad, opened an archived entry—Temple notation so old it predated the Reformation. “Cross-checking the bearing pulled this. The Ankhural Codex tags the coordinate as significant during the New Sith Wars. Fragmentary, but it’s there.”

Ben took the pad. The coordinate sat at the end of his line like a period.

He’d read the full file before launch.

“Three days,” he repeated, handing it back.

Luke accepted. Neither moved toward the door.

Mission assigned. Route locked. Coordinate logged. Everything necessary had been said; the single word no one had spoken—*partner*—hovered between them, definite and ignored.

Luke didn’t ask.

Ben didn’t offer.
