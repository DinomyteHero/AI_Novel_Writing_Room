The Jedi partner roster glowed on the co-pilot seat as Ben ran through his pre-flight checklist.

Corso, Alden. Two years out of Knighthood. Good instincts, loose mouth. Anything Ben told him before departure would reach the Council by the time he hit the Perann Nebula.

He flipped the primary fuel coupling switch with unnecessary force.

Marsh, Yela. Solid combatant. Better than solid in the field. Also on three other mission queues with Council backing. Pulling her would mean questions he preferred to avoid.

Port stabilizer. Check.

Okara, Venn. The obvious choice—experienced, discreet, no political ties. And Force-sensitive in that particular way where every conversation turned into a wellness check. Ben had received enough of those to last a lifetime.

He set the datapad face-down. The roster could stay there.

The hangar buzzed with ordinary morning noise—two mechanics arguing over a T-6 shuttle's landing strut, Padawans dragging supply crates toward the freight elevator, droids rolling precise patterns across durasteel. Everyone with somewhere to be. Ben's ship sat in its bay like a held breath.

He opened encrypted personal comms and entered a frequency he hadn't used in eight months.

Static. Then a click.

"You've reached Desh Rolan, licensed security consultant and unwilling early riser." Flat. Dry. Entirely awake. "If you're calling to announce the galaxy's ending, please specify which one."

Ben exhaled through his nose. "It's Skywalker."

"I know. My comm has a display, Ben. What's wrong?"

"I need a partner for a survey run. Unknown Regions, three weeks minimum, probably longer. Terminus Station in three days."

A pause. Not hesitation—consideration. Ben pictured Desh sitting up, already calculating, the way he always did when something moved from hypothetical to operational.

"Survey run."

"Survey run."

"Right." Another beat. "What's the pay?"

"Jedi rates plus hazard."

"Define hazard."

"Unknown Regions."

"That's not a definition. That's a threat." But his tone had already shifted to the register that meant *yes, I'm already packing*. "Three days. Terminus Station. You want me to bring the usual kit?"

"Whatever you think you need."

Ben watched the hangar through the viewport, hand resting on the nav-computer panel. He waited. He knew Desh well enough to know the next words, or their absence.

Desh said, "I'll see you there."

No *why aren't you taking a Jedi?* No *does your father know?* No careful pause telegraphing concern. Just logistics and a departure time.

The relief hit below his sternum—not comfortable, exactly. More like shifting weight from one arm to both shoulders. He'd forgotten what that felt like. Or he'd been avoiding remembering.

"Still not taking one of your fellow space wizards, then?" Desh's voice carried that particular dryness that meant he already knew the answer.

"Jedi have questions. You have equipment."

"And here I thought you liked me for my sparkling personality."

"I like you because you don't pretend the galaxy's simple."

"Learned that the hard way." A brief pause. "GAG taught us that much, anyway."

Ben's grip tightened on the comm. Three years ago, in the last ugly months before the GAG dissolved, they'd both learned certain questions were better left unasked. Not because the answers were secret. Because asking meant acknowledging the answers mattered, and in that work, in those rooms, acknowledging anything beyond the immediate problem was a liability. You noticed what you needed to notice. You didn't discuss it. You got the job done and didn't compare notes afterward on what it had cost.

It wasn't healthy. It was what they had.

Ben pressed the ignition sequence. The engines built from a vibration in his teeth to a steady roar.

"Three days," he said.

"Three days," Desh confirmed. "Try to sleep before then. You sound like someone who hasn't."

The comm cut.

Ben sat with that for one second, then pulled the repulsorlift controls.

The ship lifted clean. Through the viewport, mechanics and Padawans and droids shrank, hangar lights sliding past as he rose through the bay's exit shaft into gray Coruscant sky. Traffic lanes materialized—the constant arterial flow of a planet that had never once gone quiet. He slotted into the outbound lane and climbed.

The city fell away in layers. Tower clusters thinning to transit hubs, transit hubs thinning to orbital platforms, the planet-city finally giving way to the black at atmosphere's edge. Coruscant's lights still glowed below, the whole disk mapped in illumination, but up here the stars were sharper.

The wrongness was sharper too.

It had hovered at the edge of his awareness since he'd woken—since before that, if he was honest, since before the training salle and Arev and seventeen flagged survey reports. He'd grown used to it the way you grow used to a draft through a bad seal: always there, always slightly wrong, not quite urgent enough to stop what he was doing.

Ben fed the Ankhural Codex coordinates into the nav-computer and checked Luke's pre-plotted route. The line ran southwest, past the Perann Nebula, off reliable charts.

He pulled the hyperdrive levers.

The stars stretched. White lines blurred into the mottled blue tunnel, instruments cycling through jump calibration, and Ben reached out with the Force—habit, a check, the hyperspace equivalent of confirming restraints.

The wrongness didn't blur with the stars.

It should have. Hyperspace flattened most Force perception to static—distances collapsed, reference points vanished, the usual web of connection went thin and unreliable. Whatever he'd felt in realspace became background noise.

This didn't.

Ben's hand stayed on the console, very still. The instruments flickered once—a minor fluctuation, well within tolerance—and settled. The tunnel held its blue. Everything normal.

Except the dead air had stopped being ambient. It wasn't pressing from all directions anymore, wasn't the diffuse wrongness he'd mapped onto the southwest bearing and filed under *anomaly, directional, investigate*. It had pulled taut. A single vector, clear and deliberate, running straight through the hyperspace tunnel ahead of him.

Not leading southwest.

Pulling.

He'd spent three years learning the difference between a Force impression and his own wishful thinking. This wasn't wishful. The distinction between *I'm following a bearing* and *something is drawing me toward it* was small in practical terms and enormous in every other way.

Ben kept his hands on the console. The blue tunnel streamed past. The coordinate sat in his nav-computer like an answer to a question he hadn't finished asking.

He didn't reach for the Force again.
