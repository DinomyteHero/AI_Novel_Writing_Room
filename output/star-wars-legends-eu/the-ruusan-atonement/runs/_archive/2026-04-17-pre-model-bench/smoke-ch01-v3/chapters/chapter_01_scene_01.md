His guard dropped a half-second late, and the training blade caught him across the shoulder hard enough to sting through the practice padding.

Ben stepped back. The Knight across from him lowered her weapon, head tilting with the question she hadn't asked yet.

"I'm done," Ben said. "Shoulder's tight. Call it."

She accepted this without comment — she was good enough to know he was lying and polite enough not to say so. He watched her deactivate her training saber and clip it to her belt, offered a nod that she returned, and then she was gone, boots quiet on the salle floor, the door hissing shut behind her.

Three times this week. The same beat, the same half-second lag, the same result. A Knight with his training record didn't lose that beat. He'd been sparring since he could hold a practice blade and fighting for real since he was fourteen years old, and his timing didn't just evaporate.

Except it had. Three times.

Ben turned away from the door and stood in the empty salle, breathing. The Temple's ambient presence settled around him — dozens of Jedi going about their evening, their presences warm and familiar at the edges of his awareness. He pulled his shields tighter without thinking about it. No need to broadcast.

The wrongness was still there. It had been there through the entire session, sitting underneath the spar the way a string on an instrument sits under the melody: unobtrusive until it wasn't, and then impossible to ignore. A single note tuned down just a fraction, so close to right that you'd miss it if you weren't listening, and so persistent that once you'd heard it, you couldn't stop.

He'd heard it for six weeks now.

Ben walked to the equipment wall and pulled a towel from the rack, dragging it across his face. Then he set it down, squared his feet, and brought his unlit saber to a low guard.

Form I. Start simple.

He moved through the opening sequence without igniting the blade, letting his body do what it knew. The forms were muscle memory layered over muscle memory — years of repetition, Jacen's early instruction, Mara's corrections, his own stubborn drilling in the years since. His footwork was clean. His transitions were clean. Every beat landed where it should.

He pushed into Form III, faster, and the wrongness pulsed.

Ben stopped.

He backed up, ran the same sequence again, and felt it: not in his muscles, not in his balance. His body was doing exactly what it was supposed to do. The interruption was upstream of that — in the place where Force timing fed into physical timing, where instinct and ability blurred together. The wrongness cut in there, at the junction. Not his body failing. Something else failing, something he was drawing on without realizing it, and the failure was in that source.

He lowered the saber.

Physical fatigue would show up in the muscles. He'd trained through exhaustion enough times to know what that felt like — the way the legs went first, then the arms, then the fine motor work. This wasn't that. His body was ready. The disruption was in the channel, not the instrument.

He stood there in the quiet salle and made himself think about that clearly, the way Mara had taught him to think about things he didn't want to face: directly, without flinching, until the shape of the problem was visible.

The wrongness wasn't in him. It was in the Force itself.

He'd been trying not to arrive at that conclusion for six weeks, which was probably why it had taken him this long. The alternative explanations were more comfortable — trauma residue from the Abeloth crisis, hypervigilance, the psychological weight of the last few years finally catching up with him. The Order's healers had cataloged a dozen such presentations after Abeloth. Ben had read the reports. He'd half-convinced himself he was just another data point.

But data points didn't have a direction.

This did. He'd tracked it without meaning to, the way you track a sound you can't quite place — turning your head slightly, adjusting your position, until you've triangulated something you weren't trying to find. The wrongness came from a consistent bearing. Not from inside him, not from the Temple, not from Coruscant's dense tangle of life. From somewhere out along the Outer Rim, persistent and patient and slightly, persistently flat.

Six weeks of slightly flat.

He knew what structural damage to the Force felt like. He'd grown up alongside Jacen's slow self-modification and spent the years since learning to recognize what he'd been too young to name then — the way Jacen's presence had developed that particular quality, like a chord with one note pulled out of true. Not corrupted, not dark. Wrong in a different way. Foundational. And Abeloth had been worse: not a note out of tune but whole registers simply absent, the Force around her operating on rules that didn't match anything Ben had been taught.

This wasn't Abeloth. It wasn't Jacen either. But the quality — the structural quality, something missing rather than something added — sat in the same category as both of them.

He'd filed two reports. The duty officer on his first submission had noted it as likely post-crisis sensitivity and suggested meditation. His second report had made it into the queue for the anomaly review committee, where it was presumably sitting alongside a hundred other flagged readings from Knights who'd been through the Abeloth crisis and were still recalibrating. Knight Pellan had logged something similar eighteen months ago — absent signal rather than corrupted signal, she'd written, which was exactly the right language — and that report had been acknowledged and filed.

Filed.

Ben put his back against the salle wall and let himself slide down it until he was sitting on the floor, forearms resting on his knees. The training saber hung loose in one hand.

He wasn't going to file a third report. He could already write the response himself: *Thank you for your continued diligence. We are monitoring the situation as the Order stabilizes. Please continue to document any further anomalies.*

And he would continue to document them, and they would continue to be documented, and somewhere out along that Outer Rim bearing, whatever was producing that persistent flat note would continue to produce it. Alone in the salle, with no one to perform competence for, he could admit that the thought sat in his chest like a stone.

Skywalkers didn't ask for help. They handled things. His father had carried the fate of the entire Rebel Alliance on his shoulders and kept it there until the job was done. His mother had run a war. His cousin had —

He stopped that thought before it finished.

The point stood. Self-reliance wasn't a character flaw. It was the family tradition.

The salle doors opened.

Ben didn't look up immediately. He heard the step — one person, unhurried, familiar in the Force in the way that very few presences were familiar to him.

He looked up.

His father stood in the doorway, a datapad tucked under one arm, posture carrying that specific quality of readiness that Ben had learned to read before he could read words. Not the loose ease of an evening visit. The contained focus of a man who'd already decided where he was going and was here to collect someone.

The stone in Ben's chest didn't disappear. But something adjacent to hope settled in beside it.
