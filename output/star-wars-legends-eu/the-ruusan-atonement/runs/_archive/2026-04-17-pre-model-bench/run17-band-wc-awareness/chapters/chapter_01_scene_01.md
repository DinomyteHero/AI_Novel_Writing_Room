The training blade cracked across Ben’s shoulder, a sharp report echoing through the salle. His teeth snapped together hard enough to taste copper.

His guard had arrived half a second too late. Not his footwork. Not his shoulder angle. His guard—the reflex the Force had handled automatically since he was six. The sparring saber’s hum whipped past his ear; he turned the stumble into a retreat that looked deliberate if you didn’t know where to look.

Third time this week.

“Good hit.” The words left a bitter tang.

The Knight lowered his blade—one of the Temple’s rotating sparring pool, reliable, unremarkable, the kind of practitioner who filled the middle ranks of every generation. Ben had beaten him sixteen times straight. Seventeen, counting backward.

“Again?”

Ben pressed two fingers to the bruise blooming across his deltoid. The skin already burned, swelling beneath his touch. He rotated the joint, let the wince speak for itself. “Tweaked it hauling crates this morning. Call it.”

No skepticism crossed the Knight’s face. Either Ben’s acting had improved, or the man’s situational awareness lagged. Probably the latter. Four separate openings on his left flank.

The Knight nodded, racked his blade, and was gone in under thirty seconds.

The doors sighed shut.

Ben exhaled through his teeth. The salle reeked of disinfectant and old sweat, stone floors scuffed by thousands of soles. Overhead, glowpanels flickered once, steadied. Empty racks lined the walls like waiting ribs.

He wiped his palms down his pant legs and began the first kata—no ignition, just motion. Diagnostic routine: prove the hardware wasn’t at fault. Fifteen thousand repetitions lived in his ligaments; he let them run while he listened to the building.

The Temple’s presence murmured overhead—dozens of Force signatures moving through their evenings, a low chord he’d known since childhood. He could hum its pitch in his sleep.

Which was why the dropped note struck so hard.

He reached the fourth kata’s pivot—micro-push from the Force to extend the arc—and the chord cut to silence. Not darkness. Not pain. Dead air. The next beat simply didn’t arrive. He froze, arms half-extended, waiting for the transmission to resume. After three heartbeats it did, pressure equalizing like a cabin seal re-engaging. These were the data he couldn’t print for the Council: the moment the rhythm vanished, the moment it crept back, the exact duration of the hole. Five days of covert timing told him the gaps were lengthening.

He lowered his arms. The empty space in his chest matched the empty space in the Force.

Jacen’s fall had felt like gravity bending toward a black hole—everything pulling subtly out of true. Abeloth’s aftermath had been static, white noise bleeding through every channel. This wasn’t either. This was a frequency erased from the chord entirely. Not the wrong note. The missing one. The silence where something load-bearing had been.

He walked to the wall, hooked the saber, snagged a towel. The fabric came away cool, already damp from someone else’s session. He scrubbed his neck while the facts stacked like dominos.

If the absence had become baseline, the rest of the Order wasn’t careless. They were calibrated to a scale that no longer included the missing tone. You can’t miss a note you’ve never heard.

Which meant he wasn’t tracking a wound.

He was tracking an extraction.

The thought hit like ice water. Someone had reached into the fundamental structure of the Force and carved out a piece. Not corrupted—removed. The difference between poisoning a well and simply stealing the water.

Towel back on the rail, he returned to the center, blade still unlit. Around him the Temple breathed—Knights meditating, apprentices polishing emitters, Masters filing mission reports. Healthy signatures. Normal evening. No one else pacing an empty floor, counting heartbeats between dropouts.

He was probably wrong. He’d been wrong before, spectacularly, and the galaxy had invoiced him for the damage. But he’d also been right when no one else noticed, and the bill for that had been steep as well. The weight of the Skywalker name was a ledger, and he was still learning what counted as a debit.

The doors slid open.

Luke Skywalker filled the doorway, datapad in hand, face set to the expression that had rewritten Ben’s calendar four times previously. His father had found something.
