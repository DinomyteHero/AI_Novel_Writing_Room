The *Steady Returns* dropped from hyperspace like a man stumbling on a stair he’d expected.

The cockpit lurched. Ben’s harness dug into his collarbone. His mug left the console entirely, dark caf splashing across the copilot’s sensor panel before dripping onto his knee.

Desh already had both hands on the yoke, killing their sublight drift with three sharp corrections Ben felt through the deck plates. The ship steadied. The mug rolled under the console and wedged against something that buzzed in protest.

"Thermal stabilizer," Desh said, gaze fixed on the yoke. "She does that on long reversions."

"You knew she’d do that."

"I had a strong suspicion."

Ben pulled his sleeve over his hand and wiped caf off the sensor panel. The instruments were jury-rigged enough that most needed interpreting anyway—a secondary humidity gauge masqueraded as a sublight drift indicator, and the original drift readout routed through a salvaged Corellian display running three degrees warm. He knew the panel’s quirks. Six days in hyperspace had drilled that into him.

"You could’ve warned me."

"You could’ve used a lid."

Ben eyed the mug under the console. He’d used a lid once. Desh had welded a holder to the strut at the perfect height. The lid was in his bunk—he’d taken it off two days ago to check the caf level and never put it back.

He kept wiping.

Desh eased off the yoke as the drift bled to zero, then cracked his neck with a sound like dry kindling snapping. The cockpit reeked of hot metal and burnt caf—an improvement over recycled air alone. Outside the viewport, stars hung fixed and unremarkable. No hyperspace blue. No elongation. Just stars and the void between.

"Nav recalibration should take twelve minutes," Desh said. "Assuming she doesn’t argue."

"Does she argue?"

"She has opinions."

The navicomputer chimed once, as if settling the debate. Desh fished a rag from under his seat—a real cloth, not a shop towel—and tossed it to Ben without looking.

"Left pocket of my kit bag," he said. "Third shelf."

"You keep a rag in your—"

"Twelve years of crewed transports, Skywalker. You always keep a rag."

Ben caught it. The cloth was soft enough not to scratch the sensor housing. Desh had considered that. He finished wiping. The instruments flickered back online as the moisture cleared: drift, attitude, power distribution. The ersatz humidity gauge held steady at what he’d learned to read as *fine*.

He handed the rag back. Desh tucked it away under his side of the console without ceremony.

Silence settled between them. Desh’s hands moved through post-reversion checks—toggles and readouts Ben could follow but didn’t need to. The rhythm was comfortable. Familiar in the way old habits are familiar: not because they’d done this exact thing before, but because they’d done *something* like it. Small operations. Tight spaces. The unspoken shorthand of two people who’d weathered enough bad situations to stop explaining them.

They’d had a different ship then. Different circumstances. Ben kept his eyes on the cleared sensor panel.

"Remember the Duros checkpoint?" Desh asked.

Ben’s hand stilled on the armrest. The question settled into the silence like a stone dropped in still water.

"No."

"Right." Desh’s voice was flat. "Neither do I."

The navicomputer chimed again—not the twelve-minute tone, but something shorter, irregular. Desh leaned forward and tapped the secondary display. His frown arrived before Ben could parse the readout.

"Hm."

"Hm what?"

Desh didn’t answer right away. He pulled up the route overlay—their bearing in pale green, the first survey world marked at the far end. He cross-referenced the secondary panel, then tapped a cluster of flagged readings Ben hadn’t noticed.

"Started logging these two days ago," Desh said. "Spatial anomalies. Localized. They don’t match standard interference—no ion wake, no gravitational shadow, no sensor degradation from the thermal stabilizer." He paused. "They follow our course."

Ben stared at the flagged points. They weren’t random or evenly spaced, but arranged in a pattern that matched the unease he’d felt since Coruscant. The wrongness had a shape in the navicomputer’s data that defied words.

The route overlay pulsed once. The secondary panel flagged another anomaly.

"Computer’s been rerouting," Desh said. "Keeps trying to add a margin. I’ve been overriding it because the alternate route adds three days and I don’t see anything on the charts to justify it." He glanced at Ben. "You see something on the charts?"

Ben’s throat tightened. The question wasn’t casual. Desh was asking him to name it.

"It’s not the charts," Ben said.

Desh studied him the way he studied flagged readouts—assessing, searching for the data point that made the pattern legible.

"It’s not instrument error either," Ben added. "The computer’s reading something real. It just doesn’t have the programming to classify it."

"How bad?"

No preamble. No softening. Ben appreciated that about Desh. He didn’t ask permission to know things.

"I don’t know yet."

"That’s not a no."

"No," Ben said. "It’s not."

Desh sat back. He studied the bearing overlay, then the secondary panel, then the fixed stars beyond the viewport. His hands rested lightly on the yoke, but his jaw had set—the only tell Ben had learned in six days of close quarters.

"We keep flying toward it," Desh said. Not a question.

"We keep flying toward it."

Desh reached for his coffee—the one in the welded holder, lid on. He drank. The gesture was deliberate. A statement. He set the mug down and didn’t look at Ben.

The navicomputer finished its recalibration. The tone was clean and final. Desh entered the coordinates without comment, and the starlines stretched, snapped, and the hyperspace blue rushed in around them.

It felt wrong.

The blue was the same. But the space behind it was hollow. The Force’s living lattice thinned along this bearing, not gone, not replaced—just diminished. The resonant hum beneath everything had gaps. Holes where the resonance dropped out. And at the center of Ben’s awareness, where another being’s presence would normally register, Desh was a warm, steady absence. A quiet space where the Force’s background hum simply wasn’t.

Ben pressed two fingers to his sternum. Nothing to feel. That was the point.

The navicomputer chimed—long-range scanners populating. Desh read the display.

"First survey world," he said. "Coming up on scope."

The planet’s data resolved: mass, rotation, atmospheric composition. Ordinary figures. The wrongness hit the moment the scanners locked on—sharp and directional, a pressure that wasn’t pressure, an absence that carried weight.

Ben’s hands fell from the console. His fingers clenched the copilot’s armrest until his knuckles turned white.
