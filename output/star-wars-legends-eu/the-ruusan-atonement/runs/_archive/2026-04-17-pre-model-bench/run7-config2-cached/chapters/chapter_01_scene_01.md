The training blade struck Ben's shoulder. He'd counted wrong—that was his first thought.

Three times this week. The same half-second gap in the same parry sequence, like a skip in a recording, like a word mispronounced the same way until you stopped hearing the error and started hearing a new word instead. He'd told himself he'd miscounted.

He hadn't.

"You okay?" Knight Teva lowered her blade, head tilting the way people do when surprised but trying not to show it. "You never miss that block."

Ben rolled his shoulder, testing the sting. The training blade had left a line of heat across the joint—nothing serious, just embarrassing. "Tired. Three sessions back to back this week. I'm sloppy."

Teva studied him with the mild skepticism of someone who'd sparred with him enough to know his sloppy and his sharp were difficult to distinguish. Then she deactivated her blade. "Take the night. I've got forms review at oh-seven-hundred anyway."

"Good call."

She left without pressing. That was the thing about the Temple these days—everyone was tired, everyone had their private weights, and nobody asked twice. Ben had counted on that. He kept counting on it.

The door sealed behind her. He stood in the middle of the salle, listening to the room settle. Overhead panels hummed at their standard frequency. The polished floor reflected the lighting in long, pale strips. Somewhere down the corridor, another pair of Jedi sparred—footwork muffled through stone.

Ben deactivated his blade and set it on the equipment rack.

Then he walked to the center of the floor, reset his stance—guard position, weight forward, blade hand raised—and ran the form.

No saber. No Force. Just the body.

The sequence came back without hesitation. Parry. Redirect. Step-through. Check. Arms tracked angles correctly. Weight shifted at the right moments. Timing held across all twelve beats without a single dropped step. He ran it again. Same result. A third time.

His body knew the form. His body had always known the form.

He stopped with his arm extended in the final guard position and breathed.

So it wasn't fatigue. Not distraction. Not overtraining. Not the accumulated stress of three months of anomalous readings and mission reports that went nowhere. The explanations he'd constructed—layering them carefully, maintaining them like a ship's systems log when something's wrong with the drive and you're not ready to write that in the official record—were scaffolding around a gap he'd refused to name.

The gap was real. He'd known that for a while.

Ben lowered his arm and stood still in the quiet salle. His shoulder throbbed where Teva's blade had struck. The sting sharpened into something deeper, the kind of pain that would bruise dark by morning. He could have eased the inflammation through the Force, but instead he stood there and let it hurt. Let it be concrete. Let it be something he couldn't rationalize.

For the first time all week, he stopped trying to work around the sensation and simply listened.

It was there immediately. Always there.

A flatness in the ambient hum of the Temple's Force presence—not silence, not darkness, not the cold hollow of grief or the grinding pressure of something malevolent. The Temple resonated with the presences of hundreds of Jedi the way an instrument resonates with its strings. Threaded through that resonance was this other thing: a note almost correct, almost in tune, but not quite. A frequency that should have locked into the harmonic and instead sat a fraction of a degree beside it, producing a dissonance too subtle to name and too persistent to ignore.

He'd been ignoring it for three months. Or trying to.

Ben reached further, past the Temple's ambient hum, past the comfortable noise of other Jedi going about their evenings. He reached the way he'd learned to reach in those bad years, when feeling the shape of something without touching it directly had been the difference between survival and disaster. He reached the way he'd reached during the last major crisis, when the Force itself had felt like standing in a room where the walls were bowing inward.

The dissonance didn't move. Didn't pulse or breathe or hunger. It sat in the Force the way a structural crack sits in a wall—not growing, not shrinking, simply present, patient, doing the quiet work of undermining.

That was what stopped him.

He'd felt dark side contamination before. Trauma echoes. Grief-signatures. Places where violence had burned itself into the living Force and left a cold mark. He knew those textures. They had weight and intention, even the old ones, even the ones that were just residue.

This had none of that. This was the absence of a property the Force was supposed to have. Not something added. Something missing. The way a load-bearing beam is missing when the floor begins to sink—you don't see the beam, you see the floor, and the floor is wrong, and you understand only by inference what used to be there.

His arm came down to his side. Breathing even. Hands steady.

The Order had been attributing his sensitivity readings to prolonged exposure to extreme dark side events, residual trauma affecting Force perception. He'd filed the reports. Accepted the cross-referencing. Let them write his concern into the documentation as a known variable, a flag to monitor, the kind of thing that gets reviewed quarterly and generates no action.

He'd let them, because arguing required explanation. Explanation required vulnerability. And the last time he'd trusted someone with the full architecture of his concern—

He turned away from that memory the way you turn away from a fire.

This wasn't from any of those past events. Those had felt like standing next to something vast and consuming and wrong in a way that made his whole nervous system want to flee. This felt like standing in a room where the floor plan had changed while he wasn't looking—familiar surfaces, familiar sounds, but the angles were slightly off, and the off-ness was load-bearing.

Something structural had shifted in the Force. Not here. Not in this room. Somewhere out along the bearing his nav-logs kept circling, somewhere in the Darrpa Sector where seventeen anomalous readings had clustered into a pattern over three months and then stopped generating new data as if whatever was causing them had stopped caring about being subtle.

He was the only one in the Order who'd felt it this way. He was certain of that now. The others felt the ambient disturbance the way you feel a change in air pressure before weather—vague, diffuse, attributable to a dozen other causes. Ben felt it the way you feel the moment a ship's hull begins to flex under stress it wasn't designed to handle. Specific. Directional. Getting worse.

He'd been waiting to be sure before he pushed harder on the reports.

He was sure now.

The salle doors hissed open behind him.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway, datapad in hand, wearing the expression that meant he wasn't stopping by.
