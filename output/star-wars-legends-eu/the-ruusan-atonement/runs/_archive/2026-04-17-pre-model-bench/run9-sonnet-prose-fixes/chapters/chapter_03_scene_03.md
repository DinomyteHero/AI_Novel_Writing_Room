The third draft looked exactly like the first two.

Ben read the opening line again. *Anomalous pre-Republic installation discovered at coordinates—* His finger hovered over the delete key. The cursor blinked, patient as a predator. He deleted it. Started again. *Jedi Knight Ben Skywalker reporting discovery of a large-scale containment network of unknown—* Deleted that too. The console’s plastic casing creaked under his grip.

"You're not sending that."

Desh’s voice sliced through the cockpit’s recycled air like a vibroblade through silk. Ben hadn’t heard him come forward. He turned to find Desh braced in the copilot’s doorway, mug steaming in his hand, reading the blank screen like it was written in blood.

"I'm drafting it."

"You've been drafting it for forty minutes." Desh moved to the copilot’s seat and dropped into it with the weight of a man who’d seen this dance before. "Three times. I watched the deletion log fill up from the galley."

Ben’s jaw worked. The cockpit felt smaller suddenly, the dark pressing in except for the instrument wash—green and amber, the nav computer still cycling through coordinates that felt like a confession. Outside, stars hung indifferent to Skywalker family drama.

"Protocol says—"

"I know what protocol says." Desh set his mug down with a click that sounded final. "I also know what a man who’s looking for an excuse not to hit send looks like. We had a whole department of them."

The GAG. Always the GAG, clinging to Desh’s voice like smoke that never quite cleared. Ben had been twenty-two and convinced he was different. The memory left a bitter tang.

"The Council will convene a review," Ben said, each word dropping like stones into still water. "Cross-reference the anomaly data against the classified archive. Debate the jurisdictional question between the Order and the Senate." His throat felt raw. "Six weeks, minimum. Optimistically."

"And the seal?"

Ben’s silence stretched between them, thick and elastic.

Desh’s nod carried the weight of a verdict. "Right." He reached past Ben toward the nav interface, his movements deliberate as a man defusing a bomb. "What are the coordinates?"

"I already loaded them."

"Then I'm just confirming the route." Desh pulled up the calculation screen. Sixty-two percent. The computer had been chewing on the problem like it was trying to solve the meaning of life instead of plotting a course through empty space. "Unmapped jumps. Multiple legs. Your ship's computer will need another twenty minutes, and then we're looking at a long transit."

"I know."

"I'm saying it so you have time to write the report while we wait." Desh’s voice could have sliced through durasteel. "If you're going to write it."

Ben stared at the blank screen. Luke’s face materialized in the pixels—careful, measured, asking questions that would lead to more questions. He could practically hear the Temple’s high chamber echoing with deliberation while whatever had broken through that twelfth chamber got further away, got stronger, got ready.

His fingers moved: *Jedi Knight Ben Skywalker, formal report, date—*

Deleted it.

"Your report-writing has gotten worse since the GAG days," Desh said. "I didn’t think that was possible. You once submitted a field assessment in terse field markers without action verbs."

"The situation didn’t require verbs."

"The situation always requires verbs, Ben. That’s what situations are."

Something tried to claw its way up Ben’s throat—a laugh, maybe. He swallowed it hard. "I'm thinking."

"I can see that. It’s making you very still." Desh picked up his mug, drank like a man hydrating for a long march. "For what it’s worth, the math isn’t wrong. Whatever’s in those coordinates, it’s not going to wait for a committee. That’s a real fact. You’re allowed to act on real facts."

"That's not what's stopping me."

Desh didn’t ask what was. He set the mug down and watched the nav computer work, the silence between them taking root.

The weight in the Force pressed harder—not threatening, just present. *Here. This way. Now.* Ben felt it behind his eyes, in the space between his shoulder blades, a compass that had forgotten every direction except one.

He could write the report. He could send it. He could sit in this cockpit for six weeks and wait for the Order to catch up to what he was already holding in his hands, what was already crawling under his skin.

He closed the comms interface.

The nav computer chimed like a dinner bell.

Route calculated. Three legs. Estimated transit: nine hours.

Desh’s hand rested on the hyperdrive lever. He didn’t pull it. He looked at Ben instead—clear-eyed in the way that meant he was about to say the thing that would make this real.

"You're not reporting this."

The cockpit air thickened.

Ben heard the recycled air moving through the vents, the soft tick of the hull contracting in deep space cold. He heard his own pulse counting down seconds he couldn’t get back.

"Not yet," he said.

Desh held his gaze for a moment that stretched like hyperspace itself. Then he nodded, once, and pulled the lever.

The stars stretched into infinity.

Blue-white light swallowed the viewport as the *Steady Returns* punched through into hyperspace. Ben had made this transition a thousand times, but this time the familiar compression felt like being folded into himself, the galaxy sealing around them like a tomb—

The wrongness didn’t fade.

It pressed in closer than it had been at the facility, closer than it had been in the system. Like the distance had been the only thing keeping it polite, and now that he was moving toward it, the gloves were off. It sat behind his sternum like a sustained note played just off-key—not loud, not painful, but impossible to ignore, the kind of sound that made teeth ache and vision blur at the edges.

He tried to settle into the transit. Let his mind go quiet the way it was supposed to, the way hyperspace usually encouraged, the way his mother had taught him when he was twelve and afraid of the dark.

The note sharpened to a knife point.

"You good?"

"Fine."

Desh looked at him the way he looked at instruments that were lying. "You’ve got the face."

"I don’t have a face."

"You have a face. It’s the one where you’re pretending something isn’t happening while it’s actively happening to you." He didn’t push further. He picked up his mug, found it empty, and set it down like he was putting it in evidence. "Nine hours. I'm going to sleep. You should too."

He stood, moved toward the rear of the ship with the unhurried efficiency of someone who’d learned to rest in worse places than a moving cockpit, in situations where sleep might be the last luxury they got.

Ben stayed where he was.

The hyperspace tunnel ran blue-white ahead of them, the light constant and cold and wrong. Nine hours to unmapped coordinates that someone had spent centuries making sure no one would find. The wrongness pressed steadily against him, not violent, not urgent—just patient as cancer, insistent as gravity.

They were heading toward it.

He hadn’t written the report.

He stared at the hyperspace tunnel and let both of those facts settle in his chest like stones he was going to have to carry for nine hours, maybe longer, maybe forever. Resolving them would require admitting something he wasn’t ready to name, something that tasted like his father’s particular brand of desperation mixed with his mother’s talent for solitary decisions.

The note played on, slightly off-key, growing louder with every light-year.
