The *Steady Returns* lurched out of hyperspace like a drunk missing a step.

Ben’s coffee sloshed sideways. The mug smacked the copilot console, flipped, and splattered the sensor readout in a brown fan. The freighter shuddered twice, then settled into realspace’s dull drone.

"Nav recal," Desh said, eyes locked on the board. "Needed a fixed point."

"You could’ve warned me."

"Did. You were sawing logs."

"Resting my eyes."

"In the copilot seat. Mouth open." Desh reached past, yanked a rag from behind the secondary panel, and held it out without looking. "You’ll need this."

Ben swabbed the board. Coffee had seeped between two of Desh’s field mods—a Terminus patchwork of three different manufacturers and zero respect for specs. An amber lamp blinked, steadied, blinked again.

"Stimtea or standard behavior?"

"Both." Desh’s fingers flew over keys he’d memorized, not built. "Tea didn’t help."

Ben kept wiping. The light steadied. Recycled air carried hot electronics and whatever solvent Desh mopped the deck with—sharp, chemical, enough to sting. Through the viewport, normal stars. No hyperspace blue. Just black.

Desh’s presence in the Force: a void the size of a man, warm at the edges. Quiet. For the first time in months, Ben didn’t have to brace against someone else’s mood pressing in. No Force static. No careful walls. Just a pilot running numbers and the soft click of chrono relays.

Ben tossed the rag. "Panel survive?"

"It’ll live." Desh tapped; the board chimed. "Three jumps to target. Recal’s clean—mostly."

Ben looked up. "Mostly."

Desh flicked to the jury-rigged display bolted to the port bulkhead. A column of amber flags—forty-seven—marched down the margin. Not errors. Hesitations. The nav computer shrugging: *Not my fault space looks wrong.*

"Logged since we left Terminus," Desh said. "Grav spikes. Spatial warps. Nothing on the charts. Computer files them under ‘sensor hiccup.’"

Ben leaned in. The flags clustered along their bearing, thickening toward the survey world. Same vector he’d felt tugging at him for three months.

"Not the sensors," he said.

"Nope." Desh’s tone stayed flat—no panic, just done with panic and moving on. "Triple-checked. Instruments agree with each other, not with physics. So they blame themselves."

Ben stared at the flags. Twelve hours of the ship quietly confessing it might be broken rather than admit space had buckled. All pointing the same direction.

He’d filed the wrongness as Jedi business—private, invisible. Desh had clocked it in half a day with spare parts and pilot instinct.

"Gets worse the farther we go," Desh said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"Council calls it—"

"Background noise. Echo residue." Ben’s voice hit like a dropped wrench. "Three indices."

Desh snorted, entered the next jump. Calculation took four slow seconds; amber flags pulsed, then held. Computer chimed ready.

"Your mystical bullshit giving you headaches yet?"

"Not headaches." Ben rubbed his temple, found his fingers already there. "More like... someone’s playing music in the next apartment. Can’t quite hear it, but you know it’s there."

"That’s worse. Headaches you can medicate." Desh leaned back, studied the nav plot. "This is just going to drive you crazy slow."

The casual diagnosis hung between them—GAG training showing through. Desh had always been the one who noticed when surveillance was getting to you, when the constant paranoia started eating at the edges. Different war. Same pattern.

Recal complete. The first survey world blossomed on the scanner—mass, atmosphere, orbit, the tidy stats of a rock no one had wanted until now.

The wrongness slammed into Ben’s skull—not dark, not presence, but a *lack*. The Force recoiled from that point like surf retreating before a wave. His ears rang with hollow silence. Both boards flickered; amber lights spilled down Desh’s display in a steady waterfall.

Ben’s fingers dug into the copilot’s armrest and didn’t let go.
