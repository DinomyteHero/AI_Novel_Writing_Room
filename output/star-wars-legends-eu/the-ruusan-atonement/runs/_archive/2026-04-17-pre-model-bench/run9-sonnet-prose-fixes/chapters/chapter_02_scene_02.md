The official survey record read uninhabited.

Ben tilted his datapad against the glare, rereading the sixty-two-year-old notation: WR-7741, Outer Rim, Class-D arid. Surveyed 50 ABY. No settlement indicators. No structural anomalies. No Force resonance. Filed. Stamped. Forgotten.

He looked up.

Ruins sprawled across three hundred meters of broken plateau—basalt blocks half-swallowed by wind-scoured sediment, uprights leaning like broken teeth. Not decades. Not centuries.

"Survey says uninhabited," Desh called, boots crunching gravel as he circled a fallen pillar. "Either they were blind or we're hallucinating."

"Survey says." Ben pocketed the pad. "Forty-eight-hour sweep. Clean read. Move on."

"And missed a city."

"Complex. Maybe ceremonial." Ben ran a spectral sweep of the nearest monolith—local basalt, shaped, not quarried. "Three thousand years minimum." He glanced at the sediment pack. "Possibly older."

Desh snorted. "So the team stared at three-thousand-year-old stone and wrote *no structural anomalies.*"

"Maybe they had a shuttle to catch." But Ben’s voice carried no conviction. Sixty-two years was yesterday. Orbital spectral sweeps should have flagged a mass this size even under three meters of dust. Someone had to choose not to see it.

The wind dropped between the stones—not gone, just lowered, as if someone had turned down a volume knob. Desh’s head canted; he’d noticed.

Ben opened himself to the Force.

It was there—no void, no wall—but the geometry was skewed. Reach north, find east. Ask for weight, get echo. The feedback argued with itself, like trying to tune a comlink stuck between frequencies.

No darkness. Just wrongness, patient and low, pressing behind his eyes like a thumb on glass.

"You good?" Desh asked. "You're breathing like I do after a five-klick run."

"Fine." Ben’s voice stayed steady, though his pulse had kicked up a notch. "Watch the western rim. Holler if anything moves."

He didn’t stumble. He was exact about that.

The central platform had survived best—a low circle of fitted blocks, seams tighter than his scanner’s resolution. Carvings began there, spiraling over three exposed faces. Deep grooves, geometry his training never named. Not Sith—too quiet. Not Rakatan—no lattice. Not any pre-Republic style on file.

Tool marks read non-metal, non-laser. Material removed without heat, without fracture. He’d seen that only in theory papers.

"These aren’t decorative." Ben traced the air above a carving without touching. "They're sequential. Like instructions."

"Instructions for what?"

"That's the question." The scanner beeped as it captured another segment. Dominant motif: interlocking circles stabbed through by a single jagged line. Repeat. Repeat. Not art—syntax.

Desh moved closer, his shadow falling across the stone. "You know how many survey teams would’ve killed to find something like this? Something that doesn’t fit the catalogs?"

"Right now I'm thinking about how many teams would have the sense to walk away." Ben’s mouth had gone dry. The wrongness was stronger here, pooling in the carved grooves like water seeking low ground.

He crouched, brushed grit away, and pressed two fingers to the largest circle.

Wrongness *shrieked* upward—not sound, not vision—an off-key chord that snapped his hand back before he chose. Stone burned against his skin, hotter than sun could manage. For an instant, the scrambled map snapped true: a vector, faint but fixed, pointing somewhere else.

His heart hammered triple time. The taste of copper filled his mouth.

"Ben." Desh’s voice, closer now. "Your hand is bleeding."

Ben looked down. A thin line of red welled where his palm had scraped against the stone’s edge. The pain registered late, sharp and clean.

"Just a scratch." He wiped it on his pant leg, already reaching for his scanner with the other hand. The vector had faded, but he could still feel its echo, like an afterimage burned into his retinas.

"You touched something you shouldn’t have."

"Professional hazard." But his voice cracked on the last word. He cleared his throat, started the scan again. The data would be clean. Objective. Something he could send to Luke without explaining how the stone had felt like it was humming at a frequency that made his teeth ache.

Desh watched him with that particular expression—the one that said he was cataloging tells, building a profile. "You want to tell me what actually happened there?"

"Nothing happened." Ben stood, wiping grit from his knees. "Just surprised by the texture."

"Right. And I'm surprised by space travel." Desh’s tone stayed level, but his eyes kept tracking Ben’s hands. "Those markings mean something to you."

"They're not in any database I know." Which was true enough. True enough to send.

Ben finished the scan, marking coordinates, composition, estimated age. Professional. Clean. When he transmitted the packet to Luke, he included everything except the way the wrongness had clawed up his arm like electricity seeking ground.

The vector pointed somewhere. Somewhere that felt like here but wasn’t. Somewhere that made his skin crawl when he thought about it too long.

He shut down the scanner. "Let’s finish the perimeter sweep."

Desh fell into step beside him, close enough that their shoulders brushed. "You know, for someone who claims nothing happened, you're walking like the ground might bite you."

Ben forced a grin—his mother’s grin, sharp enough to cut. "Maybe I'm just developing a healthy respect for three-thousand-year-old mysteries."

"Or maybe," Desh said, voice dropping to something that wouldn’t quite carry on the wind, "you just learned why the survey team chose not to see it."

Ben didn’t answer. But his hand found the scanner in his pocket, thumb brushing the transmit button that would send the rest—the feeling, the vector, the wrongness—to Luke in a separate packet.

He left it untouched.

First withholding.
