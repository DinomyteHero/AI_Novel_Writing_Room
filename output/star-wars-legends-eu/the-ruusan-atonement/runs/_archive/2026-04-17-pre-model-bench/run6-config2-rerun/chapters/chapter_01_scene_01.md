The Force had been off-key for three months, and Ben Skywalker was the only person in the Temple who couldn't stop hearing it.

He sat cross-legged on the meditation chamber's stone floor, hands resting palm-up on his knees. He maintained the posture Master Cilghal had drilled into him at fourteen: spine straight, shoulders level. The room offered everything it was supposed to: cool air, low light, and the soft ambient hum of several hundred Force-presences moving through their evening routines two floors above. Knights finishing training reports. Padawans in the refectory. His father, somewhere in the upper levels, likely reading dispatches while a cup of tea grew cold beside him.

The Temple breathed. Warm. Orderly. Correct.

Underneath that rhythm, persistent as a stressed crystal vibrating in an ancient pillar, pulsed that note.

Ben exhaled through his nose. Standard contemplative practice: observe the sensation, don't chase it, let it pass through rather than snagging on the mind. He had practiced this since he was nine years old. He was a Jedi Knight. He had survived Jacen Solo and Abeloth and the dark places at the galaxy's edge. A bad harmonic should have been trivial.

Except it wasn't.

The note refused to pass. It lodged in the back of his skull like a frequency the rest of the galaxy had agreed was inaudible, a message his nervous system simply declined to ignore. He pressed his attention toward it. The result remained the same: a low, grinding dissonance, flat against the world, like a singer in a Temple choir whose voice had slipped into a key the rest of the singers didn't recognize.

He opened his eyes and stared at the opposite wall.

Pale gray stone glowed with the Temple's embedded lighting. His reflection was a vague human shape, unresolved and blurred. It felt appropriate.

The first time he'd reported it was six weeks in. He had filed a formal perception report and flagged it to the senior council liaison. The response had been courteous, thorough, and useless. The liaison cross-referenced his report against residual disturbances from the Abeloth crisis, noted correlating data points, and concluded that Ben's sensitivity was consistent with prolonged exposure to extreme dark-side events. The subtext was clear: *You've been through a lot, Knight Skywalker. Give it time.*

Ben had stared at the screen, teeth gritting. He’d considered a follow-up, then thought better of it.

He tried his father next. Luke listened with that characteristic, total focus—the kind that made a person feel seen and considered. He acknowledged a disturbance in the Coruscanti subsector. He confirmed the Council had flagged it. He praised Ben’s acute perception. But Luke didn't hear the same thing Ben heard.

The conversation ended with a hand on Ben's shoulder and an assurance that these things took time to resolve. Ben nodded and left.

After that, he stopped asking.

Explaining the sensation required a vocabulary the Order didn't possess. Watching faces shift from engaged to carefully diplomatic cost him more than the dissonance itself. He made the calculation Skywalkers always made, the one he'd watched his father make a dozen times: carry the weight, keep it quiet, figure it out alone.

In the dry, self-critical corner of his mind, he knew this was a mistake. He also knew he was going to keep doing it.

He pressed his attention toward the dissonance again, mapping its edges like a bruise.

*Dark side,* he thought. *Check.* He knew that flavor. He had grown up in its shadow, watched it consume his cousin, and felt it radiating off Abeloth like heat from a reactor core. The dark side had texture: hunger, pressure, a gravity that pulled at the marrow.

This had none of that.

He pushed further. *Residual trauma echo.* Malachor, Nathema, the reaches at the galaxy's edge—he knew Force wounds could bleed into space for millennia. That had a quality, too: grief. Old, cold, and vast.

This had none of that either.

He held the dissonance in his mind, trying to hear it clearly. What made it hard to classify was what it lacked. No malice. No hunger. No rage. It wasn't a wound or a shadow.

It felt like the subsonic vibration found in the Temple's older wings, where the foundational resonance had shifted over centuries. Not damage. Not a threat. A load-bearing element, deep in the structure, that had been under strain for a very long time and was beginning to change pitch.

Something cold settled in his chest.

He sat with that realization, his breath slowing. The Order treated this as background noise—psychic aftershocks. He had half-accepted that framing because the alternative required believing he was perceiving something new, something the current framework of Jedi understanding couldn't explain.

It was a significant conclusion to reach alone in a dark room. It was also, he was certain, correct.

The Order's instruments weren't calibrated for this. His father's reassurance was based on incomplete data. The gap between Ben’s perception and the Council’s silence wasn't a symptom of his own damage.

It was information.

His hands tightened against the stone. Information required action, and action required resources he didn't have. It meant admitting the wrongness was real. It meant standing alone with a sound no one else could hear.

He pressed the heels of his hands against the floor and breathed. His shoulders had bunched toward his ears; he forced them down.

The wrongness thrummed, patient and immovable.

He remained there, working through the implications with the grim focus of a man who has found a structural fault in his own home, when the Force shifted.

Familiar. Warm. Immense, yet carried with a practiced, quiet ease.

Ben looked up.

Luke Skywalker stood in the open doorway. He held a datapad in one hand and wore the expression Ben had seen his entire life—the one that meant he'd already read the briefing, made his decision, and had come to deliver a mission.

For the first time in three months, the wrongness felt like a weight he might finally share.
