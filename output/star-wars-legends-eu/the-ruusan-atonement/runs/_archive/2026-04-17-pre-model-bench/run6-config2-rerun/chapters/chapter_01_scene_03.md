The contact list glowed in the cockpit's half-dark.

Ben had been staring at it for four minutes. The *Steady Returns* was fueled and cleared for departure, humming against the hangar deck. Through the forward viewport, the bay stretched in organized rows—maintenance droids, supply loaders, and a few other vessels in various states of prep. Temple light fell across the durasteel in warm amber. He felt the Order moving around him, a steady chorus of presences, each one oriented toward a purpose larger than itself.

He scrolled past Fen Karris.

Fen was reliable. Better than reliable—she had spent two years conducting survey work in the Outer Rim, and her situational awareness was the kind other Jedi cited in reports. She was also vividly present in the Force. Every thought she had arrived through their bond like light through a clean window.

Ben kept scrolling.

Darro Vel. Former Shadows operative, multiple commendations for independent fieldwork, currently between assignments. His file photo showed a man who asked questions until he had the complete picture, then asked more to verify the frame.

Ben’s thumb moved faster.

Lira Sond. He paused on her name. She was talented and trustworthy. She would ask him twice a day what he was sensing, and she would mean it as a partnership.

He closed the roster before the thought finished.

The personal contact list loaded. It wasn't Council-filtered or cross-referenced against active assignments. His thumb moved through the entries without stopping, past a dozen names attached to a dozen histories, and settled on one with no affiliation listed. No Order designation. No rank.

*Rolan, D. — private security, Terminus Station.*

He pressed the comm icon before he could hesitate.

The channel opened with the flatness of long-range encryption—a slight compression on the high frequencies, a faint static floor. He listened to it ring. Once. Twice. He imagined Desh in whatever unglamorous situation his current contract had provided. The fourth ring cut off mid-cycle.

"Rolan." The voice was flat as a landing pad, inflected with the specific exhaustion of someone who had learned that most surprises weren't worth the energy of a reaction.

"It's Ben."

A pause. Not surprise—recalibrating. "What did you break?"

"Nothing yet." Ben's fingers drummed against the console, then stilled. "I need a co-pilot. Outer Rim survey, six weeks minimum. Mid Rim corridor, so it's a dull route."

"Six weeks." Desh was running the math—contracts, timing, what he'd have to cancel. "Off the books?"

"Filed with the Temple. Not advertised."

Through the static, something mechanical moved—a rhythmic clank of metal, a ventilation system cycling. Desh was somewhere industrial, likely wearing grease-stained coveralls and likely not preparing the questions Ben had braced himself to deflect.

"When do you need me at Terminus?"

"Forty-eight hours."

"I'll be there."

Ben waited for the rest. The *why aren't you taking a Jedi*. The *what's this actually about*. He expected the questions that would require him to lie or explain, to defend a choice he’d made while watching his own hand move down the list like it belonged to a stranger.

Desh didn't ask.

"I'll transfer the route specs tonight," Ben said.

"Don't bother. I'll look at them when I'm there."

The channel closed.

Ben sat with the silence. The dissonance was still present—it had been for months, a structural flaw threaded through his life like a load-bearing beam gone hollow. But with the comm closed and Desh's voice still hanging in the air, the sensation felt more distant. Not gone. Crowded out.

He ran the pre-flight sequence.

The *Steady Returns* woke under his hands. Systems cycled in the familiar order, the low vibration of the repulsors climbing through the deck plating as the navigation computer pulled its departure request into the queue. Ben worked through the checklist by rote. His hands knew the ship the way they knew a lightsaber grip.

He had not asked Desh to explain himself. Desh had returned the favor. That was the architecture of their relationship, built on the understanding that some things didn't require explanation because both had been present for the relevant events. The Galactic Alliance Guard was a decade behind them. Neither brought it up.

Ben knew, in the cold and honest part of his mind, exactly why he had called Desh instead of Fen Karris or Lira Sond. Desh's mind was closed to him. Desh's judgment arrived through words and expression and the set of his shoulders—not through the Force, not through that constant ambient frequency of another person's inner life pressing against his awareness. With Desh, Ben held the metaphysical cards. He could feel the rot without anyone else feeling him feel it. He could be uncertain without broadcasting it to the stars.

He could be in control.

The hangar crew cleared him for departure. Ben eased the ship off the deck and moved toward the bay doors, the amber light sliding away as he passed through the atmospheric containment field. Coruscant opened beneath him—an endless lit grid, traffic lanes stacked in ordered corridors, a haze that never fully cleared. He took her up through the departure lanes, filed and standard. The Temple shrank in the aft sensor display.

He pushed the throttle forward.

The atmosphere thinned. The city's haze gave way to deep violet at the edge of space, and then to black. The stars came out hard and clean. Ben set the nav-computer to the pre-calculated jump and ran the final checks. The dissonance followed him out of the atmosphere, just as it had followed him for months through meditation and sleep.

He pulled the hyperdrive levers.

Starlines. The familiar lurch of transition took hold, the mottled blue-white of hyperspace replacing the hard black of realspace. 

This time the feeling did not mute. It always had before—that was the rule. The Force usually went distant in the between-space, like a signal losing its carrier wave. But the structural hollow in his chest didn't fade.

It sharpened.

In the fraction of a second between realspace and hyperspace, the wrongness stopped being ambient pressure and became a direction. An axis. A pull, like a compass needle swinging to find north.

The feeling knew where he was going.
