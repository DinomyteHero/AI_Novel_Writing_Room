The Jedi Knight roster on Ben's datapad had twelve names on it. He'd worked with most of them. Three were good enough that he'd trusted them at his back during situations where 'good enough' was the difference between walking out and not.

His thumb moved past all twelve.

He stood at the base of his ship's boarding ramp — a modified *Defender*-class corvette he'd requisitioned two years ago and quietly kept through three reassignments — while the hangar bay echoed around him with the sounds of other people's departures. The fueling umbilical had already disconnected. Pre-flight checklist was half-done. He had no reason to still be standing on the deck.

He opened his encrypted personal contacts and called Desh Rolan.

Three seconds of static, the lag of a long-range bounce routing through Nar Shaddaa's comm infrastructure, and then a voice came through with the particular quality of a man who had learned to answer calls in loud places without sounding like he was in one.

"You know," Desh said, "most people open with 'hello.'"

"I need you at the Temple docks in four hours."

A pause. Background noise filtered through — the distant rumble of Nar Shaddaa's perpetual machinery. "Four hours from now I was planning to eat something that didn't come from a vending unit."

"Pack it."

"Where are we going?"

"Unknown Regions. Survey mission. Wide discretionary authority." Ben crouched and checked the portside landing strut, running two fingers along the hydraulic housing. "It'll be interesting."

"'Interesting' is doing a lot of work in that sentence." Another pause. "Four hours?"

"Four hours."

"I'll need to push a meeting."

"Push it."

"Right." The background noise shifted slightly, like Desh had turned away from it. "Anything I should know going in?"

Ben straightened. "Bring the cold-weather gear."

Silence. Then: "Sure. See you in four."

The channel closed. Ben looked at the blank screen for a moment, then pocketed the datapad and went back to the walkaround.

Desh hadn't asked why Ben wasn't calling a Jedi. He never did. It was one of the things Ben had always appreciated about him — the absence of questions that both of them already knew the answers to. They'd spent two years in the same operational unit during the GAG's post-war restructuring, back when the Alliance was still trying to figure out what to do with an intelligence apparatus built on Jacen Solo's methods and staffed with people who'd learned to stop asking whose orders were legal. You didn't come out of that with clean hands or clean assumptions about institutional loyalty. You came out of it with a very specific understanding of which people you trusted and why.

Ben moved to the starboard thruster housing and checked the intake screen for debris. Clean. He noted it on the checklist.

He didn't think about whether calling Desh was the right choice. He'd made the right choice. Desh was experienced, reliable, and wouldn't complicate the mission with Force-related variables. The logic was clean.

The tension across his upper back didn't have an opinion about the logic.

---

Desh arrived at three hours and fifty-two minutes with a duffel bag, a thermal case, and the expression of a man who had, in fact, managed to eat something that wasn't from a vending unit. He tossed the duffel up the boarding ramp without breaking stride, caught Ben's nod, and returned it.

"You look like you've already been awake for six hours," he said.

"Eight."

"That explains the enthusiasm." Desh picked up the thermal case and followed Ben up the ramp. "I pushed the meeting. My contact was not pleased."

"Your contact will survive."

"I'm less certain than you are."

They strapped into the cockpit. Ben ran the final pre-launch sequence while Desh stowed his gear in the co-pilot's footwell and pulled up the secondary nav display without being asked. Old habit. They'd run enough joint ops that the division of tasks had become automatic — Ben on the primary systems, Desh on comms and secondary sensors, both of them operating with the compact efficiency of people who'd learned not to waste motion in close quarters.

Ben filed his departure notice with Temple Control, got clearance, and brought the repulsorlifts online.

"Survey mission," Desh said, watching the nav display populate. "Unknown Regions. How unknown are we talking?"

"The bearing points past the Outer Rim surveys."

"And the wide discretionary authority?"

"Means we decide on the ground what needs doing."

Desh was quiet for a moment. "That's either very good or very bad."

"Probably both." Ben eased the ship off the deck, and the hangar bay dropped away beneath them. The upper atmosphere opened ahead — Coruscant's perpetual grey-orange ceiling, lit from below by a city that had never decided to stop.

They climbed through traffic lanes, Ben threading the ship through the departure corridors with practiced economy, and the city's light spread out in every direction until it stopped being a city and started being a planet. Then they were through the upper atmosphere, and the stars came out hard and sharp against the black.

Ben held their heading and let the nav-computer calculate the jump window. Seventeen minutes to the edge of the gravity well. Desh ran a passive scan on the surrounding traffic and reported nothing of interest in a tone that suggested he hadn't expected anything different.

The wrongness was still there. It had been there since the Temple, since the salle, since eight months ago when Ben had first noticed it pulling at the edge of his awareness during morning forms. Coruscant's layered Force presence usually muffled it slightly — ten trillion people generated a kind of static that made individual signals harder to read. He'd half-expected it to ease as they cleared the upper atmosphere.

It didn't ease.

It sharpened.

Not louder, exactly. More like a signal cutting through interference it had been fighting for months. Ben kept his hands on the controls and let the sensation settle, not reaching for it, just letting it exist in his awareness the way you let a sound establish itself before trying to place it.

The nav-computer chirped. Jump window open.

"Ready?" he said.

"Since Nar Shaddaa," Desh said.

Ben pulled the hyperdrive levers.

The stars stretched into lines and the lines collapsed into the blue-white mottled tunnel of hyperspace, and the ship's instruments flickered once — a half-second ghost in the power readings that the diagnostic panel logged and dismissed.

Ben kept his attention forward.

The pull was still there. It had survived the jump, which it had no particular reason to do. Force signatures blurred in hyperspace, diffused by the same physics that made communication lag and sensor readings unreliable. What persisted through a jump was usually just the echo of whatever had been strongest before entry — a fading impression, not a signal.

This wasn't fading.

It was pointing. Southwest through his awareness, precise as a nav-chart bearing, drawing him forward through the blue dark with the patience of something that had been waiting a very long time and had finally stopped waiting.

Ben stared into the tunnel and did not say anything about it.

Desh, to his credit, was already asleep.
