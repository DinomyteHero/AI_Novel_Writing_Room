Luke's office smelled like caf gone cold and recycled air—the same combination Ben associated with every late briefing of his childhood, every mission debrief that had ended with him being told to sleep while adults made decisions. The holocomm on the desk was dark. A single lamp threw blue-white light across a workspace that looked lived-in rather than ceremonial, which was either encouraging or a sign that the Grand Master of the Jedi Order had stopped pretending to have work-life separation.

Luke slid the datapad across the desk without preamble.

Ben caught it and scrolled. Seventeen flagged reports, each stamped with a different surveyor's identifier, spanning eight months of Outer Rim coverage. "Only seventeen?" he said. "Must be a slow week."

"Read the third one."

He found it. The bearing matched—not approximately, but precisely, the kind of precision that stopped being coincidence around the fourth or fifth data point and became a line. Three of the reports had zoomed readings that matched the pull he'd been feeling in the salle, in the corridors, in his sleep. The same cold absence, documented in clinical notation by Jedi who had presumably filed their reports and moved on.

Ben set the datapad on the edge of the desk. "How long have you had these?"

"The most recent came in yesterday. The earliest—" Luke paused. "Eight months."

Ben looked at him.

"I know," Luke said.

"The Council filed them."

"Yes."

The lamp hummed between them. Ben picked the datapad back up, scrolled to the geographical overlay, and watched the bearing resolve into a line pointing southwest through the Outer Rim and into territory that didn't have a lot of survey coverage for reasons that generally boiled down to *here be things that eat ships*. He walked to the window. Coruscant's light grid spread to every horizon, a city that had given up on night a long time ago, and underneath the glass he could feel the wrongness doing what it always did—pulling, patient and cold, in exactly the direction that line pointed.

"It's not scar tissue," he said.

Behind him, he heard Luke lean back in his chair. "The historical precedents suggest—"

"I know what the historical precedents suggest." Ben kept his eyes on the city. "Nathema. Malachor. The Wound. Echoes that persist for centuries. I've read the same literature." He paused. "This isn't an echo. Echoes push. They're residue—heat from something that burned. This pulls."

Silence.

"It's been pulling for eight months," he said. "Getting incrementally worse. If it were scar tissue, it would be stable. Scars don't get worse."

More silence. Then Luke said, "Come sit down."

Ben turned. His father's face in the lamplight was doing the thing it did when Luke had already run the same analysis and arrived somewhere he didn't like—the careful stillness that wasn't peace, just discipline. Ben had learned to read that expression before he could read Basic.

He sat.

"I feel it," Luke said.

The admission landed differently than Ben expected. Not vindication, exactly. More like the ground shifting under a weight he'd been braced against, the resistance suddenly gone.

"I've felt it for six months," Luke continued. "My read is different from yours—I'm not certain it's active. But I'm not dismissing the possibility that you're right."

Ben kept his voice even. "What's your read?"

"Geological. Old damage expressing under new pressure. The galaxy has been through—"

"Dad."

Luke stopped.

The word sat in the air between them. Ben hadn't planned it; it had come out the way it did when the professional distance got too heavy to maintain. He watched his father's expression shift, just slightly, from Grand Master to something older and more tired.

"You've spent six months watching it get worse and filing it under *old damage*," Ben said. "Seventeen reports in eight months, all flagged, all filed. At what point does the filing system become the problem?"

Luke was quiet for a moment. "That's fair."

"I'm not trying to be fair. I'm trying to understand why the bearing on those reports points directly at an Unknown Regions coordinate and nobody has gone to look at it."

"Someone is going now." Luke reached across the desk and turned the datapad over, pulling up a mission authorization already drafted, already stamped with his seal. "Survey mission. Wide discretionary authority. The bearing, end to end."

Ben read the authorization. Solo deployment. His name in the assignment field, no partner listed.

"You want me specifically," he said.

"Your perception is calibrated to absence," Luke said. "Jacen. Abeloth. Vestara. What you lived through didn't just leave scars—it left you able to read silence in the Force the way most Jedi can't. That's not a comfortable thing to say, and I'm not saying it to—"

"I know what you're not saying it for." Ben set the datapad down. The authorization stared up at him. "You're saying my damage is useful."

"I'm saying your experience is relevant." A beat. "There's a difference."

Ben looked at his father. Luke met it without flinching, which was the most honest thing he'd done in the last ten minutes.

"The Ankhural Codex," Ben said. "The coordinate the bearing points to—is it in there?"

Something moved behind Luke's eyes. "One entry. Labeled *lattice extraction*. The Codex doesn't explain the term."

"No," Ben said. "It wouldn't."

He stood, moved back to the window. The pull was there, constant, threading southwest through everything the city threw at his senses. He'd been feeling it for eight months and it had never once felt like it was pointing toward something that was going to be fine.

"If I find something," he said. "Something that isn't scar tissue. Something active."

"Comm in. We'll respond."

"And if comms are compromised? If whatever is out there—"

"Ben." Luke's voice was quiet. "I know."

Ben turned. His father was watching him with an expression that had nothing professional in it—just the weight of a man who had sent people he loved into bad situations before and was doing it again because the math kept coming out the same way.

Ben picked up the datapad.

He thought about saying *send someone with me*. The words were right there. He could feel the shape of them, the simple practicality of the ask—another set of eyes, another saber, another body between him and whatever was at the end of that bearing. He'd supervised GAG operations with three-person minimums for situations with half this uncertainty.

He slipped the datapad into his jacket.

"I'll file departure paperwork tonight," he said. "Launch by oh-six-hundred."

Luke nodded once. His Force presence—vast, warm, familiar as the sound of a ship's engines—pressed forward slightly, the way it did when he was choosing not to say something.

He didn't say it.

Ben walked to the door.
