The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

Ben stepped back, absorbing the impact, and deactivated his saber. The practice weapon's hum died. His partner's followed a second later.

"You good, Skywalker?"

"Fine." He rolled the shoulder once. "Fatigue. Call it."

His partner powered down, gave him a nod that was professional enough not to be pitying, and left. The salle doors hissed shut.

Ben stood in the center of the mat and did not move for a long moment.

Third time this week. The shoulder would bruise impressively—a completely undignified injury for a Jedi Knight to report, sustained during a standard Temple drill against a training blade set to minimum intensity. His father had once held off a Sith Lord for twenty minutes with a broken wrist. Ben had just lost a timing exchange to a sparring routine he'd run three hundred times. The Skywalker brand was taking a beating.

He set the hilt on the equipment rack and walked the perimeter of the room.

The salle was empty, its overhead panels dialed down to late-evening amber. The Temple pressed in around him—not with threat, but with presence, the quiet weight of several hundred Force-sensitive minds going about their evening. He'd grown up with that hum as a constant backdrop, so familiar it had long since dropped below conscious awareness. Like the sound of a ship's engines.

Except tonight, under it, there was the other thing.

He'd stopped trying to name it precisely. The closest he'd gotten was a note played flat—not wrong in a way that screamed, just wrong in a way that caught at the back of his perception every time he reached for the Force and found the pitch half a degree off from where it should be. The rest of the Order heard it as background noise, post-Abeloth residue, the galaxy still settling after two years of catastrophic disturbance. Master Cilghal had noted it in three reports. The Council had acknowledged those reports and filed them.

Ben picked up his hilt again and settled into opening stance. No ignition. Just the weight of the grip, the cold metal, his boots on the floor.

He moved through Shii-Cho's first sequence.

The forms were clean. His body knew these patterns the way it knew how to breathe—below thought, below effort. Diagonal cut, parry high, pivot left, recover. No hesitation, no drift. Whatever was wrong with him, it wasn't in his muscles or his training. He pushed through the second sequence, then the third, faster now, letting his attention spread outward the way you had to in a real fight, reaching for that edge-of-perception awareness that turned reaction into anticipation.

There. The flat note, again. Not a pain, not a warning—just a gap where a resonance should be, like pressing a key on a dejarik board and hearing nothing.

He stopped mid-pivot.

The wrongness wasn't getting worse. That was almost the most unsettling part. It wasn't building toward something, wasn't the rising pressure of a dark side presence or the psychic static that had preceded every major crisis he'd lived through. Jacen had felt like standing in a room where the temperature kept dropping and pretending the cold wasn't there. Abeloth had felt like a scream with too many voices layered inside it, each one wrong in a different register.

This was neither of those things.

This was structural. He'd been circling that word for weeks without wanting to commit to it, because committing to it meant admitting the implication: that whatever he was perceiving wasn't an event or a presence but a condition. Not a wound bleeding—a load-bearing element under stress. The difference mattered. You could address a wound. You could fight a presence. A structural failure in the Force itself was a different category of problem, and the Order's current approach of filing reports and waiting to see if it resolved on its own struck Ben as the kind of plan that worked well right up until the ceiling came down.

He ran the pivot again. Reached out again. Hit the gap again.

It didn't disrupt his physical motion—not when he was moving without the Force. The moment he touched it, tried to use it for anything faster than deliberate conscious action, the timing stuttered. A half-second. Enough, in a real fight, to lose a shoulder. Or something worse than a shoulder.

Ben lowered the hilt to his side and breathed.

The Order wasn't wrong that the galaxy had been through catastrophic disturbance. They weren't wrong that residual distortion was a reasonable explanation. What they were wrong about was the assumption that all distortions eventually cleared. He'd been in enough bad situations to know the difference between a storm passing and a foundation cracking, and the Force had been doing the latter for eight months now—quietly, persistently, with the patience of something that had been going on for a very long time before anyone noticed.

He wasn't going to muscle his way through this by running forms harder. He'd known that for three weeks and had been running forms harder anyway, because the alternative was going to the Council with a problem he couldn't define, couldn't source, and couldn't prove was anything other than post-trauma hypersensitivity in a Jedi who had spent too many formative years adjacent to people who broke the Force for a living.

That was the other explanation, of course. That he was calibrated wrong. That years around Jacen and Abeloth and Vestara had tuned his perception to pick up absences that weren't significant, the way you kept flinching at sounds that weren't there after a firefight. Possible. He kept the possibility honest in his thinking because dismissing it entirely would be its own kind of bias.

But he didn't believe it.

The flat note was real. It had a location—not precise, not yet, but directional, the way you could tell which room a sound was coming from without being able to name the room. It was pulling southwest through his awareness, and it had been doing so for eight months, and it was getting incrementally, persistently worse.

He set the hilt back on the rack.

The salle felt very empty. Down the corridor, he could feel the presence-signatures of two Knights heading toward the dining hall, one Padawan running late for something, the distant warm weight of the meditation wing. Ordinary evening, ordinary Temple, everyone doing what they were supposed to be doing. He'd spoken to no one about this except in official reports, and the official reports had gone into files, and the files had generated no action.

Skywalkers handled things. That was the family tradition, right alongside spectacular collateral damage and complicated relationships with the dark side.

He was handling it. He just hadn't figured out how yet.

The salle doors hissed open behind him.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway, a datapad in one hand, his posture carrying that particular quality of stillness that meant he'd already made a decision and was waiting to explain it. Ben knew that expression. He'd grown up reading it across dinner tables and briefing rooms and the occasional burning building.

It meant a mission.
