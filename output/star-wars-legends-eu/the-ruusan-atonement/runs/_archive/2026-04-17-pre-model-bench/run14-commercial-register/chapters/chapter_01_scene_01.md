The training blade cracked across Ben’s ribs on the half-beat he should have controlled.

Ben stepped back, jaw clenched against the sharp breath that wanted out. Not from the hit—Rees hadn’t aimed to hurt him. From the way his body had failed him exactly when he needed it most. He’d read her weight shift, seen the opening form, felt the strike coming like a question with a known answer. Then that note had gone flat—not wrong, just a familiar chord resolving a half-step off—and his block had arrived late enough to sting.

He deactivated his training saber. The hum died between them like a conversation out of useful things to say.

“That’s on me,” he said.

Rees lowered her blade, eyebrows rising. A solid Knight, three years his junior in the field and twice as careful about showing it. “You telegraphed nothing. I don’t know what I hit.”

“My ego, mostly.” Ben rolled his shoulder, testing for damage that wasn’t there. “I’m calling it.”

“We’ve got another twenty minutes on the room.”

“I know. You take it.” He unclipped the safety brace and set it on the rack, not looking at her. “I haven’t been sleeping well.”

The lie settled easy on his tongue. Sleep had been patchy, sure, but sleep wasn’t what had turned his timing into a joke.

Rees toweled off, watching with the careful expression of someone debating charity. She decided against it. “Residual interference, probably. Master Cilghal’s team put out a notice—lingering effects from the Abeloth crisis cycling through the deeper meditation bands. Half the senior Knights reported attention drift.”

“That’s probably it,” Ben said.

She left him to the salle’s quiet, and the door hissed shut like it was glad to be rid of them both.

---

The room held the weight of late evening—the Temple’s presence pressing against his senses like a warm, muffled hand against transparisteel. Ben stood center floor and let it settle. Hundreds of Jedi, their signatures a low register he’d grown up inside, breathing and meditating and sparring in other rooms. The Temple had its own rhythm. He’d learned to read it before he could name what he was reading.

The wrongness didn’t belong to that rhythm. Quieter than the Temple’s hum and more insistent, like a string tuned down a fraction and left vibrating under everything else.

Two reports in three months. Both tagged ANOMALY—UNCLASSIFIED with a note to cross-reference against the post-Abeloth residual index. He had. It wasn’t there. The sparring failures were new—the fifth time this week the flat note had cost him a beat he couldn’t afford to lose.

*Attention drift.* A reasonable explanation. The Order had been through enough in the past decade to fill a library of reasonable explanations. Ben understood the appeal. Reasonable explanations let you sleep without wondering if you were slowly losing your mind.

He picked up his unlit training saber and began to move.

Not a warm-up—something harder. Shii-Cho, long and complex, drilled until the footwork was cellular memory, then built into Djem So transitions that required Force timing to execute cleanly. He ran it without igniting the blade. No hum. No light. Just his body moving through the geometry, testing the hypothesis he hadn’t let himself speak aloud.

His feet hit their marks. Weight transferred on time. The transitions were clean, balance points exactly where they needed to be.

He ran it again, faster.

Still clean.

He stopped mid-third pass and stood there, the empty hilt loose in his hand, letting the implication settle like dust after an explosion.

His body was fine.

Reflexes sharp, timing intact, balance good. The failures hadn’t been fatigue or drift or the accumulation of a difficult year. His muscles hadn’t forgotten anything. His proprioception was surgical.

Which meant the failures were happening specifically when he reached through the Force to confirm what his body already knew.

He ran the last transition again—the point where Djem So required a half-second of Force-augmented anticipation to avoid overextending. Without the Force, he pulled the motion short, compensated with footwork, made it work. With the Force, three times this week, the anticipation had arrived late. Off-rhythm. Like reaching for a handhold in the dark and finding it a centimeter lower than muscle memory insisted it should be.

The flat note. The half-step off.

Ben had been in enough bad situations to know the difference between dark side contamination and trauma echo and what he was feeling now. Dark side contamination pressed and offered and tasted like smoke and copper. Trauma echo resonated with personal memory, the Force showing you what you’d already survived. He’d lived through both. He had the reference points.

This was neither.

This was structural. Like a weight-bearing element that had shifted in a wall—not collapsed, not burning, just no longer quite where the rest of the building expected it to be.

He’d felt something like it before, twice. Once when he was seventeen, in the months before anyone had a name for what Jacen was becoming. Once during the Abeloth crisis, in the moment before the worst of it. Both times, the wrongness had been a precursor. Both times, he’d dismissed it until dismissal became impossible.

He didn’t want to be the person who dismissed it a third time.

The problem was what to do with that. File a third report and watch it come back tagged again? Bring it to the Council and watch them listen carefully and explain, gently, that prolonged exposure to extreme events could produce persistent perceptual distortions? He knew how that conversation ended. He’d sat in enough of them on the other side, reassuring junior Knights that what they were sensing was probably grief or exhaustion or the lingering resonance of a battlefield.

He’d believed it when he said it.

He wasn’t sure he believed it anymore.

Ben clipped the training saber to his belt and stood center floor, the Temple’s warmth pressing around him and the flat note underneath it, patient and quiet and wrong in a way he couldn’t file in triplicate and put away. Skywalkers were supposed to be the ones who didn’t miss things. The reliable anchors. The people the Order called when something had gone past the point where reassurance worked.

He’d spent a long time earning that reputation. He wasn’t going to walk it into the Council chamber on a suspicion and two rejected reports.

He’d find it himself. Whatever it was. He’d track it down and understand it and then bring the Order something they couldn’t tag and dismiss.

The thought sat uncomfortably in his chest, because he recognized it, and he recognized it wasn’t entirely about the Force.

The presence at the door registered before he turned.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad, wearing the expression Ben had first learned to read at fourteen—the particular set of his father’s mouth that meant *something has come up* and *I need you for it* and *I’m sorry to interrupt* all at once.
