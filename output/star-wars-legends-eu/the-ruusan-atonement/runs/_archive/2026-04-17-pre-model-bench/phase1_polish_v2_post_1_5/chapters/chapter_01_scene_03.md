The hangar smelled like fuel polymer and hot metal, the particular combination that meant a ship had been sitting pre-flight long enough for the recycled air to start cooking off the residue in the vents. Ben's ship — a *Horizon*-class scout, nothing impressive, which was the point — sat in bay twelve with its running lights already cycling through their pre-departure sequence. He'd filed the paperwork at 0600. The fueling crew had come and gone.

He was still in the pilot's chair with the datapad in his lap.

The partner roster had forty-three names on it. Jedi Knights available for short-notice assignment, sorted by specialty. He'd opened it three times in the last ten minutes.

His thumb moved down the list. Passed Corran Horn's apprentice, who was good in the field and asked sharp questions. Passed Valin, who he'd trusted with his back more than once and who would notice everything Ben was doing and say nothing — and somehow that was worse. Passed a Knight two years his junior who he barely knew, and stopped on that one for a moment, because at least she wouldn't have context for whatever she observed.

He closed the roster.

The hangar echoed. Somewhere in the bay below, a maintenance droid ran a calibration sequence on a set of repulsorlifts, the whine cycling up and down in a pattern that never resolved. Ben watched the running lights on his own ship blink through their sequence and opened his personal contacts instead.

Desh Rolan was three names from the top. Ben pressed call before he spent another ten minutes not pressing it.

The long-range channel took a few seconds to establish. Static, then more static, then Desh's voice crackling through with the particular flatness of a signal bouncing off two relay stations.

"Skywalker. It's early."

"It's 0700."

"I'm on Nar Shaddaa time." A pause. "What do you need?"

Ben leaned back. The pilot's chair creaked under him. "Survey run along the Outer Rim. Two weeks, possibly three. I need someone who can handle logistics on the ground and doesn't ask me questions I can't answer yet."

Silence on the channel. Not hesitation — Desh had never hesitated in the eleven years Ben had known him, not once, not even during the GAG's last operational year when the questions they weren't asking each other had grown heavy enough to require active maintenance. This was the silence of a man checking his schedule.

"I've got a contract through the sixteenth."

"I'm departing tomorrow morning."

"The sixteenth was yesterday." Another pause. The static shifted pitch, some relay adjustment on the far end. "Send me the departure brief. I'll be at Docking Bay Aurek-Seven at 0800."

Ben exhaled. The way Desh didn't ask *why not a Jedi* — that small omission — was the entire reason Ben had called him. Desh understood the architecture of questions that didn't get asked. He'd spent four years learning it inside an organization where the wrong question at the wrong time could end a career or a life, a post-war security force that had done necessary work in ways that left marks on everyone who'd done it. He'd walked away from the GAG when it became something he couldn't operate inside anymore, and he'd never once suggested Ben should have walked away sooner. Ben had never once suggested Desh should have stayed longer. Eleven years of that particular courtesy had built something that didn't require explanation.

"Appreciated," Ben said.

"Don't thank me until I see the brief." Desh's voice was dry enough to sand durasteel. "Last time you said *survey run*, I spent six hours in a Hutt's cargo manifest."

"This one has fewer Hutts."

"Fewer isn't none."

"I'll send the brief." Ben ended the call.

The maintenance droid below finished its calibration sequence. The whine cut off and the hangar went quiet in a way that felt pointed, like the building itself had been waiting for him to make a decision and was now satisfied. Ben set the datapad on the co-pilot's seat and reached for the pre-flight console.

The wrongness was present in the hangar the same way it had been in his father's office — low, persistent, occupying the register just below conscious attention. He'd learned to stop checking it the way you learned to stop checking a bruise. Checking it didn't change anything. It was still there when you looked.

He ran through the pre-flight checklist. Repulsorlifts nominal. Shields nominal. Hyperdrive motivator reading clean, which it wouldn't be by the end of the trip if the last service estimate was accurate, but that was a problem for later. Navigation system had already loaded the survey route.

Ben powered up the repulsorlifts and lifted off.

Bay twelve fell away below him. He guided the ship through the hangar's exit corridor, past the traffic control node where a bored-looking tech waved him through without looking up, and out into the lower levels of Coruscant's airspace. The city spread in every direction — towers and transit lanes and the particular density that stopped being impressive somewhere around the ten-thousandth time you looked at it. He'd grown up here. He knew the traffic patterns the way he knew his own breathing.

He climbed through them without thinking about it.

The upper atmosphere thinned. The city lights resolved into a continuous glow beneath him, and then that too fell away as he cleared the exosphere and Coruscant's orbital traffic control handed him off to departure routing. He filed his heading. The controller gave him a lane. Standard procedure, the kind of thing that happened three thousand times a day above this planet.

Ben set the course and pushed the throttle forward.

The stars were clear at this altitude, unhazed by atmosphere. He held the heading for the calculated distance from the planet's gravity well, watching the nav computer count down to the jump window, and did not think about the forty-three names he hadn't called.

The counter hit zero.

He pulled the hyperdrive lever.

The stars stretched into lines and the lines collapsed into the blue-white tunnel of hyperspace, and the wrongness —

Didn't stop.

It should have. Force perception in hyperspace was unreliable at best — the folded geometry of the jump disrupted most subtle readings, which was why Jedi survey teams always noted their pre-jump and post-jump readings separately. The wrongness had no business persisting through the transition. No business being detectable in hyperspace at all.

But it was there. Low, persistent, unchanged.

Then, between one breath and the next, it shifted.

Not stronger. Not weaker. It acquired direction the way a compass needle acquires north — a subtle, implacable pull with no visible source and no need of interpretation. It wasn't pointing at anything he could name. It was simply pointing.

Ben sat with that for a moment. The hyperspace tunnel streamed past the viewport, indifferent.

He'd told himself he was running a survey. Baseline data, his father had said. Assess whether there's a pattern, report back.

The pull was patient and specific and had been waiting, apparently, for him to get far enough from Coruscant to hear it clearly.

He checked his heading against the nav computer. The survey route ran almost exactly along the direction of the pull, the way two lines drawn from different starting points will sometimes converge on the same destination without either of them intending it.

Ben left the heading unchanged. He didn't adjust anything.

The wrongness held its direction, steady as a beacon, pointing into the dark.
