The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back before his brain had finished registering the hit.

Third time this week.

"Good match." His sparring partner — Jedi Knight Orev, two years his senior and currently trying not to look pleased with himself — deactivated his training saber and clipped it to his belt. He managed it for about four seconds. "Rematch?"

"I'm done." Ben rolled his shoulder, more from habit than pain. The blade had left nothing but faint heat and a bruise his ego would carry longer than his body. "Early start tomorrow."

Orev accepted this without comment. The door hissed shut behind him, and the training salle contracted into quiet.

Ben stood in the middle of the floor, saber hilt loose in one hand, and reviewed the last exchange with the dispassionate clarity his mother had called his best quality and his worst. The footwork had been clean. The angle of his parry had been textbook. His Force read on Orev's attack had been accurate — he'd felt the shift in weight, the telegraphed shoulder drop, the half-breath before the strike.

He'd just been a half-second late acting on it.

Not slow. Late. There was a difference, and it was the difference that had been eating at him for six days.

He walked to the equipment rack and exchanged his training saber for the real one, then left it unignited. The salle was empty this late — Temple curfew wasn't enforced for Knights, but the junior Padawans were long gone, and most of his peers had more interesting things to do on a Coruscant evening than run forms in a room that smelled like recycled air and old floor polish. Ben had always found the smell comforting, in the way familiar discomfort could be. Tonight it just meant he was alone.

He started with Shii-Cho. The opening form, the one they taught children, the foundation everything else was built on. His body moved through it the way breathing moved through him — no thought required, no Force required, just muscle and balance and the geometry of violence reduced to its simplest elements.

Perfect. Every beat exactly where it should be.

He transitioned into Makashi, then Soresu. Same result. His footwork was clean, his transitions were clean, his timing was exactly what twenty-odd years of training and three years of actual combat had made it. Nothing wrong with his body.

He stopped in the middle of a Djem So opening sequence and stood still.

The wrongness was there right now, low and persistent — a droning instrument played a quarter-tone flat, undercutting an otherwise clean piece of music. Not loud. Not painful. Just wrong, in a way that made the part of him that read the Force hesitate before committing to information it had always trusted. Like reaching for a handhold in the dark and finding it a few centimeters lower than expected.

He'd reported it to the Council three weeks ago. Master Sebatyne had listened with her customary patience and suggested residual disturbance from the Abeloth crisis — the Force was still settling, she'd said, the way water settled after something large moved through it. Master Cilghal had run a medical scan and found nothing. His father had listened carefully and said he'd look into it, which was exactly what his father said when he was already looking into it and didn't want to worry anyone.

Ben trusted his father's instincts more than almost anything. He also knew his father had seventeen active problems at any given moment and had learned, somewhere around age twelve, that "I'll look into it" sometimes meant "this has to wait."

He resumed the form. Djem So into Shien, Shien into a Jar'Kai sequence he ran single-handed for the muscle work, and through all of it the wrongness persisted, a low drone beneath the clean mechanics of his own performance. His body was fine. His mind was fine. The Force was present, available, responding to his draws on it without hesitation.

It was just wrong.

He stopped again, breathing steady, and this time he didn't immediately move. The salle hummed around him — ventilation, the distant vibration of a city that never actually slept, the faint pressure of other Force presences deeper in the Temple, diffuse and calm. All of it familiar. All of it exactly as it should be.

Except for the note underneath.

Ben had spent three years as a teenager watching Jacen Solo erode into something unrecognizable, and one of the things he'd learned from that — one of the things he'd paid for in ways he didn't revisit often — was what a structural wrongness felt like compared to an emotional one. Jacen's darkness had been an emotional resonance. Heavy. Personal. The Force reading of a man making specific choices in specific directions.

This wasn't that.

This was impersonal. Diffuse. Not a presence but an absence, not weight but a gap where weight should be — like pressing your tongue to a tooth and finding the tooth wasn't there anymore. It didn't feel like someone doing something. It felt like something that was supposed to be there wasn't.

He'd felt something in the same register twice before. Once at Centerpoint, during the crisis with Abeloth, when the Force itself had seemed to strain at its own joints. Once at the edge of the Maw, on a survey mission he wasn't supposed to talk about, when they'd flown close enough to a dead zone that the Force had gone thin and strange for six hours.

Both times, everyone around him had felt it.

He'd asked around carefully after the first time this current wrongness spiked. Subtle questions, framed as curiosity rather than concern. The answers had been politely unhelpful. Background noise. Residual disturbance. The Force settling. Everyone else's instruments were reading clean, and the consensus was that Ben Skywalker had perhaps been through enough in the last decade that his own instrument might need recalibration.

He didn't think they were right. He also couldn't prove they were wrong.

That was the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He was a Skywalker. He'd been trained by his father and his mother and half the Jedi Council. He'd faced things that would have broken people twice his age, and he'd come through them intact — or close enough to intact that the difference wasn't visible. The idea that his perception was compromised, that the wrongness was his problem rather than the Force's problem, was the one explanation he kept turning over and the one he couldn't fully dismiss.

The one he couldn't afford to accept, either, because if he was wrong about this, he was wrong in a way that didn't matter.

But if he was right —

The salle doors opened.

His father stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben had catalogued years ago: the particular set of Luke Skywalker's shoulders that meant he'd already made the calculations, chosen the operative, and was now delivering the assignment in person because the mission was either important enough or complicated enough that he wanted to see Ben's face when he heard it.

Ben's saber was already cold. He waited.
