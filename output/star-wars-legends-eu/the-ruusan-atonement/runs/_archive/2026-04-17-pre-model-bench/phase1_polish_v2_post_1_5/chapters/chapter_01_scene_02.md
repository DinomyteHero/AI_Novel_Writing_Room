Luke's office smelled like old flimsiplast and the particular stillness that collected in rooms where important things were decided. Ben stepped inside and the door sealed behind him.

His father was already sliding the datapad across the desk.

No preamble. That was Luke's way when the thing itself was the briefing.

Ben picked it up. The blue glow caught under his chin as he scrolled — seventeen flagged reports, each tagged with the same anomaly classification, each timestamped across a six-month window that mapped almost exactly to when the wrongness had started pulling at the edge of his perception. Survey teams. Garrison posts. One deep-range hyperspace relay station that shouldn't have had anyone sensitive enough to file an anomaly report at all.

He zoomed in on three of the readings. His thumb stopped.

The visual representation was a standard Force-resonance plot, the kind the Order's survey teams generated by the dozens every month. He'd read hundreds of them. These three looked wrong in a way he recognized from the inside — a gap in the baseline curve where there should have been a gradual slope, like a weight removed from one side of a scale so slowly that only the scale knew it was gone.

"Seventeen," he said.

"Seventeen confirmed." Luke leaned back in his chair. "Possibly more. The relay teams don't always have the sensitivity to catch it at threshold."

Ben set the datapad on the desk without putting it down entirely, keeping his fingers on the edge. The wrongness was present here too, even in the Temple — faint, the way a bad circuit smelled before it failed, something under the familiar warmth of his father's presence that had no business being there. Luke's Force signature filled the room the way it always had, vast and steady and bone-deep familiar. But the note beneath it was still there. Low. Persistent.

He'd spent three weeks being told his instrument needed recalibration.

"The bearing," Ben said. He pulled up the map overlay and the flagged reports resolved into a line. Not quite straight — no anomaly ever plotted clean — but directional. Running along the Outer Rim like a crease pressed into old cloth. "It's moving."

"Or the degradation is spreading from a fixed point and we're reading it as movement." Luke's voice was even, the tone he used when he'd already worked through an argument and was presenting it as a possibility rather than a conclusion. "The Council's current read is residual disturbance. Cascading effects from Abeloth."

Ben looked up.

His father met his eyes with an expression that was doing a lot of careful work.

"You don't think it's Abeloth," Ben said.

A pause. Not long. Luke reached over and turned the datapad slightly, adjusting the angle as if he needed to see it again. "I think the Abeloth explanation is coherent with the data we have."

"That's not what I asked."

The room was quiet for a moment. Somewhere deeper in the Temple, he could feel the diffuse presence of Padawans in evening meditation — clean, untroubled, nothing like the sourness that sat under everything now.

"No," Luke said. "I don't think it's Abeloth."

The admission sat between them. Ben waited.

"I've felt it since the spring." His father's voice dropped — not in volume, but register, the tone he used in early mornings when it was just the two of them and the weight of everything didn't require a professional frame. "I kept thinking it would resolve. That it was the Force finding its own balance after—" He stopped. Started again. "It hasn't resolved."

Ben looked at the three readings he'd zoomed in on. Jacen had felt wrong for two years before Ben understood what he was reading. Abeloth had felt wrong in a way that was enormous and obvious and still managed to be worse than expected. Vestara's absence in the Force — the particular silence she'd left — had taught him what it felt like when something was removed rather than broken.

This felt like the third thing. Not broken. Removed.

He didn't say that. Not yet. His father wasn't wrong about the data being ambiguous — he was wrong about what the ambiguity meant, and that was a different argument.

"The survey route," Ben said.

Luke slid a second document across the desk. Ben scanned it: a twelve-stop itinerary along the Outer Rim, hitting six of the seventeen flagged sites with three additional observation points built in. Standard survey configuration. One ship, one Jedi, a two-week window.

The route ran directly along the bearing.

"The Council wants baseline data before committing resources," Luke said. "Ground-truth the anomaly readings, assess whether there's a pattern, report back." A beat. "It's a survey mission, Ben. Not an engagement."

"I know what a survey mission is."

The words came out flat. He didn't soften them. Luke didn't react — just watched him with that particular patience that had always made Ben feel, in equal measure, deeply known and slightly crowded.

Ben set the second document on top of the first and walked to the window.

Coruscant spread out below — the upper-city lights blurring into a continuous glow at this altitude, traffic lanes threading between towers in patterns that looked random from any single angle and resolved into something coherent only when you stepped back far enough. He'd grown up looking at this view from different windows in this building. It had always looked like a problem someone else was in the middle of solving.

"I'll take it," he said.

"The departure window is—"

"I'll read the brief." He turned from the window. "Is there anything operationally relevant that isn't in the documents?"

Luke looked at him for a moment. The warm steadiness of his presence was unchanged — it never changed, which was one of the things about his father that Ben had stopped being able to decide was a comfort or a burden. "Not operationally, no."

The *not operationally* hung there, an open door neither of them walked through.

Ben picked up the datapad and tucked it under his arm. The three anomaly readings were still on the screen, the gap in the baseline curve patient and precise. His own read on them was sharper than the data suggested it should be, and he knew why, and he wasn't going to explain it in this room tonight.

"I'll file my departure paperwork in the morning," he said.

Luke nodded. He didn't say *take someone with you*. He didn't say *be careful* in the way that meant something was actually dangerous. He said, "I'll be reachable on the secure channel if the readings change."

"Understood."

The door opened. Ben stepped through it.

Neither of them had mentioned a partner. The silence on that point was complete and mutual, and Ben carried it down the corridor with him like the datapad under his arm — something with weight, something with answers in it, something he wasn't ready to look at directly yet.
