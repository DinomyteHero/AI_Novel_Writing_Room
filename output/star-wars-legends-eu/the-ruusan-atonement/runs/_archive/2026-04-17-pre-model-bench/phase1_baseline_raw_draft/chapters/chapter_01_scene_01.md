The blade was coming in high-left, and Ben already knew the angle.

He knew the timing too — the Force feeding it to him clean, the arc of the strike laid out like a diagram. His guard should have risen. He was already telling his arm to rise. And then, between the intention and the motion, something went flat, like a string that drops a quarter-tone without warning. His arm came up half a beat behind the moment.

The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

It wasn't a hard hit. Padawan-level contact, the kind that left a sting and nothing else. The kind that shouldn't happen to a Knight who'd survived Centerpoint and the Maw and three separate occasions where Sith Lords had tried to remove his head. Ben stepped back and let his training saber drop to his side, not quite meeting his partner's eyes.

"Good," he said. Because it was, technically.

"You okay?" His partner — one of the newer Knights, Corellian accent, fast footwork — lowered her blade. She wasn't suspicious. She was being polite.

"Tired." Ben rolled his shoulder once, feeling the sting settle. "Long week."

She accepted that. He watched her deactivate her saber and collect her water flask from the bench by the wall, and he kept his expression easy until the salle door closed behind her and the room was quiet.

Third time in five days.

Ben set his training saber on the bench and didn't sit down. The salle was empty now except for the low hum of the ventilation system and the faint, ever-present warmth of the Temple's collective presence — hundreds of Force-sensitives living and sleeping and meditating under one roof, a background resonance so familiar he usually stopped noticing it. He noticed it now because underneath it, threading through it at an interval he couldn't quite locate, was the other thing.

Flat. That was the only word that fit. Not dark — darkness had texture, had intention. Not corrupted. Just flat, the way a room sounds when all the soft furnishings have been stripped out and every sound bounces wrong off bare walls.

He'd been blaming it on Abeloth. The Order had been blaming a lot of things on Abeloth, and the Council's position was that the Force was still settling, that fluctuations and disturbances were expected in the aftermath of that scale of disruption, that time and meditation were the appropriate response. Ben had read that briefing three times. He'd found it less convincing each time.

He walked to the center of the salle and raised his empty hand to a guard position.

---

What followed wasn't a form, exactly. More like a diagnostic. He moved through the opening sequence of Shien, slow, no blade, letting the Force current carry his weight from one stance to the next the way it always had — the way it had since he was fourteen and Jacen had drilled these patterns into him until they were reflex. His body remembered. Transition, pivot, extension, the whole sequence smooth and unbroken, muscle memory intact.

He stopped. Started again, this time reaching into the current for the timing layer — the split-second anticipation that told a fighter where the next strike was coming from before it arrived. The layer that had dropped on him three times in five days.

It was there. And then it wasn't.

Not gone. Not blocked. More like the signal had passed through a gap in something and come out the other side slightly wrong — present but unreliable, the way a comm signal sounds when you're on the edge of a relay's range. He reached again and got a clear response, then reached a third time and got the flatness.

Ben lowered his arm.

The ventilation hummed. Sweat had dried on his palms. He pressed them flat against his thighs and stood still, and the wrongness pulsed once through the current — not painful, not dramatic, just that quarter-tone drop that made his teeth want to press together.

He'd felt something like this twice before.

The first time, he'd been twelve. Jacen had been teaching him to reach through the Force to sense the emotional temperature of a room, and Ben had brushed the edge of something Jacen had been carrying that he hadn't intended to share — a cold, structural absence where something should have been. Ben hadn't had words for it then. He'd told himself it was nothing.

The second time had been Abeloth, in the Maw, in the moments before everything went catastrophically wrong. Not dark. Not evil in any way he'd been trained to recognize. Just a place where the Force's usual feedback — the moral weight, the sense of consequence — had been removed, like load-bearing walls pulled out of a building that was still somehow standing.

This was smaller than that. Much smaller. But the quality was the same.

Ben pressed his palms flat against his thighs and held the comparison in his head and didn't like what it told him. Post-crisis settling didn't feel like this. Lingering trauma didn't feel like this. Trauma had texture — echoes, emotional residue, the specific grief-frequencies of places where people had died badly. What he was touching was the opposite of that. It wasn't the presence of something terrible. It was an absence of something that should be there.

The Council would tell him he was pattern-matching. That after Jacen and Abeloth, his nervous system was primed to read catastrophe into noise. He could hear Master Cilghal's careful voice constructing the argument, and the argument was reasonable, and he almost believed it.

Almost.

He picked up his training saber and turned it over in his hands without activating it. The Order had spent three years rebuilding after the Abeloth crisis — restructuring the Academy, repairing the Temple, drafting new protocols for Force-entity contact. Everyone was tired. Everyone was still healing. Bringing this to the Council meant watching twelve Masters listen politely and refer him to the post-trauma counseling track, and he'd rather run Shien forms for another two hours than sit through that conversation.

He could investigate quietly. Keep logs. Map the intervals, the locations, the intensity. Build a case before he brought it to anyone. That was rational. That was the sensible approach.

Ben set the training saber back on the bench.

The sensible approach had been working great so far, he noted, given that he was standing alone in an empty salle at the twenty-second hour with sweat-dried palms and three unexplained combat failures in a week. He had logs. He had a theory. He had no one to test either against because he hadn't told anyone, and he hadn't told anyone because explaining it required admitting that something was wrong with the Force and he was the only one who seemed to notice, and that was either very important or very concerning, and he wasn't sure which possibility unsettled him more.

The salle door opened.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and the particular quality of stillness he got when he already knew what he was about to say. He looked at Ben the way he'd been looking at him lately — not worried, exactly. Attentive. Like a man watching a frequency he expected to change.

He was holding the datapad the way he held things when there was somewhere he needed someone to go.
