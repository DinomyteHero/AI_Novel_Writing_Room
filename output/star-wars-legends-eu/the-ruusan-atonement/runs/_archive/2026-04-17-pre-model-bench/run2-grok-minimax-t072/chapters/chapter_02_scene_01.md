The *Steady Returns* flew as if assembled from three different ships that had never been properly introduced. Its port stabilizer lagged a half-second behind the starboard. To compensate, the nav-computer overcorrected, forcing Desh to walk the yoke two degrees left to hold their bearing. He did it without looking down. He moved with the muscle memory of a man navigating a familiar staircase in the dark.

Ben watched him work. He said nothing.

"Checklist item seven," Desh said. A cup of caf sat balanced on the console housing, secured by a strip of adhesive that had been there long enough to leave a permanent shadow on the plasteel. The liquid had gone cold an hour ago. Desh drank it anyway.

"Hyperdrive motivator output."

Ben checked the gauge. "Nominal. Barely."

"Barely is fine."

"The Temple's maintenance crew would have opinions about barely."

"The Temple's maintenance crew can file them with my other complaints." Desh tapped the gauge with one finger—a habit Ben had catalogued over years of shared cockpits. Tap it, watch the needle, decide whether to care. The needle remained stationary. "Item eight."

"Inertial compensator."

"Running."

"Navigation beacon."

"Confirmed." Ben made the notation. The checklist was a relic—actual flimsi clipped to a board, filled with Desh's compressed shorthand. Every ship Desh flew carried one. He didn't trust data entry to remain legible in an emergency. Ben had stopped finding the habit eccentric after the third time it saved their lives.

Outside the viewport, hyperspace ran blue and endless. The tunnel walls rippled with a light that had no equivalent in realspace—neither luminescent nor reflective, but a shifting middle ground. Ben had made hundreds of transits. He still looked.

The recycled air tasted of carbon scrubbers and burnt grain. That specific scent—Desh’s caf—always brought back memories of early mornings, long waits, and the patience required to stay alert when there was nothing to do.

"You're going to tell me the starboard sensor array has been throwing garbage since Terminus," Desh said. It wasn't a question.

"I was waiting for you to notice."

"I noticed at Terminus." Desh picked up the cup. "I was waiting for you to speak up."

Ben pulled the sensor log. He scrolled through the data. The inconsistencies didn't match any mechanical failure he recognized; vacuum density readings spiked and dropped without pattern, while mass-shadow calculations contradicted each other within the same transit window. "What's your read?"

"Bad motivator in the secondary array. Or the calibration slipped when we loaded cargo at Terminus." Desh set the caf down. "Or both."

"Or both," Ben agreed.

He kept his eyes on the screen. The inconsistencies weren't random. That was the detail he withheld. Random instrument error usually scattered across readings, but these clustered, tightening toward their current bearing. The nav-computer wasn't failing. It was struggling to describe something for which it had no programming.

He closed the log.

"Item nine," Desh said. "Life support."

"Green across the board."

"That's the one time I actually want barely."

Ben almost smiled. "Noted."

The hyperdrive hummed beneath the deck plates. Desh made the two-degree correction again, as automatic as breathing. The caf sat cooling in its adhesive cradle. Ben had shared cockpits with enough pilots to know that this silence—comfortable, undemanding—was rare. Most people felt a need to fill the void. Desh used it.

It was the one thing Ben had forgotten in the three years since they'd last worked together. Not the competence. The quiet.

In the Force, Desh felt like a warm absence. He wasn't a void; voids had sharp edges defined by what they lacked. This was softer. The Force moved through the cabin and around Desh the way water flowed around a smooth stone. No disruption. No demand. No bleed of emotion for Ben to filter out. He was simply a person-shaped space the Force didn't bother to claim.

Ben hadn't understood why that was restful when he was younger. He understood now.

"Remind me why we're surveying Darpa Sector," Desh said. His tone was carefully calibrated—not pressing, just opening a door.

"Anomalous readings. Luke flagged it three weeks ago."

"Luke flagged it and sent you."

"Luke flagged it and sent me."

Desh was quiet for a moment. "And you called me instead of—"

"Someone who would want to discuss the philosophical implications of anomalous readings, yes."

The hyperdrive hummed. Desh made his correction.

"Fair enough," he said.

That was the limit. They had reached the edge of the years they didn't discuss: the orders followed, the faces attached to mission files that Ben still saw when he closed his eyes. Desh had been good at his job. That was the problem. He had been exceptional, and neither of them had examined the cost until it was too late to do so cleanly.

Ben reached for the sensor readout again, needing to occupy his hands. Desh tapped the sensor array housing instead.

"Item ten," he said. "Secondary nav-beacon."

"Confirmed."

They moved on.

Forty minutes from reversion, the sensation became impossible to ignore.

It had been building since Terminus—a structural wrongness Ben had carried for three months, a steady pressure against his perception. He had managed the discomfort by staying busy. The checklist. Desh’s complaints about the motivator housing he’d need to replace at their next port, provided they could find a dealer who wasn't gouging prices.

Now the wrongness was louder. It had a direction. It lay directly ahead.

He kept his expression neutral. He had practiced that mask since he was fifteen.

Desh tapped the sensor gauge again, frowning. "That's new."

"What is?"

"Mass-shadow calculation just contradicted itself. We're reading two different vacuum densities in the same coordinate window." He scrolled through the output. "That's not a calibration slip."

Ben studied the readout. The numbers were wrong in the specific way the Force felt wrong—not broken, but thin. It was as if something had been sucked out of the data, leaving the sensors confused by the vacuum.

"Motivator?" he asked.

"Maybe." Desh didn't sound convinced. He struck the housing a second time, harder. The needle didn't budge. "Or the sector is doing something to the sensors."

"The sector," Ben repeated.

"Don't give me that look."

"I'm not giving you a look."

"You're giving me the look where you know something you're not saying." Desh kept his eyes on the instruments, but his shoulders went still. It was the stillness of a man who had learned to detect a threat in the change of a room's atmosphere. "You've been giving me that look since Terminus."

The nav-computer chimed. Reversion in four minutes.

Ben gripped the copilot's armrest. The wrongness spiked—not as a sound or a pain, but as a sudden drop in pressure, the kind that preceded a structural collapse. His breath came shallow. Through the viewport, the hyperspace tunnel remained blue and indifferent.

"The instruments aren't wrong," Ben said.

Desh looked at him for one second. Then he turned back to the controls and adjusted the yoke. His hand was steady.

"Great," he said. "Good to know."

The countdown ticked toward zero. Outside, the blue light waited. Ben’s knuckles turned white against the padding.
