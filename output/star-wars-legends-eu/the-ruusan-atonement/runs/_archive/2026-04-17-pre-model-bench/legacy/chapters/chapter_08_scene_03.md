The coolant valve had complained since the third node. Ben had noted the sound then—a flat, rhythmic *clack-hiss* buried under the ship's normal register—and filed it away. Standing at the edge of the engineering crawlspace, he realized Desh had filed nothing away.

The pilot lay on his back, half-submerged in a conduit access panel. A diagnostic pad balanced on his chest; a work light clamped to a pipe above cast harsh, white circles over his grease-stained coveralls. Only his boots were fully visible. They remained perfectly still, the way boots did when the wearer was deep in thought.

Kael was folded into the corner where the crawlspace narrowed toward the port thruster housing. He had pulled his knees to his chest to fit. He watched Desh with the fixed attention of a man trying to identify a threat.

Ben stayed in the shadows of the upper gantry. The air down here tasted of hydraulic fluid and heat bleed from the secondary exchanger—a close, functional scent. He had come to check the valve. He stayed because of Kael's expression.

"You talk to it," Kael said.

Desh's voice echoed from inside the conduit. "Doesn't everyone?"

"No."

"Hm." A clank followed, then a hiss of released pressure that quickly subsided. "There you are. Held on long enough, didn't you?"

The *clack-hiss* stopped.

Kael watched the boots. "You're not Jedi."

"Correct."

"But you serve one."

Desh slid out from the conduit far enough to look at Kael. A smudge of carbon crossed his cheekbone. He checked the diagnostic pad, reviewed the scrolling data, and set it aside. "I fly the ship. Skywalker pays for the fuel and the docking fees. If that's service, then yeah."

"You're not compelled."

Desh paused. "By what?"

Kael’s posture stiffened. "In the Brotherhood, those without the Force who traveled with those who had it were held in place. Their loyalty was—maintained."

"Maintained." Desh repeated the word as if testing its weight. He sat up, crossing his legs in the narrow space, and reached for a rag to wipe his hands. "No. Nobody's maintaining me. Ben asked if I wanted the contract. I said yes. That's the whole story."

"Why?"

"Because the ship was going somewhere interesting and the money was fair." Desh folded the rag with deliberate precision. "Why did you think?"

Kael’s fingers flexed against his knees. Ben watched the internal grinding of a man whose categories were insufficient for the data. In the Brotherhood, you were placed. In Gavran's operation, you were placed. A thousand years of being a structural component did not prepare a man for the concept of choice.

"He has the Force," Kael said, his tone bordering on accusatory. "He has authority. You don't."

"Sure." Desh reached past him to open a secondary access panel. "Hand me that diagnostic pad. The small one, on your left."

Kael’s hand extended. His fingers released the pad into Desh's grip without hesitation or calculation. It wasn't a command obeyed or a manipulation navigated. It was a request from someone who needed a tool and assumed Kael was capable of providing it.

Ben caught the moment—the way Kael's body went still afterward. His eyes tracked the pad into Desh's hands and lingered there.

Desh plugged the pad into the secondary port. "Coolant mix is off by about four percent. Ship wasn't built for this many passengers. She's compensating." He patted the bulkhead. "She'll be fine."

Kael looked at his own hand. His fingers curled into a fist, then released.

"He doesn't control you," Kael said. It wasn't a question. It was a plea.

"I just told you that."

"I heard. I'm—" Kael stopped, his throat working. "The people I traveled with. The ones who had the Force. They didn't ask. They directed. You followed or you were made to follow. The distinction was—" He turned the word over like something unfamiliar. "Academic."

Desh unplugged the pad. He looked at Kael with the level attention he gave to anything requiring an accurate assessment. "How long were you in that chamber?"

"A thousand years."

"Right." Desh set the pad down. "So you knew a specific group of people who operated a specific way, and then you were sealed in a box. Now you're out and you're running the same model on new data." He wasn't harsh. He spoke the way he had about the coolant mix—as a reading, not a verdict. "Might want to update the model."

Kael’s breath sharpened. His fingers dug into his knees, then slowly relaxed. He looked away toward the darkened corner of the crawlspace. His shoulders dropped.

Ben leaned against the doorframe, his presence still unacknowledged. This was what Gavran's people couldn't do. They could seal a thousand Force-sensitives into a containment system and call it necessity, but somewhere in that math they stopped seeing people who needed someone to just ask. Not command. Not conscript. Ask.

He realized he was guilty of a version of the same thing. He had brought these three out of their chambers and handed them a mission briefing and a ship bunk. He had told himself it was for their benefit because they needed direction. He had not asked Kael what he wanted. He had not asked Sera. He had asked Desh, once, at the beginning, before any of this had weight.

The thought sat in him like a stone in still water.

"You don't feel it," Kael said. "The wrongness."

Desh was already reaching back into the conduit. "Grab that hydrospanner. The twelve-millimeter. My hands are full."

Kael looked at the tool rack and found the spanner. The metal was warm from the engine room. He picked it up and held it for a second longer than necessary, registering the weight and the solidity of it. He placed it in Desh's extended hand.

"No," Desh said, his voice muffled by the machinery. "I feel the ship shaking and four Force-users arguing about why."

The corner of Kael's mouth twitched upward. For a moment it stayed there—a genuine, unperformed thing. Then it settled back into stillness. But the tension had left his shoulders. He folded himself back into his corner, and the way he held his body had changed. He looked less like a man waiting for a blow and more like one who had set down a heavy burden.

Ben pushed off the doorframe and walked back toward the cockpit, keeping his footsteps light.

They need someone to just ask. He carried the thought with him. He did not yet know what to do with it, but he understood that not knowing was the beginning of something.

The ship pressed on through the dark.
