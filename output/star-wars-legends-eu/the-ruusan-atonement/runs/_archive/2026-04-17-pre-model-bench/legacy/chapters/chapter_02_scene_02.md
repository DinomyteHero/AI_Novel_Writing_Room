The 12 ABY survey entry was forty-three words long. *Unnamed Outer Rim body, designation WR-7741. Uninhabited. Geological composition: iron-rich sandstone. Atmosphere: thin, breathable. No structures or artifacts of note. Survey complete, no follow-up recommended.* Ben had read it three times during the descent. He read it a fourth time now, standing before a contradiction.

The cliff rose fifteen meters from the valley floor. Wind-deposited sediment buried the lower half, but the upper third remained exposed, its surface crowded with carvings.

They weren't inscribed. They were gouged. The channels ran deep, cut by a tool that had displaced stone rather than merely scratching it.

"Erosion rates," Desh said. He crouched near the base, tracing a thumb along the sediment line. "To bury this much of the stone, you're looking at several centuries. Minimum. Maybe more, depending on the wind." He stood, brushing grit from his glove. "This was here long before anyone thought to look."

Ben lowered his datapad. The survey entry sat in the corner of the screen, patient and incorrect.

"The 12 ABY team was thorough," Ben said. "Orbital and surface scans. They would have seen this."

"Then they didn't see it."

The words hung between them. Desh had worked intelligence long enough to understand the implication: not incompetence, but erasure. Ben watched his companion, waiting for the calculation to finish.

"Or it wasn't here," Ben added, testing the alternative.

Desh looked at him. The silence lasted exactly one second before he turned back to the cliff. He didn't argue. That was worse.

The wind moved through the valley in low, grinding waves, pushing grit against Ben's visor. He had kept his helmet on; the atmosphere was breathable but thin enough to make exertion uncomfortable. Desh wore full kit—field jacket, sealed gloves, a scanner clipped to his belt. They both stood still, staring at the carvings with the focused intensity of men trying to hear a faint sound.

Ben activated the LIDAR array and swept it across the stone. The results flickered back in seconds. The channels averaged eleven centimeters deep. Whatever tool had fashioned them was precise; the variance across the entire face was less than two millimeters.

He stepped closer.

The carvings weren't language. He had cataloged enough ruins to recognize the difference. Language possessed rhythm and recursion—characters appearing in clusters, then alone, then in clusters again, building the statistical fingerprint of grammar. These lacked that pulse. They repeated, but through a different logic. They interlocked. Each geometric shape connected to the next through shared angles and vertices, as if every element was load-bearing.

A schematic.

He tilted his head, following one line with his eye. It branched, intersected two others, and continued. He followed a second line. It circled back to the first. A third connected to the second at the same angle the first used. The entire face functioned as a single structure drawn from every direction at once—the same geometry expressed in a hundred interlocking iterations.

He unclenched his teeth.

"No Sith influence," he muttered. Sith construction had a recognizable grammar: weight and dominance, angles designed to diminish the observer. This was different. It wasn't High Republic either; the sweeping arches and organic curves of that era were absent. He ran through the architectural traditions memorized from the Ossus archives. Forty-seven pre-Republic styles. He discarded them one by one. Nothing matched.

His hands remained steady as he worked. That mattered. Steady hands meant control, and control meant he could think.

"Heat's getting to me," Desh said. His voice remained level. "You want water before you get closer?"

"I'm fine."

"I didn't ask if you were fine. I asked if you wanted water."

Ben accepted the canteen without turning. He drank and handed it back. The water was warm, tasting of recycled filters. He focused on that—the specific, manageable problem of thirst—while the wrongness continued its work.

The sensation had been building since they touched down. It wasn't the spike he’d felt on reversion; that had been blunt and directional. This was subtler. The air near the cliff had a quality he couldn't locate in any single sense. It wasn't a sound or a temperature. It was the way a room's acoustics change when a wall is removed—the same space, but the resonance shifted. He felt it in the bones of his face. In his back teeth. In the hollow of his sternum where the Force usually moved with the easy rhythm of breathing.

Here, the rhythm stuttered.

He stopped two meters from the stone and studied the central section. The lines converged there, forming a focal point where the geometry was densest. The recurring motif appeared at least thirty times across the face, but here it was largest. A branching structure that folded back on itself, each vein containing the same pattern as the whole.

His glove was in his right hand before he registered removing it.

*Caution.* His father's voice arrived as it always did when Ben was about to do something inadvisable. Not a prohibition. Just an acknowledgment.

Ben pressed his bare palm to the stone.

The ache in his teeth stopped.

The Force didn't dim. It didn't withdraw. It was simply gone, the way light vanishes when a switch is thrown. The flow he had lived with his entire life—the constant warmth of the living world—fell silent. The stone under his hand was cold, real, and nothing else. Desh's presence, usually a comfortable shape behind him, vanished. The valley disappeared. The wrongness he had been tracking for months evaporated, because the wrongness required a medium to exist within, and there was nothing.

Raw power remained. He could feel that—the ability to act, to push, to burn. The instrument without the music. The hand without the nerve endings.

He pulled back.

The Force returned like a wave filling a depression in sand—fast, complete, violent in its normalcy. He stood with his palm against his chest and breathed through the rush. Once. Twice. His heart was still accelerating.

"Ben."

Desh was behind him. Closer now. His voice had changed. Not alarmed—Desh didn't alarm—but alert in the way a soldier becomes when the environment reveals a predator.

"I'm fine," Ben said.

"You stopped moving."

"I know." He pulled his glove back on. His hands were steady. He was grateful for that. "I'm fine."

He activated the holorecorder and worked methodically, the way his mother had taught him to work when a situation fought his ability to focus. Frame, capture, advance. He documented the face in sections, then the central motif, then the sediment line, then the tool marks on the channel walls. The LIDAR data compiled. He captured everything.

The comlink opened on the second try. His father picked up on the third chime.

"Ben."

"I have something for you," Ben said, keeping his voice even. "Sending it now. Survey world WR-7741. The 12 ABY entry lists the cliff face as blank. It isn't. Carvings across the exposed section—pre-Republic based on the erosion data. LIDAR puts the channels at eleven centimeters average depth. No match to any architectural tradition I can place. Geometric, not linguistic. Structural rather than decorative."

A pause. He could hear his father moving in his office on Coruscant.

"Any Force signature?" Luke asked.

Ben looked at the central glyph. He remembered the void, the way the lattice of the Force had simply ceased to exist under his touch. He thought of the wrongness that had led him here, and the way it had vanished into a hollow silence.

He thought of the lie he was about to tell.

"Nothing definitive," Ben said. "I'll keep documenting. Sending the full package now."

The connection closed.

He stood with the comlink in his hand for a moment before clipping it to his belt. Desh said nothing, though he watched Ben with unblinking patience. The wind pushed grit against Ben's visor in long, grinding waves, and the glyph sat in its stone, waiting for the right question.
