The fork hit the tray with the finality of a gavel.

Ben looked up. Sera’s hand remained clamped on the handle, her knuckles white, her meal untouched. Across the narrow table, Torin sat with his own utensil suspended. His expression shifted from surprise toward a guarded neutrality.

"—elegant, is all I'm saying." Torin set the fork down. "Not the outcome. The underlying concept. You have to admit the engineering was—"

"I do not have to admit anything." Sera's voice was level, like the surface of a deep well. "And you should not be able to use that word with that tone."

The *Steady Returns* hummed. Air recyclers kicked over at irregular intervals, venting a scent of rehydrated protein and the metallic tang Ben had stopped noticing days ago. At the far end of the table, Kael ate with systematic efficiency, his gaze fixed on nothing. Desh leaned against the galley hatch, cradling a cup of cold caf, his weight shifted like a man who had spent a career reading rooms for exits.

"The *technique*," Torin insisted. He let the word hang. "That is all it is. A technique. The lattice is a structure—we know this now. Structures can be modified. The error at Ruusan was not the modification itself. It was the failure to model the downstream variables."

"The downstream variables." Sera's diction sharpened. "You mean the wound in the Force. The breach that has been festering for a thousand years. The crisis we are currently flying toward."

"Yes." Torin's warmth remained, but it had thinned like morning light behind a cloud bank—present, but no longer providing heat. "I mean exactly that. A miscalculation. Not a sin."

Sera set her fork down again. This time, the silence of the gesture carried more weight than the previous clatter. "The lattice is not a containment system. It is the will of the Force made structural. It is how the Force teaches us what we are and what we are not. Removing it is not engineering." She paused, her next words heavy. "It is a lobotomy performed on the living universe."

Torin tilted his head. "That is poetry, Varik. I am asking about mechanics."

"I know what you are asking. I am telling you that the question itself is the problem."

Ben opened his mouth. He had a line ready—something about the Force binding all living things, the tradition his father had rebuilt, the lessons that had anchored twenty years of his life. He had recited some version of it a hundred times in training halls and diplomatic briefings.

"The Force is what—"

He stopped.

The words were there, familiar and polished. But he was sitting in a ship threading through a region where the Force guttered like a candle in a draft. His reach found nothing three times in four. The sentence in his throat was a description of a sensation he could no longer find. He was quoting a map that didn't match the ground.

His hands tightened on the table's edge.

No one noticed. Sera and Torin remained locked in the focused intensity of those who had waited a millennium for this argument.

"Gavran believed the same thing you believe," Torin said. "Sacred architecture. The will of the Force. He built the seal because he could not accept that the wound was a variable. And the seal is failing."

Something flickered across Sera's face—a brief tightening around the eyes. She knew something about Gavran she wasn't going to share. "The seal is failing because of what was done to create it. Not because the principle was wrong."

"Evidence would be useful."

"I am the evidence." Her voice didn't rise; it dropped. "I was inside the seal for a thousand years, Hal. Do not explain the mechanism to me."

The recycler cut out. The sudden silence had mass.

Desh rotated his caf cup, a slow, deliberate movement. "You know what I see?"

Neither Sera nor Torin answered. The question wasn't really for them.

"I see two people whose convictions made them useful for a specific operation a thousand years ago." Desh’s tone was clinical. "Varik, your certainty about the sacred order made you a reliable anchor. Someone who wouldn't question the assignment." He looked at Torin. "Hal, your pragmatism made you willing to use the technique when others refused. Someone who could be trusted to act." He took a sip of caf. "Both of those were useful qualities. Both of them got you sealed into a bunker for a millennium. I'm just noting the pattern."

Sera’s expression hardened. Torin’s hand stilled.

*Desh sees it,* Ben thought. *He doesn't have the Force and he sees it clearest.*

"The pattern," Torin said carefully, "is not the point."

"Sure," Desh replied, and went back to his drink.

The recycler kicked back on, filling the void.

Ben looked at his plate. His food had gone cold. He thought about his father’s lessons—the ones that came sideways, in the middle of other conversations. *The Force is not a tool, Ben. It's a relationship.* He had understood that. He had built his life on it.

Now, reaching for that relationship produced only static.

Sera believed the lattice was sacred. Torin believed it was engineering. His father had taught him something in between—something that had felt true because the Force had always been there to confirm it. But the Force wasn't confirming anything now.

He looked at his hands. Ordinary hands.

"We should table this," he said.

Torin glanced at him. "Skywalker—"

"We're less than an hour from the next node." Ben kept his voice flat. "Opinions about the nature of the Force are not going to change in forty minutes, and I need everyone functional when we arrive." He looked at Sera. "Both of you."

Sera held his gaze for a moment before picking up her fork. She began to eat with mechanical precision.

Torin leaned back, the warmth returning to his face in a pale imitation of its former self. He picked up his own utensil. "As you say."

The ship pressed in around them.

Kael set his empty tray aside. The movement felt loud in the cramped space. He looked at each of them in turn with the unhurried attention of a man deciding whether a conversation mattered.

"You're all arguing about whose cage has nicer bars," he said.

He stood and carried his tray to the galley.

No one answered. There wasn't an answer that worked. Ben sat with the hum of the ship and the hollow place in his chest where the Force used to resonate. He thought about bars, and the map he'd been reading his whole life, and the ground that wasn't on it.

He thought about the report he had sent to his father—the one that omitted the name Veranthos and the truth of the lattice. He wasn't just reading a map; he was redrawing it to hide the holes. And everyone at the table was doing the same thing, just drawing different maps.
