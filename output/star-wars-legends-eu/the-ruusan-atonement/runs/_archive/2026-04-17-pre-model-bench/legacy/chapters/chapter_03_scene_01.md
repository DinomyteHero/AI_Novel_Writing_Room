The structure did not look like a ruin.

That was the first warning. Ben stood forty meters from the blast doors and watched the sun crawl across the facade. No erosion lines marred the surface. No stress fractures or root systems threaded through the cracks of a millennium. The dark basalt, cut in massive rectangular blocks, fit together without mortar. The corners remained sharp. Whatever the Jedi Lords had built here, they had intended it to outlast their own names.

The wrongness pressed against him like a palm flat against glass. It wasn't the dark side, nor was it Abeloth’s chaos, which had screamed like static across every frequency. This was structural—a place where the Force’s lattice had been bent around an object the way a river diverts around bedrock. He could feel the facility’s outline through that displacement. The Force did not flow here; it detoured.

"Chrono's running fast," Desh said. He wasn't looking at the facility. He was watching the treeline.

Ben glanced back. "How fast?"

"Two seconds since we left the ship. Forty minutes ago." Desh's voice carried its usual flatness, but his weight had shifted. He wasn't standing like a surveyor; he was standing like a man who had already identified three pieces of cover and was deciding between them. "That's not drift."

"No," Ben agreed. "It's not."

The air thickened as he turned back to the entrance. His skin prickled with the body's recognition of a fundamental anomaly. He had felt similar sensations in places where the Force had been wounded or weaponized, but this was older. The facade offered no visible interface—no panel, no keypad, no pressure sensor. Just stone and a faint vibration that Ben felt in his sternum. A low, cycling hum. Whatever generated power in there had been churning for a long time.

He walked toward the doors. The atmosphere compressed as he closed the distance, dense as deep water. His Jedi signature extended ahead of him on instinct, a hand reaching into a dark room.

The doors read him.

It wasn't a passive scan. Something in the facility's infrastructure reached back along his Force presence and assessed him. Methodical. Cold. It catalogued his lineage the way a military tribunal reviews credentials and finds them insufficient on a technicality. He was Jedi; that registered. But the facility's logic was calibrated for something older—something that wore the Force with more weight and less restraint. 

He was close enough. The doors parted like a reluctant sentry waving through a civilian with the right badge in the wrong style.

The heavy slabs separated with a hiss of escaping pressure. The air that surged out was stale, recycled, and moving.

"There it is," Desh muttered, appearing at Ben's shoulder. He had closed the distance in silence, his eyes already dissecting the interior. "Power plant or generator?"

"Something else." Ben stepped through, his hand drifting toward his lightsaber before he checked the impulse. "Don't touch anything."

The corridor sloped downward at a shallow angle. The ceiling was high, built for armored figures carrying heavy equipment. The walls held iconography Ben recognized from the Ossus archives—images that occupied exactly two pages in the Republic’s military history before the Ruusan Reformations rewrote the record. These were Jedi in full combat plate, not the robes of his childhood. Symbols of the Army of Light were rendered in functional bas-relief, the kind of decoration that served as identification, not art. Every line was load-bearing. Purposeful. Designed by people who understood that the next building project might be a fortress.

His father had never mentioned this place. Ben wasn't sure Luke even knew it existed.

Amber lights along the floor glowed dim but steady. Standby power. Something had maintained this facility in low-function mode for a thousand years, waiting for a signature that matched the right parameters. He had matched them, barely, and now he was inside a tomb that had been dreaming for a millennium.

Desh moved with him, though his focus remained on tactics rather than archaeology. He tracked sightlines and noted exit points, translating the architecture into a battlefield. When Ben glanced back, Desh’s jaw was set. The practical man had just upgraded the threat level.

The corridor opened into a central chamber where the ceiling vaulted twenty meters overhead. In the center stood a terminal cluster—six consoles arranged in a hexagonal configuration, each connected by conduit to a central column. It hummed at a frequency that made Ben’s back teeth ache. 

A containment cell. Not a generator. The power source was a trapped Force reservoir, draining for a thousand years and still active.

He approached the nearest console. Dust sat half a centimeter thick on the surface; his finger left a dark trail when he touched the edge. On the facing panel, inlaid in the basalt, was a plaque. The lettering was archaic Basic—formal and military. 

*This installation constructed by order of Lord Gavran, Army of Light, in the 1001st year of the Old Republic, for the purpose of perimeter monitoring and integrity maintenance. Authorized personnel only.*

Gavran. Ben knew the name from the lists of battles he’d studied but never lived—Ruusan, Duro, Hoth. Gavran had been a general, not a philosopher. He was a man who had fought the Brotherhood of Darkness and ended something at Ruusan, though the histories were vague on the specifics. The Jedi had changed after that. The Reformations had restructured the Order entirely, disbanding the Army of Light and severing ties to the Republic military.

He had always assumed the Reformations were philosophical. Looking at the brutal functionality of this room, he began to doubt it.

The console nearest him flickered.

A different frequency tried to assert itself beneath the amber glow. Ben stepped back. Desh moved to his left, positioning himself with a clear line to the corridor. His hand rested on his blaster—not drawn, but ready. The transition from observer to operative took less than a breath.

The flicker spread across all six consoles. The hum from the central column shifted pitch, climbing into a labored whine, like a system straining against a load it had carried too long. The amber standby lighting died.

Then the red came on.

It strobed twice, found a rhythm, and held. The chamber transformed into a landscape of harsh crimson and deep shadow. The terminal cluster fully activated, casting low-resolution holographic interfaces into the air—archaic blue displays with the grainy quality of ancient historical footage.

Columns of data scrolled by, most of it in a technical notation Ben couldn't decipher before the audio engaged.

The sound bled from the walls. It started as sharp static that bypassed the ears and vibrated the base of the skull. Ben’s hand went to his lightsaber, though there was nothing to strike. The static smoothed out, resolving into a voice.

Synthesized. Repeating. The cadence belonged to a system that had been practicing this announcement for nine centuries. 

The holographic text stabilized above the central column. Archaic Basic. Formal military notation. Unmistakable.

Two words.

*SEAL FAILING.*

Ben looked at Desh. Desh looked at Ben. The red light strobed between them, and the synthesized voice kept cycling through its message in the empty room, patient and terrible, reporting a breach to an army that no longer existed.
