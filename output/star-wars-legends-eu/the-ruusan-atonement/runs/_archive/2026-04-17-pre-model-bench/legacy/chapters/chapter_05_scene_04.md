The *Steady Returns* was built for two.

Ben knew this in the abstract way he knew most things about the ship—fuel consumption, jump range, the specific rattle in the port stabilizer that Desh had been promising to fix for months. He had never felt the cramped reality of it like this. Five people, three of them strangers who had died before his grandfather was born, breathed the same recycled air while the fortress shrank below the viewports like a tomb that had forgotten to stay closed.

He ran preflight checks to give his hands something to do.

"Hull integrity reads clean," Desh said from the co-pilot seat. His voice carried the deliberate flatness of a man who had decided not to think beyond the next task. "Repulsors are nominal. We've got fuel for two jumps, maybe three if we're conservative."

"We won't be." Ben's fingers moved across the console with practiced precision. Muscle memory. Control. "Sera—the map. Walk me through it again."

The hold was silent. Sera Varik stood with her arms crossed, her posture less a sign of discipline than a refusal to collapse. Torin Hal sat with his elbows on his knees, head bowed, eyes open. Kael Drenn had wedged himself into the far corner, watching the back of Ben's head with the focus of a man cataloging exit routes.

Sera pulled the data chip from her belt—the one she had salvaged from the monitoring station after discovering Veraine's purge. The holoprojector threw a pale blue line across the hold. The Corridor. Twelve points of light appeared, nine of them a dead, hollow gray.

"The seal isn't a wall," she said. "Each anchor is a load-bearing point in a distributed network. Remove enough of them and the remaining points can't compensate. Veraine knows the failure threshold. She doesn't have to destroy the seal—she only has to accelerate the decay."

"Pruning," Desh muttered.

"Yes."

Ben traced the line. The first active anchor sat deep in the Outer Rim, just inside the Meridian sector. Three weeks by hyperlane. Maybe two if they pushed the *Steady Returns* harder than the engines liked.

"How long does the first node hold without intervention?"

"If Veraine reaches it before us—" Sera paused. "Then we're already adjusting our estimate. She has a six-week head start, Ben. She spent six days in that archive learning your galaxy before she left."

Ben stared at the survivors. They were displaced, stripped of everything they had known, carrying a millennium of institutional knowledge about a crisis the modern galaxy had spent a thousand years pretending didn't exist. They had no credits. No contacts. No way to explain themselves to a customs checkpoint without triggering a quarantine order and a grueling interrogation by the Jedi Council.

He had been rehearsing that conversation since the archive.

*Master Skywalker, we found three survivors from the New Sith Wars in a hidden stasis facility. They were being used as structural anchors for a containment seal around a Force wound the Ruusan Reformations were designed to cover up.*

The words formed in his mind like a trap. He could see the Council chamber. Sera Varik handed off to research teams. Torin interviewed for months about historical military doctrine. Kael assessed for threat potential.

Veraine would reach the first anchor while the paperwork cleared.

Ben's hands moved across the comms console. He didn't remember deciding to do it.

*Outer Rim survey, potential structural anomaly confirmed. Pursuing secondary site for full assessment. Engine calibration issue complicating timeline—delay of three to four weeks anticipated. Will report when in range.*

He read it twice. His fingers hovered over the send key. The words sat there, incomplete, a gap between what he told Luke and what Luke needed to know. He sent it before he could hesitate.

The fortress remained visible below, dark and massive. It felt wrong in the Force, the way a missing tooth feels wrong in a mouth. That sensation didn't disappear when he looked away. It had spread. It was everywhere now, concentrated there like pressure at a fracture point.

He reached for the repulsors. Torin stood and moved forward.

"Question," Torin said. He settled into the space between the seats, one hand on the overhead support. He looked at Ben with an expression that wasn't hostile, but wasn't warm. It was an assessment, slower and more patient than Sera’s. "Your Jedi. Do they still believe the Force has a side? That the boundary is a fixed thing—light here, dark there, a wall between them?"

Ben kept his hands on the controls. "We believe the Force has a will. That the dark side is a corruption, not an alternative."

"A corruption." Torin repeated the word as if testing a shaky translation. "Not a natural property."

"No."

Torin smiled. It was a small, weary expression that reached his eyes. That was the unsettling part—it wasn't contempt. It looked like sorrow worn smooth by time. He looked at Ben as if watching a child walk into a room Torin had already learned to fear.

"Gavran thought so too," he said. "Before."

He returned to his seat without waiting for a response.

Ben’s pulse spiked. He sat still for three seconds, recognizing the shape of the trap, the way Torin's words had slotted into a space he hadn't known was empty. Gavran. The lattice inversion. The technique that stripped moral intuition to ensure victory. The choice that had led to the end.

He filed it away. *Survivor cynicism, follow up later.* The thought felt like swallowing glass.

He engaged the repulsors.

The fortress fell away. The plateau shrank. The wrongness receded in his Force-sense—not disappearing, but diffusing into the background hum of something vast and structural.

He set the coordinates for the Meridian sector. Desh handled the jump calculation in silence.

"She left a trail," Desh said. "Six weeks, no resources, a galaxy she doesn't recognize. She still hit the first anchor ahead of us."

"She planned for this," Ben said. "Probably for centuries."

Desh nodded. He didn't ask the question hanging between them: *What makes you think you can catch her?* He didn't have to. Ben could hear it in the quiet.

The blue tunnel of hyperspace swallowed the ship. It ran the length of the forward viewport, a narrow, bright corridor with everything else compressed to nothing on either side.

She had left the trail because she wanted them to follow.

Ben knew the ship was too small. He knew the burden was too large. He knew the lie he had sent to Luke would grow heavier every day they remained silent.

He would deal with it when they arrived.

That was what Skywalkers did.
