The bird called from somewhere in the canopy. Its cry carried through two hundred meters of wet air and arrived flat—muffled, like cloth pressed against a speaker. Ben stood at the base of the ruin and listened. Again the sound reached him wrong.

Not quieter. Just less.

The jungle performed its expected functions. Moisture beaded on his sleeve. Insects worked the undergrowth in layered, overlapping frequencies. A ribbon of pale fungi traced a fallen trunk to his left, vivid against the dark rot. The Force moved through all of it. He felt the living world pressing in from every direction, the warm noise of ten thousand organisms.

Except here.

A two-meter radius around the cliff face remained exposed stone. The Force existed within it—he wasn't touching the rock yet, having learned that lesson once already—but the quality had shifted. The living world pressed at the boundary and stopped. It was like watching light terminate at the edge of a shadow with no object to cast it.

The markings were identical.

Not similar. Not the product of two cultures sharing an ancestor. The branching motif, the channel depth, the convergence point at the center—all of it matched. He had spent six hours on the first world photographing those lines. He could have overlaid the LIDAR data from this cliff face without adjusting the scale by a millimeter.

"Same hand," he said.

"Same quarry tool." Desh crouched over the undergrowth several meters back. "Look at this."

Ben turned. Desh had found the edge of the cleared zone without sensing the metaphysical void. He had found it because nothing grew past the perimeter. The fungi stopped. The ground cover vanished. Even the leaf litter was absent inside a line so precise it looked surveyed.

"How far does it go?" Ben asked.

Desh walked the perimeter, counting paces. "Twelve meters diameter. Give or take." He stopped at the far edge and checked his wrist. "My chrono drifted four seconds on the first world. I reset it. It’s drifted six here."

"How long since the reset?"

"Three hours."

Ben looked at the central glyph. He pulled on his insulated gloves—both of them this time—and pressed his palm flat to the stone.

Cold. The void arrived as it had on the previous world, but Desh was right: it was louder. On the first world, it had been like pressing his ear to a wall and hearing the silence on the other side. Here, the wall had thinned. The pressure behind the vacuum was greater, the way water pushes harder through a smaller gap.

He counted four seconds, pulled back, and stepped out of the dead zone. The Force rushed back. He let it settle. His breath came steadier, though his pulse remained fast.

Desh stood, brushing leaf matter from his knee. He didn't ask what "it" was; he had stopped asking after the first world. His gaze tracked Ben’s hands as they trembled. Ben forced them still.

He pulled up his datapad and opened the survey coordinates Luke had transmitted before departure. Seven worlds. He had written them off as a standard search grid, the kind the Order used when they had a general area but no specific target. It had given the mission a sense of structure.

He plotted the coordinates manually, the way his GAG instructors had insisted. No shortcuts. Each point entered by hand. His fingers moved with the precision of habit as the first two points resolved on the display.

The seventh world wasn't in the opposite corner of the grid. It sat on a line.

He checked his work and entered the coordinates again.

Still a line.

"What are you seeing?" Desh asked.

Ben didn't answer immediately. The question hung there, waiting.

*Did he know?* The thought arrived with weight. Luke had handed him this mission the way he handed Ben most things—with enough information to be useful and enough withheld to let Ben find his own way. It had felt like trust. Now Ben wasn't certain.

Maybe Luke had sensed the wrongness along this bearing and sent Ben to confirm it without instructions. Or maybe Luke had looked at these coordinates and seen only a search grid, and the line was what happened when the Force itself pointed at something and a traveler drew a route toward the signal without recognizing the pattern.

He didn't know which was worse.

"The survey route," Ben said. "It's not random."

Desh moved closer. He didn't lean over Ben’s shoulder, but positioned himself where he could see the screen without crowding. "Show me."

Ben rotated the projection. "First world. Second world. They're on a bearing."

"Toward what?"

"That's what I'm trying to figure out."

Desh studied the data methodically. "You didn't notice this before."

"I wasn't looking for it," Ben said.

"And now?"

Ben didn't answer. He stood and walked toward the viewport. The jungle pressed against the glass, green and thick and utterly indifferent. His reflection stared back—a young man who had accepted a mission without asking the right questions. A Skywalker taught to trust his instincts, even as those instincts screamed he had been led somewhere blind.

"We should move," he said.

The walk back to the ship took eleven minutes. Neither spoke. Ben kept his attention on the Force the way one listens for a faint, repeating sound. It stayed at the edge of perception, a flat silence radiating from the ruin.

The *Steady Returns* sat where they had left her, half-concealed under a canopy overhang, her hull sweating in the humidity. Inside, the air was recycled and the nav computer hummed. Ben was grateful for both.

Desh went straight to the console. He didn't wait for direction.

"Give me the first world coordinates," he said.

Ben read them off. Desh entered them, then pulled up their current position. His fingers moved with the same methodical precision Ben had used. No shortcuts.

Ben watched him work. Desh’s face remained calm, but his shoulders shifted—the subtle tell of a man processing a threat.

Desh ran the calculation for thirty seconds. "Where's the next survey world?"

Ben read the numbers.

Desh entered them and stared at the projection. He slowly sat back in his chair.

The line on the starchart was clean. Three points, no deviation, extending from the Outer Rim toward the interior of the galaxy's southwest quadrant. Desh extended the projection past the third survey world, following the bearing. The line kept going.

It crossed the Rimma Trade Route. It continued past the last named systems on the chart.

The line ended in nothing. Not a system or a nebula, but a block of unmarked space. The notation was wrong. Blank space looked different from surveyed space with nothing in it. This looked like something had been removed.

"Where does it end?" Desh asked.

Ben studied the dead zone. The notation style was old—pre-New Republic, possibly older. Someone had filed a correction to the navigation records in the last millennium. The entry was simply: *nothing here.* Not *surveyed, uninhabited.* Not *hazard, avoid.* Nothing.

"I don't know," Ben said. "The records don't show anything."

"Records or charts?"

The distinction mattered. Desh had spent years in intelligence where the difference between no data and classified data was everything.

"Charts," Ben said. "The records might exist somewhere. But not in any database I can access."

Desh turned the projection, looking at the bearing from a different angle. He was reading the situation through training and pattern recognition, looking for what a Jedi might miss.

"Someone pulled these systems off the charts," Desh said.

"About a thousand years ago." Ben looked at the zone where the stars hadn't been named in a millennium. The wrongness pressed at him, stronger now. He could feel it even without the stone—a pressure against the Force, like something pushing back from the other side of a barrier. "It's not a map, Desh. It's a seal."

Desh’s hands stilled. He didn't turn around.

"And it's leaking," Ben finished.

Desh absorbed the statement without visible reaction, processing it into an operational question. When he turned, his eyes were sharp. "Then what do we do?"

Ben was already at the nav computer. His hands moved with the precision he had learned from instructors who understood that certainty was often the only thing that kept a pilot alive. Underneath that certainty was a current of momentum that felt less like a choice and more like inevitability.

"We follow the line," he said.

Desh nodded. He didn't ask if it was wise. He didn't ask if Luke had intended this. He simply began plotting the next jump.
