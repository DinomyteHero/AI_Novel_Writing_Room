The Force had gaps.

Not the clean silence of hyperspace—Ben had made a thousand jumps and knew that absence, the pressure against the edge of his perception like cotton packed around glass. This was different. The Force remained present but unreliable, a room with bad acoustics swallowing sound in pockets. He would reach, his perception would catch, and then the void would open before returning without explanation. Reach, catch, nothing, return. He kept his breathing steady and his face neutral, trying to stop anticipating the lapses. Bracing only made the impact worse.

Desh watched the sensor board with the focused stillness of a man who had learned not to say *that's strange* until he had three data points to support it. "Getting some ghosting on the passive array," he said. "Stellar bodies dropping off and reappearing. Could be interference from the sector's magnetic field."

"It's not the magnetic field," Ben said.

"I know." Desh didn't look up. "But that's what I'll put in the log."

Behind them, in the main hold, Kael had taken the seat nearest the emergency locker. Not the door—the locker. He hadn't opened it. His hand rested on the armrest, two fingers loose against the edge, close enough to the handle that the posture read as casual to anyone not paying attention. Ben was paying attention. Kael wasn't afraid of something he could fight. He was afraid of the room itself.

Sera stood at the forward bulkhead with both hands flat against the durasteel. Her weight was centered, her shoulders locked, her chin lowered—the posture of someone who had spent a long time learning how to hold a position when every instinct told her to move. The Force around her felt like a clenched fist. She was bracing, the way one might compress a wound.

Torin leaned against the port-side bunk frame with his arms crossed. His eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling or perhaps something beyond it. For the first time since the stasis chamber had cracked open and expelled him into the modern galaxy, his shoulders had dropped.

Ben watched him for three seconds.

"How are you holding up?"

The question was addressed to the room, but Ben’s gaze never left Torin.

Kael said nothing. Sera's head moved a fraction—not a response, just an acknowledgment that the question had landed.

Torin exhaled, a sound that was almost appreciative. "Better." He let the word sit. "The noise is gone."

"The noise," Ben repeated.

"The lattice." Torin turned his head toward Ben without lifting it from the bunk frame. "It has a pressure to it. I'd forgotten. A thousand years of it and you stop noticing, but out here—" He gestured vaguely at the space around them. "You can feel where it thins. Where it's just the Force, without the structure on top of it."

Kael's fingers went still against the armrest.

"And that feels better," Ben said.

"Clean." Torin's voice was easy, almost amused. "Like putting down a weight you'd been carrying so long you forgot it was there."

The *Steady Returns* shuddered—a minor fluctuation, likely a gravity wake from something Desh's ghosted sensors weren't tracking. Ben didn't look at the console. He kept his eyes on Torin.

"Ankhural called it the natural state," Torin said.

The words landed like a change in cabin pressure—not loud, but immediately physical. Something in Ben's inner ear recalibrated. His hand tightened on the armrest.

Torin wasn't citing a text. Ben had heard scholars cite texts—the elevated diction, the careful quotation marks built into the tone. This was different. Torin spoke the way a man spoke about a place he had lived, a view he had memorized, a particular quality of light in the early morning. *Ankhural called it the natural state* the way one might say *my mother used to call it home.*

Ben's throat tightened. He swallowed the sensation down.

Sera's hands came off the bulkhead. The movement was controlled, but the grip she took on the handhold above the hatch was too tight. Her knuckles paled. Her face was stone—not the stone of someone suppressing emotion, but of someone who had already processed the data and was deciding how to act. The Force around her shifted, a subtle reorientation, like a weapon being drawn.

Kael looked at Torin. The angle of his attention had changed. He was no longer a man watching a room; he was a man who had identified the specific threat in the room he needed to track. His expression remained flat, a focus so absolute it looked like nothing at all.

Ben thought about Jacen.

He didn't want to. The comparison arrived without invitation—not Jacen in the dark, not Jacen with a lightsaber, but Jacen *before*. He remembered Jacen explaining with complete sincerity that pain served a purpose, that the Force had shown him a greater architecture, that the suffering was the price of the peace to come. Jacen had never sounded like a villain. He had sounded like a man who had found the answer and was frustrated that no one else could see it.

Torin didn't sound like a villain. He sounded relieved.

That was the problem.

Ben's mouth was dry. He forced his voice to remain level. "The Ankhural Codex is one interpretation of the de-structured state."

"It is," Torin agreed. He sounded fond, as if speaking of a teacher who had been right about something vital. "There were others. Most of them were wrong." He finally lifted his head, his posture straightening. He was Torin again—warm, capable, the man who had made the group feel like a team for exactly long enough to stop being a concern. "Get some rest while you can, Skywalker. The next node won't give us time for philosophy."

He moved toward the cockpit. Ben watched him pass in silence. His hands stayed on the armrest, his breathing controlled. There was nothing to say that wouldn't either reveal his hand or start a conversation he wasn't ready to finish.

The hold felt larger after Torin left.

Kael picked up a ration pack, opened it, and began eating with methodical efficiency. He didn't look at Ben. His movements were mechanical—the discipline of a soldier who knew the body's needs didn't stop for a crisis.

Sera moved.

She crossed the hold in four steps and stopped beside Ben, close enough to keep her voice low. The Force around her was still locked tight, but a sharp clarity had replaced the tension. She spoke with the caution of a woman who had survived a millennium by weighing every word.

"Watch him." Her eyes were fixed on the cockpit hatch. "In the thin places. Watch what happens to his face."

She didn't say Torin's name. The hold was small and sound carried.

Ben looked at the hatch. Beyond it, he could hear Desh explaining the sensor array to Torin, patient and professional, unaware of what he was standing next to. Ben’s stomach remained tight.

The Force gaps continued. Reach, catch, nothing, return. Each nothing lasted a little longer than the last.
