The *Steady Returns* had been built for two people and a moderate amount of cargo. It was not built for two people, three passengers who had last occupied physical space during the New Sith Wars, and whatever Kael Drenn counted as.

Ben felt the ship’s weight distribution through the controls. In the Force, too—five extra presences crowded the cabin’s edges like furniture shoved against walls in a room too small to hold it. Sera, Torin, and Desh read as sharp, distinct signatures, compressed by stasis but still present. Kael read as almost nothing. A gap where a person should be. Ben checked for him the way a tongue probes a sensitive tooth.

The air recycler wheezed. The cockpit’s scent of synthetics and coolant was sliced by something older—stone dust and a flat, mineral dryness that clung to the three survivors. A thousand years of sealed air mixing with the recycled atmosphere of a mid-range freighter.

Ben kept his eyes on the hyperspace tunnel. He tried to ignore the smell.

The hatch slid open. He didn't need to look.

Sera Varik moved into the copilot’s space with the efficiency of someone accustomed to ship bridges—shoulders low, weight centered, no wasted motion. She stood over the navigation console. Her hand went out, reaching for a lever that wasn't there. Her fingers closed on air. She stared at the panel, then at her empty hand, then back at the display.

"The course correction," she said. "Manual or slaved to the navcomputer?"

"Navcomputer runs the hyperjump. I handle the exit vector." Ben watched her hand withdraw. "The interface is voice or haptic. There’s no physical helm lock in 47 ABY. Hasn't been standard for about four hundred years."

Recalibration flickered across her face. She studied the haptic interface as if mapping a new weapon system. Ben felt the cabin’s pressure shift—not with hostility, but with the weight of someone deciding whether to trust the terrain.

"The Corridor approach," she said, settling into the copilot’s seat. "You’re taking the Hydian."

"Section of the Hydian, then a private lane through the Meridian."

"Veraine will have the security infrastructure mapped. If she knew enough to copy the archive data before she left, she knows the modern transit lanes." Sera pulled up the star chart with two keystrokes—she’d learned that much in twenty minutes—and traced a line with one finger, hovering just above the display. Old habit. "The Jedi Lords would have gone direct. Announce the mission, request military escort, use the weight of the Order to clear the path."

Ben looked at the route she indicated. "That gets us there in eight days with the Council watching every move."

"It gets us there with backup."

"It gets us there with Veraine knowing our exact timeline and the Council pulling you four off my ship for debriefing." He kept his voice level, though his hands tightened on the armrest. "Which is the part you’re actually worried about."

Sera’s hand stopped. Her expression went rigid.

"You want to run this without authorization," she said. "No Council. No Order support."

"I want to run this without a three-week delay while bureaucrats decide whether you’re a historical artifact or a security threat."

She turned to face him fully. Ben caught the moment she understood the implications. He was willing to operate outside the structure she had spent a thousand years defending.

"The sector has a security net," she said, her voice sharpening. "Modern Alliance sensors. You’re proposing to move six people through it without clearance."

"Six," Ben confirmed.

"How?"

Ben reached past her and pulled up a secondary display. "The Meridian sector runs three sensor relays on the outer approach. They’re networked, which means they share a timing protocol. There’s a ghost window of forty seconds every ninety minutes when the handoff creates a blind spot." He tapped a coordinate. "We run modified transponder frequencies during the window. I can set the *Steady Returns* to read as a geological survey drone—automated, no crew manifest, no hail required."

"Modified transponder frequencies," Sera repeated. "That’s illegal."

"It’s illegal in four jurisdictions and unremarkable in twelve." He looked at her directly. "I spent two years running operations for the Galactic Alliance Guard. We exploited bureaucratic inconsistency for a living. The security net is full of holes."

Silence stretched. The hyperspace tunnel ran the length of the viewport, blue-white and indifferent. Ben waited, letting her process. She had come to him with doctrine; he was answering with pragmatism.

"The Galactic Alliance Guard," Sera said finally. It was an assessment being revised in real time.

"Intelligence operations. Enforcement. Some of it I’m not proud of." He left it there. "But I know how to move people through systems that aren't looking for them."

Sera looked at the star chart, then at him—really looked, the way she had examined the missing lever. When she spoke, the edge had vanished.

"The Corridor’s second node—the Veranthos relay—is a physical anchor structure, not just a coordinate. If Veraine reaches it before us, she won't destroy it. She’ll invert its resonance. Turn it from containment to amplification." She spoke as if handing him a weapon. Not out of generosity, but necessity. "That’s the one we cannot afford to lose."

Ben committed the detail to memory. "Then we don't lose it."

She didn't agree, but she didn't argue. She stood and moved back toward the hatch. Ben felt the tension in the cabin rebalance. She had tested him. He had answered in a language she understood.

***

Through the open panel to the common area, Ben watched Desh Rolan run a standard weapons check on his blaster. Strip, check, reassemble. The movements were automatic. Professional. Kael was folded into the corner bench opposite him, arms loose across his knees, watching Desh’s hands with specific, unwavering attention.

It wasn't a threat assessment. It was more precise, as if he were cataloguing the technique.

Desh glanced up once, registered the scrutiny, and returned to his work. GAG training: notice everything, show nothing.

Torin Hal was in the corridor when Ben stepped out of the cockpit. He leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, occupying the passage with the ease of someone who had spent decades in close-quarters military spaces. He smiled—a warm, self-deprecating expression.

"Your Knight Varik," Torin said. "She’s adjusting."

"She’s doing better than I would."

"She’s doing better than most." He paused. A moment of calculation flickered in his eyes. "Your father’s name reached us even then, you know. Not the man—the name. Skywalker. The Battle Masters argued about what it meant. Whether it was a title or a bloodline." He tilted his head. "I don't think they expected it to be both."

Ben kept his expression easy, but his shoulders stayed loose. Torin was fishing. He was complimenting Luke to see how Ben would respond, testing the distance between father and son.

"My father would say it’s neither," Ben said. "Just a name."

"Of course he would." Torin’s smile remained fixed. "That’s what makes it mean something."

It felt like a genuine compliment. Torin believed what he was saying, but belief and assessment were not mutually exclusive. He was offering warmth while measuring the gap between Luke Skywalker and the man standing in front of him.

"Get some sleep," Ben said. "We hit the Meridian approach in thirty hours."

Torin nodded and moved toward the passenger cabin without pressing further. The corridor felt wider after he left, though the dimensions hadn't changed.

Ben stood in the recycled air and the stone-dry smell of a millennium he hadn't lived. He listened to the ship strain. Sera had accepted his answer. Torin had measured him. Kael watched from his corner, saying nothing.

Thirty hours until the security net. Thirty hours to prove he could lead people who had fought wars before he was born.

Thirty hours until something broke.
