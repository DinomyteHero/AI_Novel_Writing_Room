The *Steady Returns* had been patched so many times that its original components were likely in the minority. Ben counted four different manufacturers' panels in the cockpit alone. The throttle assembly was held in place by a bracket that looked hand-machined—Desh’s work, almost certainly. The man had always held rigid opinions about tolerances.

Ben sat in the co-pilot's seat with a diagnostic stylus, chasing a fluctuation in the port-side fuel mix. The readout drifted. It wasn't enough to matter, but it was enough to be wrong. He found the flickering numbers easier to track than the other sensation he was trying to ignore.

"Injector coupling," Desh said, eyes fixed on the pilot's console. "Third one from the left. I've got a replacement in the aft locker, but she runs fine once she's warm."

"You knew about this."

"I know about everything. I just don't fix everything."

Ben logged the coupling and let the philosophy slide. Outside the viewport, hyperspace ran blue and silent—the color of deep water, the color of nothing.

Desh's hands remained steady on the controls. Scarred across the knuckles and thickened at the joints from old breaks, they had healed well enough. Ben noticed them the way he noticed all the things that weren't there: no hum, no resonance, no echo in the Force. Most people registered as a mood, a pressure, or a faint directional pull of intent. Desh registered as the shape of a person with the weight removed. A warm absence. In the years since Jacen, Ben had found it easier to breathe around absences.

He filed that thought before it could take root.

"Caf?" Desh asked.

"You already made it."

"Observant. Yes or no."

"Yes."

Desh poured from a battered thermos and extended the mug. The handoff was clean. They'd done it a thousand times in a hundred different cramped spaces—observation posts, deployment transports, the narrow corridor of a certain garrison ship on Coruscant. Ben took the mug. The caf was strong enough to strip paint, exactly the way Desh had always brewed it.

He drank. For a few minutes, the only sounds were the hum of the hyperdrive and the minor percussion of the injector coupling.

Then Desh's left hand moved. Two fingers extended, tapped the console twice, paused, then tapped once more.

*Perimeter's clean. Stand down.*

The signal had no place on a civilian freighter. Neither of them had used it intentionally. Desh's hand returned to the throttle. Ben stared into his mug.

The cockpit felt smaller.

"The coupling needs replacing before we hit atmo," Ben said.

"I know."

"I'm just saying."

"You're looking for something to fix," Desh countered.

Ben turned the mug. The thermos had a dent in it he remembered causing, though the exact day had slipped his mind. Around 40 ABY, probably. The years blurred in that stretch. They blurred on purpose.

The wrongness arrived low and without warning. It wasn't a sound. It was the moment a room becomes silent for too long, when the lack of noise gains mass. The sensation moved through his sternum and settled there—a hollow pressure with no visible source. He'd felt it for months, though it never manifested the same way twice. This time, it felt like something pulling slowly apart.

He set the mug down before his grip could crush it.

Desh glanced over for a single second. He reached for the thermos and refilled his own cup.

"Sensors are throwing garbage again," Desh said, nodding at the nav display. A string of amber icons marched across the hyperspace corridor—minor deviations, meaningless on their own. Eleven of them, spread across the route. "Started forty minutes out. I keep correcting, they keep drifting back."

Ben leaned forward. The icons pulsed. "Same direction each time?"

"Forward and to port. Like the lane wants to avoid something." Desh tapped the display. "I've run this corridor twice. It doesn't do this."

"Instrument error."

"Could be." Desh didn't sound convinced. "Or the galaxy's getting bigger and nobody told us."

Ben stared at the eleven amber markers. All leaning the same way. The hollow pressure in his chest deepened, pointing like a compass needle toward that bearing.

He'd spent three months trying to explain this to his father. He had searched for language that made the sensation legible to someone who experienced the Force as light and flow. His father had listened carefully and heard *trauma* when Ben said *fracture*. The distinction had seemed vital then. Now, watching the flags align with the pull in his chest, the argument felt academic.

Something the sensors could see. Something Desh could see.

"It's not instrument error," Ben said.

Desh looked at him—the long version, the look he used when deciding how to frame a difficult truth. 

"I know," Desh said. "That's why I mentioned it."

He turned back to the console and began a manual correction. His movements were deliberate. He didn't ask what the flags meant, nor did he ask about Ben's expression. He just worked.

Ben watched the display. The corrections took. The flags turned green, held for ninety seconds, and drifted amber again.

Desh's shoulders tensed. He ran the correction a second time.

Same result.

"Two minutes to reversion," Desh said. His voice remained flat, but his grip tightened. He reached up and silenced the automated approach chime, keeping the cockpit quiet.

Ben gripped the armrests.

The hyperspace tunnel collapsed. Blue-white light snapped into stars and the hard geometry of realspace. The ship lurched—a vibration that ran through the frame and into Ben's palms. He'd felt rough reversions before, but this was different. The ship had pushed against a resistance in the lane that shouldn't have existed.

The survey world appeared on the scanners. Small. Mostly rock. A thin atmosphere.

Ben didn't look at the data.

The wrongness hit him like a door opening onto a void. It wasn't a surge; it was the removal of a buffer. The hollow pressure in his sternum became specific and directional, pointing toward that smear of rock like a needle finding its pole.

His knuckles went white.

Beside him, Desh's breathing grew shallow and fast. His body acknowledged what his mind wouldn't: something was wrong in a way that hands couldn't fix.

"There," Desh said, reading the scanner. He spoke with the forced steadiness of someone holding very still. "Looks quiet."

It wasn't.
