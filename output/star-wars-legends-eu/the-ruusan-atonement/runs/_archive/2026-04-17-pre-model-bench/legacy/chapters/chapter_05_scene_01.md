The release mechanism was a single recessed lever, cold alloy under Ben's palm. It trembled. Not from age, but from the field itself—a millennium of stored potential pressing against its housing like water behind a dam. He felt the vibration through the metal, up his arm, and into the Force where the stasis field registered as a held chord on the edge of resolution.

He pulled.

A long, low hiss followed. Pressure equalized. The hum of the power cycle dropped from operational to absent, and the blue light in the alcove died. For a heartbeat, the chamber remained a dark niche in a dark room.

Then the woman inside moved.

She didn't stumble. Her left hand gripped the alcove's frame before her right foot had cleared the housing. She stood upright before Ben could offer support. Her muscles seized—the tremor visible in her forearms—but she locked her knees and endured it. Her Force presence hit him at the same moment. Ben took an involuntary step back.

Dense. That was the only word. Modern Jedi training refined, smoothed, and minimized waste; her presence lacked such polish. It pressed against his perception like a blade against a palm—sharp, deliberate, and ancient.

Her eyes fixed on him. Brown, precise, assessing.

"Jedi," she said. The word was clipped, formal, pronounced as a rank rather than a title. "Status report. Is the perimeter secure?"

Ben's instinct was to match her precision, to establish that he understood the protocol. He caught himself. "We're the only ones here. The upper levels are clear."

"You are the only ones," she repeated, as if the sentence had a structural flaw. Her gaze moved to Desh, catching on his gear—the compact blaster at his thigh, the tactical vest, the modern helmet pushed back. Her posture shifted. Not hostile, but categorizing. "Identify your House affiliation and commanding Lord."

The questions landed like a test he was failing. Ben felt the weight of her assumption—that there was a House, a Lord, a chain of command that still meant something.

"I don't have one. I'm Ben Skywalker, Jedi Knight. This is Desh Rolan."

The name Skywalker moved across her face and left nothing. No recognition. He had expected the void, yet it still stung—a small, sharp thing he filed away.

"Knight Varik," she said. "Sera Varik. I was Gavran's lieutenant." She turned, taking in the ring of alcoves. Her voice dropped half a register. "The others."

It wasn't a question. Ben moved to the second active chamber, grateful for the directive. "Two more. I'll get them."

He didn't wait for permission. Behind him, she took a breath more controlled than it had any right to be.

The second lever released with the same hiss. Ben stepped back.

The man inside remained motionless for three seconds. Then he emerged sideways, pressing his back to the frame and keeping the wall behind him before his eyes had fully opened. He focused on Desh first—on the blaster—and stayed there. His Force presence was almost nothing. Not empty, but sealed. He had trained it inward until it barely registered, the way an operative might breathe shallowly to hide a heat signature.

He was younger than Sera. He looked at Ben last, then at Sera, then at the floor between them. He remained silent.

"Name," Sera commanded.

A pause stretched. Ben felt the man's calculation—the weight of the decision to answer at all.

"Kael." No House. No rank. Just the name, offered like a concession.

Ben filed that away and moved to the third chamber.

Torin Hal collapsed.

The field released and he pitched forward with momentum his legs couldn't match. Ben caught him, shoving a shoulder under the man's arm before he hit the floor. He was large—Ben felt the mass of someone who had spent years in physical training—and he sagged heavily before finding his footing.

"Apologies." His voice was rough from disuse but warm, a low register that carried. "I had intended a more dignified arrival."

Ben helped him straighten. Torin's Force presence was heavy, like the heat off a cooling engine. Underneath it, Ben's instincts flagged something he couldn't name—a pressure that didn't match the warmth, the way a smile might mask the eyes.

Torin looked at him directly and grinned. It was a good smile, the kind that invited trust. Ben felt the pull of it even while recognizing the effect.

"Battle Master Torin Hal." He looked past Ben to Sera. His expression shifted—relief, genuine and unguarded, before it settled back into assessment. "Varik. You're well."

"Better than some." Her voice was less clipped. A recognition passed between them that Ben couldn't quite place.

The lights flickered.

All five looked up. The emergency strips pulsed once, held, and steadied at a lower output. Somewhere deep in the facility's infrastructure, a mechanism ground out a final protest and went silent. A vibration traveled through the floor and into Ben's boots before fading.

Desh rested a hand on his blaster. He didn't draw. He looked to Ben for a lead.

Ben shook his head. Not yet. But the gesture felt thin—a performance of control when the room had already shifted.

Torin scanned the room, cataloging the ring of alcoves. He was quick and methodical, the automatic assessment of a man used to counting threats and resources. He counted the dark chambers. He counted the active ones they had just vacated. His gaze circled the ring twice, then stopped.

"There are twelve positions," he said.

Ben waited. This was the moment the scene would fracture.

"We are three." Torin's voice went level. "Nine are gone." He looked at the gap in the ring—the bare mounting brackets, the bent metal, the absent transparisteel. "Except that isn't twelve."

Kael had moved to the far side of the room. He stood with his back to the wall, watching Torin. Watching all of them. His Force presence had tightened further, a man making himself small.

Sera had already seen the breach. Ben could tell by her stillness. She was waiting, just as Ben was, to see what Torin would do with the information. To see if he would force Ben to say it aloud.

"Knight Varik," Torin said. "Explain the twelfth position."

"I don't have an explanation. I woke minutes ago."

He turned to Ben. His expression was open, almost gentle. That was worse than anger.

"The monitoring log on the console will tell you more than I can," Ben said. "But the short version is that someone breached that chamber from the inside. The log dates it at six weeks ago."

Torin processed this with the stillness of someone accustomed to bad news. His expression didn't fracture. He looked at the empty alcove, then toward the central shaft leading down to Level 4. The warmth in his Force presence went cold.

"What year," he asked, "is it?"

The question was quiet. Ben had known it was coming from the moment Sera asked about his House, from the moment she looked at Desh's gear as if it were an alien language. He had prepared for it, yet it still felt like stepping off a ledge.

"47 ABY," Ben said. "After the Battle of Yavin."

Silence.

Sera's posture remained rigid. Her control was absolute, but Ben watched it cost her—the tendons in her hands tightened, and her jaw set. Kael looked at nothing. Torin looked at Ben with an expression doing too much work to stay neutral.

"The Ruusan Reformations," Sera said. Each word was placed deliberately, like a stone in a wall that might collapse. "When did they end?"

"A thousand years ago."

The silence stretched. The facility's lights held their dim output. The room seemed to hold its breath.

Torin moved first. He turned back to the ring, to the dark chambers and the shattered gap where the twelfth had been. His hand came up, fingers spread. For a moment, Ben thought he might touch the empty alcove. He didn't. His arm dropped.

"The seal," he said. He wasn't asking; he was accounting. "The others didn't make it." He paused, performing the arithmetic—not just the bodies, but the reality of twelve anchors becoming three. His voice dropped. "And whatever was in that chamber—"

He looked at Ben.

"Who got out?"
