The hangar bay smelled like fuel and hot metal, the high ceiling amplifying every sound into something larger than it was. Ben sat in the cockpit of his ship with the departure checklist half-complete on the secondary screen and his datapad in his hand, thumb hovering over the Jedi partner roster.

Three names. All of them good. Jysella Horn had filed with him twice before and knew how to work a survey run without turning it into a collaborative debrief. Valin was steadier than he'd been two years ago. Natua Wan was methodical, thorough, exactly the kind of partner you wanted on an Outer Rim observation mission.

He swiped past all three and opened his personal contacts.

*Practical,* he told himself. *This is a survey run, not a combat deployment. A non-Force-sensitive with field experience reads the data without projecting onto it.* The logic held up. He'd constructed it carefully on the walk down from the Temple's upper levels, and it continued to hold up now, which was the thing about well-constructed logic — it didn't develop cracks just because you looked at it.

He tapped Desh Rolan's name before he could run the argument again.

The long-range connection took four seconds to establish. The comm crackled once, twice, and then Desh's voice came through, slightly tinny with distance.

"Rolan."

"It's Ben. You busy?"

A pause — not hesitation, but the quick silent inventory Ben recognized from three years of GAG joint operations, back when he and Desh had both been running on too little sleep and the work had seemed cleaner than it looked in retrospect. Desh assessed before he spoke. He'd always assessed before he spoke.

"Define busy," Desh said.

"Outer Rim survey run. Four weeks, maybe five. Twelve systems along the Perlemian's outer spur. I need someone who can handle comms, logistics, and secondary sensor analysis without asking me what the Force thinks about it every six hours."

"So that's a no on the scenic tour of Coruscant's lower levels."

"Hard no."

"Pay?"

"Standard consulting rate, mission hazard supplement if anything develops."

"Anything likely to develop?"

Ben looked out through the cockpit viewport at the hangar's far wall. A maintenance crew was running diagnostics on a transport two bays over, their voices bouncing off the durasteel in fragments. "I don't know yet. That's part of what we're going to find out."

"Right." Another pause, shorter. "I can be at Hangar Bay Fourteen in forty minutes."

"I'm in Twelve. And I'm leaving in thirty."

"Then I'll be in Twelve in twenty-eight."

The comm clicked off. Ben set the datapad on the copilot seat and went back to the checklist, the familiar relief of it settling in his chest — not warmth, exactly, but the particular ease of knowing how someone operated. Desh wouldn't ask about Jedi backup. He never asked about things he'd already worked out. It was one of the qualities that had made him useful to the GAG, and one of the qualities that had made him dangerous to the people the GAG had been pointed at, and one of the qualities that made Ben trust him in a way he didn't examine too closely.

Twenty-six minutes later, Desh dropped his kit bag through the hatch and climbed in after it.

"You look terrible," he said, settling into the copilot seat and pulling up the secondary console without being asked.

"Survey mission. Outer Rim. Twelve systems."

"You said." Desh ran his eyes over the pre-flight data. "Perlemian spur, flagged anomalies, Force readings. I'm inferring the rest." He glanced sideways. "Departure window?"

"Open."

"Then let's go."

Ben ran the final pre-flight sequence. The engines rose from idle to operational, the vibration changing pitch under his boots, and he walked the ship through the hangar bay's departure protocol — traffic control, clearance codes, the careful maneuvering through the Temple's approach corridor before the Coruscant traffic grid handed him a lane and he opened the throttle.

The city dropped away below. He didn't watch it go.

The upper atmosphere thinned, the sky darkening from orange-brown to deep violet to black, and then they were clear and the nav computer had the first jump plotted and Coruscant was just a glow at their backs. Ben checked the instruments out of habit — all nominal, all green.

He reached out with his senses, expecting the pre-jump quiet he'd had his whole life. The moment before hyperspace always had a particular quality, the Force settling into the compressed awareness of near-lightspeed transition.

The wrongness was still there.

He'd expected it to soften with distance from the Temple, where seventeen survey reports' worth of accumulated anomaly data had been sitting in his father's files. It hadn't softened. It had the same quality it had in the office, in the training salle, in the archives — not a disturbance, not noise, but the flat absence of something that should have been present.

He hit the hyperdrive.

Stars stretched into lines, lines collapsed into the blue-white tunnel of hyperspace, and the instruments flickered once — a single-frame glitch across three screens that resolved immediately. Normal. Hyperspace transition artifacts were normal.

The wrongness didn't muffle.

Ben's hands stayed on the controls. In hyperspace the Force went quiet in certain ways — not absent, but compressed, the vast network of living things reduced to whatever was aboard the ship. He'd made hundreds of jumps and he knew the texture of that compression. This wasn't it. The absence was still there, clear and persistent, the same as it had been in the Temple.

And then it shifted.

Not louder. Not different in character. But where before it had been a static presence — everywhere and nowhere, a quality of the space around him — now it had an angle. A direction. He tried to locate it the way he'd locate a disturbance and found that the analogy didn't work; you located disturbances by moving toward or away from them until the sensation changed. This didn't change when he moved his attention. It simply pointed.

He sat with that for three seconds, his hands loose on the controls, the hyperspace tunnel streaming past the viewport in its usual indifferent blue.

*Residual effect,* he thought. *Hyperspace artifacts, the jump destabilizing the perception—*

The direction didn't waver.

He'd chosen this heading. He'd plotted the first jump himself, based on the survey corridor his father had assembled, based on the cluster of flagged reports near the Perlemian spur. He had reasons for going where he was going. Good reasons. A solid mission profile.

The absence held its heading, steady and patient, like a vector he'd been handed without being consulted.

Beside him, Desh was running through the sensor array calibration, making small adjustments with the focused quiet of someone who knew his job. He didn't look up.

Ben kept his eyes on the hyperspace stream and did not reach for the rationalization already forming at the back of his mind, because he knew, with the particular clarity that came from having avoided a thing long enough to recognize it, that the wrongness wasn't following him.

It had been waiting for him to move.
