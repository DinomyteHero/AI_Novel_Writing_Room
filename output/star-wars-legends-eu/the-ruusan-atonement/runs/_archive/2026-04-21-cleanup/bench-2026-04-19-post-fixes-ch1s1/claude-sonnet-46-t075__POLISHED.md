The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Not a bad hit. Clean form, good extension — the kind of score that would have earned a nod from Cilghal back when she was still running the advanced salle. He knew it was clean because he'd seen it coming and still hadn't moved in time.

"Three for three," his partner said, not gloating. Tomas Reeve was too even-tempered for gloating. He lowered his blade and tilted his head slightly, the way people did when they were deciding whether to say the thing they were thinking. "You want to go again?"

Ben clicked his training saber off. The hum died. "I'm done."

"Fatigue?"

"Fatigue." He pulled the word out of the air and handed it over like it was his.

Reeve accepted it without comment. He was a good partner that way — no follow-up questions, no concerned looks held a beat too long. He deactivated his own blade, tucked it under his arm, and said, "Tomorrow, then," and left Ben the salle.

The door slid shut.

Ben stood there for a moment, hilt loose in his fingers, the recycled air cooling the sweat at the back of his neck. The overhead panels cast the room in their standard late-session white. Somewhere down the corridor, a lift chimed, and the soft scuff of boots marked the end of someone else's evening.

He should leave.

He didn't leave.

---

The salle was long, with a high ceiling and floor mats worn pale at the center from years of footwork. Ben walked to the far end, set his training saber on the equipment rack, and stood still for a moment with his hands at his sides.

Then he started the first form.

No blade. Just the stances, the transitions, the weight shifts his body had memorized before he was old enough to remember memorizing them. He moved through the opening sequence slow enough to feel each position, each angle, each breath.

His body knew the form. That wasn't the question.

He came to the fourth transition and paused.

There it was.

Not in his muscles. Not in his joints or his balance or his footwork. His body was exactly where it should be, doing exactly what he'd asked it to. But underneath the physical rhythm — under the sound of his own breathing and his feet on the mat — the salle's presence was arriving late. One half-beat behind where it should be, where it had always been. Like a musician who's been playing the same piece for years and suddenly the hall's acoustics are wrong, and he can't tell if the echo is late or if he is.

He moved through the fifth transition. The sixth. Each time, the same quiet wrongness. Not louder. Not different. Just there, waiting for him to stop pretending he couldn't feel it.

He stopped pretending.

Ben lowered his arms and stood in the middle of the empty salle, breathing. His feet were bare on the mat. He pressed them flat, feeling the slight give of the worn surface, and waited to see if the lag would pass the way it sometimes did — briefly, like a signal clearing static — or if it would just sit there.

It sat there.

The thing was, he'd been through bad stretches before. His father's disappearance. Abeloth. The Sith-influenced period he still didn't like thinking about, the one that had cost him things he hadn't fully finished cataloguing. He knew what it felt like when his own perception was compromised, when the Force was showing him something filtered through his own damage. He'd learned that particular flavor of wrongness the hard way.

This wasn't that.

This was the salle itself. The ambient presence of the place — the residue of a thousand training sessions, the low hum of other Knights' practice laid down over years like sediment — arriving off-rhythm. Not absent. Not dark. Just late, like the signal was traveling a fraction of a second further than it should.

He'd felt it first four days ago. Dismissed it as post-mission fatigue. Felt it again the next morning during meditation, dismissed it as post-meditation adjustment. Felt it twice during solo drills, dismissed it as — he couldn't even remember what he'd told himself by that point. Something plausible. He was good at plausible.

Tonight he'd dropped his guard in front of Reeve, and the excuse he'd handed over tasted exactly like what it was.

*Fatigue*, he thought, with the particular self-directed dryness of a man who has caught himself in a lie and is at least honest enough to find it slightly funny. *Sure.*

He ran the form again from the beginning. Slower this time, not testing his body but listening past it, trying to locate the lag the way you'd try to locate a sound with your eyes closed — turning your head, waiting for the point where it got louder.

It didn't get louder. It didn't get quieter. It stayed exactly where it was, threaded through the ambient field like something constant and wrong. The salle's presence arriving at him through something slightly thicker than air, the way sound moved differently underwater — not absent but changed, delayed by a fraction he couldn't measure and couldn't ignore.

He moved through the third transition and felt it again, the half-beat that wasn't his, and this time he didn't compensate and didn't dismiss it. He let it cost him the timing, felt the form go fractionally wrong, and stood in the resulting position — off-balance in a way that had no physical cause — and held it.

The Order's current position, as best he could tell from three weeks of corridor conversations and one briefing he hadn't been invited to, was that the residual disturbances since Abeloth were fading. Background noise. Healing. The kind of thing you didn't flag unless it was actionable.

Ben was a Skywalker. He'd grown up knowing what his name meant to other people in the Order — the assumption that certain things came naturally, that his perception was reliable by inheritance, that if Ben Skywalker said the Force felt fine, it felt fine. He'd spent years not correcting that assumption because correcting it would have required explaining things he didn't want to explain.

He wasn't fine.

He didn't know if that meant *he* wasn't fine or if something else wasn't fine, and that distinction mattered more than he could currently articulate.

His feet found the mat. The form waited.

He was halfway through the seventh transition when the door slid open behind him.

Ben didn't turn immediately. He finished the position — weight forward, arms completing their arc — and then let his hands fall and looked over his shoulder.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and the expression Ben had known since he was twelve years old. The one that meant Luke had already decided, had already thought through the angles, and had come to find his son because the mission had a shape that fit him.

Ben's pulse steadied, which was strange, given that the half-beat lag was still there at the edge of everything.

He turned to face the door.
