The Steady Returns came out of hyperspace with a soft lurch that barely moved the floor under Ben’s boots.

He stood at the forward viewport with his hands behind his back, letting the bridge glass fill with a world that ought to have been ordinary. Temperate. Blue oceans. Broad green continents mottled with cloud. A settlement world with neat coastlines and a single moon hanging pale over the limb.

Normal.

Desh broke that thought before it could settle.

“That’s wrong,” he said.

Ben glanced over. Desh had one hand braced on the edge of the sensor pit, the other holding his datapad low enough that the display cast a cold wash over his face. He looked less impressed by the view than by a bad accounting error.

Ben let his gaze move from the world to the readouts on the forward repeater. “Define wrong.”

Desh tapped the screen. “Define impossible. Thermal returns say half the visible tree cover is ash. Atmospheric scatter says there’s a storm front over the western basin, and the viewport says clear skies. Bioscans are throwing ghosts. And the settlement grid—” He stopped, rechecked the feed, then exhaled through his nose. “The settlement grid says there aren’t any birds.”

Ben looked back out at the planet.

A pair of white shapes drifted over a river delta. For a second he took them for birds.

Then they held their spacing too neatly, turning in lockstep, and he saw they were clouds mirrored in the water far below.

“No birds,” Desh said again, quieter this time, as if saying it harder might make it true.

Sera came up beside Ben, already straight-backed, arms folded under her chest as if she had to physically contain the situation. “Scan resolution?”

“Fine enough to shame the techs on Coruscant,” Desh said. “And before you ask, yes, I checked the calibration.”

Kael lounged in the shadow of the overhead strut, one shoulder against the bulkhead, as if the bridge had inconvenienced him by existing. “Maybe the birds are shy.”

Sera gave him a look that should have filed edges off metal. “This is not amusing.”

“It is a little amusing,” Kael said. “Not much. Just enough to keep us honest.”

Torin stood near the access hatch, hands clasped behind his back, wearing calm the way some men wore a uniform. He tipped his head toward the planet. “Any sign of an active scramble? Jamming? Decoys?”

Desh snorted. “If someone put a decoy system over an entire world, I want their vendor list.”

Ben kept watching the planet. The ocean colors were right. The landmasses were right. Even the weather patterns looked plausible. That was the problem. The surface was dressed like a real world and wearing the costume too well.

He let his awareness ease outward.

The Force came back to him.

Not gone. Not absent. That would have been easier, in a way. It was there in the same instant as the ship’s vibration under his feet, there in the pressure of recycled air and the faint tang of coolant, there in the pulse behind his eyes.

He reached for it the way he always did, for shape, for pattern, for the old familiar current that told him where danger crouched and where the path ran clean.

Nothing answered.

Not silence. Not even resistance.

Just presence with no seams. Space with no edge. A vastness that did not hold. He caught at it and his mind slid through, as though his hand had passed into water and found no wall, no current, no depth to brace against.

Ben went still.

Sera noticed. Of course she did. “What is it?”

He took a slow breath. Kept his voice even. “I’m not sure yet.”

That was true enough to be useful.

Torin’s gaze flicked to him, then back to the world. “You’ve got that tone.”

“What tone?”

“The one you use when you’ve already decided something ugly and you’re trying to be polite about it.”

Kael gave a low murmur. “So, basically, his normal face.”

Ben almost smiled. Almost.

The Steady Returns began its descent. The planet swelled in the viewport until cloud bands gave way to ridgelines and long seams of waterways. The landing site came into view: a former settlement valley, streets still traced, structures still standing in grids that should have reassured him.

They didn’t.

Green growth climbed the buildings in the wrong directions.

Vines draped down from rooflines where they ought to have reached up for light, and narrow leaves angled toward shadowed alley mouths instead of the sun. Flower clusters bloomed under overhangs where no rain touched them, white and delicate in the dark. Their stems bent against gravity in patient little arcs, as if the world had decided up and down were suggestions.

Ben saw a bird-shaped shadow cross a plaza below.

Nothing followed it.

No call. No wingbeat. No rustle from the trees.

The silence hit him harder than the view.

Not a vacuum. Not the sealed hush of space. An absence made by subtraction, by something that should have been there and wasn’t. The landing struts came down with a muted clang that seemed too loud for the world outside, and Ben’s skin tightened along his forearms.

Desh read the ramp diagnostics aloud as if talking to himself might keep the numbers from doing whatever they were doing. “Chrono drift’s still there. Twelve point six milliseconds off standard in less than a minute. That’s not transport lag.”

Sera frowned. “Localized?”

“Localized enough to make me wish it wasn’t.”

The hatch opened.

Warm air slid into the ship carrying damp earth, crushed green growth, and a faint mineral scent from the exposed ground. Ben stepped down the ramp first. Habit. The others followed, boots thudding onto a landing pad half-swallowed by that wrong-growing vegetation. The concrete should have been cracked by roots. Instead, the roots pushed through intact seams as if they had learned where to touch to avoid breaking anything.

He looked across the settlement.

The streets were there. The skeletal frames of markets and habitation blocks. Solar arrays that leaned at a modest angle, clean enough to have been maintained by someone who had given up and then changed his mind about it. Patches of moss gleamed wet in the shade. Flowers opened under a stairwell where sunlight never reached.

No birds in the trees. No insects in the reeds by the drainage ditch. No skitter of small life under the grass.

Nothing moved except the vegetation, and even that seemed to move with a delay, as though the world had to remember which way growth pointed.

Torin scanned the area, one hand shading his eyes. “There should be settlement traffic.”

“There should be a lot of things,” Desh muttered.

Kael crouched by the edge of the landing pad and touched a vine. He let it slide over his knuckles, then looked up. “This stuff is warm.”

Ben looked at him. “Warm?”

“Not sun-warm.” Kael’s mouth twisted. “Alive-warm. Fresh.”

Sera’s hand hovered near her belt, though there was nothing to draw against the air. “This site was hit weeks ago?”

Ben nodded. “That’s what Veraine’s trail said.”

“Then why does it look like this?” she asked.

No one answered. The question sat in the warm air between them.

Ben stepped off the landing pad.

The ground under his boots gave a little, as if soil had loosened since the last scan. He moved down the access path toward the old central green, and with every step the wrongness thickened. A line of flowering shrubs on his right leaned toward the shadow under an awning, not toward the light. Their blossoms opened wider there, petals pale as bone. He stopped and looked at them from the corner of his eye.

The flowers closed a fraction.

He kept walking.

A dry sound came from somewhere to his left. He turned and caught sight of a tree branch flexing as a cluster of leaves shifted toward the underside of a balcony, chasing the dimmer patch there. Not growth. Direction. The plant knew where not to go.

Ben’s throat went dry.

He drew in the world through his senses. Damp earth. Living green. Sun on dust. The metallic edge of old building materials warming under daylight. All of it should have anchored him.

Instead, the Force rose around him without shape.

He reached for it again, sharper this time, using the meditative habit drilled into him since he was old enough to sit still longer than a minute. Let the current come. Let it name itself. Let the familiar handrail guide him.

There was no handrail.

He dropped inward.

His stomach lurched. Not from motion. From the loss of something his body had expected and not gotten. The world tilted, and for one alarming instant he had the impression that the ground had gone soft beneath all of them, not in texture but in rule, as if every stone, every wall, every breath had been built on an agreement that no longer held.

He caught himself with a hand on the edge of a planter.

The concrete was solid. The planter was solid. His fingers were solid against it.

None of that helped.

Ben drew one long breath through his nose and tried to center. The old sequence rose automatically: body, breath, place, present. Simple. Reliable. He had used it in smoky engine rooms, under blaster fire, inside collapsing caves. He could use it here.

Body: boots on ground.

Breath: warm air, damp earth.

Place: settlement valley, world unknown but mapped.

Present—

The Force offered no feedback at all.

No warm pulse. No pressure in the chest. No subtle tilt toward danger or calm. He stood there with his mind extended and got nothing back but raw existence, bright and unbuffered. Energy without contour. Potential without direction.

Like light in a room where every surface had been stripped away.

Ben straightened too fast. His pulse had started to climb and there was nowhere for it to go.

Sera was watching him now with that severe, measuring focus she used when she wanted to cut a problem down to size. “Ben.”

He swallowed once. “Don’t touch the trees.”

Kael barked a quiet laugh. “That’s your expert assessment?”

“Stay with the landing site,” Ben said, sharper than he meant. He drew the edge off the words before Sera could turn them into a fight. “Something’s wrong with the local lattice.”

Desh’s head came up. “The what?”

Ben nearly answered. Nearly gave the explanation that would have made it less immediate and more survivable. He stopped himself. “The way the world feels to a Force-sensitive. It’s broken.”

Sera’s gaze narrowed. She knew enough to hear what he wasn’t saying.

Torin glanced from Ben to the trees, then back. “Broken how?”

Ben opened his mouth.

Desh beat him to it.

“Like my chrono,” he said, and held it up.

The little device sat in his palm, its display bright against the greenery. Ben looked, and at first nothing seemed wrong. Then the second hand twitched forward, stopped, jerked back a fraction, then advanced two ticks at once. The numbers on the display stuttered by one, then corrected, then drifted again.

Desh turned the chrono so everyone could see. “That’s not damage. That’s not interference. It’s tracking time badly enough to annoy me.”

Kael leaned in, squinting. “Mine’s on schedule.”

Desh gave him a flat look. “Of course it is. You’d survive an asteroid field by being too irritating for space itself to keep up.”

That earned the smallest sound from Ben that might have been a laugh if it had had somewhere to land.

Then Desh’s expression changed. Not fear. Something flatter, more stripped down. He looked past the chrono, past the others, at the empty plaza.

“Great,” he said. “That’s new.”

Sera turned. “What is?”

Desh blinked once, as if testing the words before he admitted them. “There’s this pressure. Not pain. Not exactly. Like I’m about to be told someone I knew died and nobody’s decided which person yet.”

Torin’s face lost some of its warmth. “Can you be specific?”

“I’d love to be specific.” Desh pocketed the chrono with careful fingers. “I can’t.”

Ben stared at him.

Desh was not Force-sensitive. He had no reason to stand here with that expression, one hand resting on his own chest as if the air had gotten heavier inside it. The realization hit Ben hard and ugly: whatever Veraine had done, it wasn’t a Jedi problem. It wasn’t a problem for people who could be taught to compensate. It hit the bones under all of that.

Sera’s voice sharpened. “Chronological drift and emotional displacement. That supports a localized de-structuring event.”

Kael gave her a sideways glance. “You say that like it’s comforting.”

“It is not meant to be comforting.”

“It’s a miracle you have any friends at all.”

She ignored him. Ben almost did too, then caught the movement of his own hand as he flexed his fingers and realized he had been trying to anchor himself through muscle tension, as if the body could substitute for intuition.

It couldn’t.

He tried again, smaller. He reached into the Force the way he’d reached for a corridor handrail in low gravity once as a kid, trusting his grip to tell him where the floor was.

The presence answered.

No, not answered. Persisted. The Force was there, undeniable, thick as air. But the lines that normally gave it shape had been pulled out. No current. No slope. No pulse that said this way is safe and that way is harm. No subtle whisper at the edge of thought. Just the raw fact of it, infinite and unorganized.

Ben’s breath caught.

He felt the world under him with brutal clarity. Every heartbeat in his chest. The grit under his boot. The tiny prickle of sweat cooling at his collar. The texture of the planter against his palm. All of it. Too much of it.

And nothing to guide him through it.

He took a step back without meaning to and nearly missed the curb behind him. Torin’s hand came out half an inch, then stopped when Ben righted himself. No one else seemed sure whether to help or give him space.

He hated that he needed the space.

He hated more that the Force had always been the thing that made space feel safe.

Ben closed his eyes.

The old cadence came anyway, a reflex layered into years of training. There is peace. There is purpose. There is the breath between—

The words hit a blank wall inside him.

Not because he had forgotten them. Because they no longer arrived with the certainty they used to carry. He could say them. He could believe them. Nothing in the de-structured field pushed back or confirmed.

Without that pressure, they sounded like statements made in a dark room to keep from being afraid of the dark.

He opened his eyes fast.

Kael noticed. “That bad?”

Ben looked at him and almost said no.

The lie sat ready, neat and useful. He could sell it. He had spent most of his life being excellent at looking more sure than he was. The reflex rose, polished and familiar.

Then he saw Desh staring at the chrono, lips pressed thin, as if he were measuring an invisible slope. Sera with her rigid stillness, using discipline like a brace. Torin’s concern, too broad to be tactical. Kael’s watchfulness, which wasn’t indifference at all, just caution dressed for comfort.

None of them had a handrail.

Ben let the lie fall.

“It’s worse than I expected,” he said.

Sera nodded once, as though that confirmed her worst assumptions. “Can you still use the Force?”

Ben looked at the trees. At the flowers under shadow. At the sky above the settlement, blue and harmless and wrong. “Yes.”

The word came out clipped.

He could use it. That was the terrible part. He could still draw on power. He could still move, sense, reach. But the structure that told him what he was touching, what it meant, what to do with it—gone. Pulled away. Not destroyed. Not silenced.

Stripped.

The thought made his skin crawl in a way he hadn’t expected. Not because it was alarming on its own. Because it was clean. The sort of clean a blade had after a careful wipe.

Veraine had done this on purpose.

A field test, Desh had called it. A test.

Ben stared out at the settlement and tried to imagine this scale spread wide, tried to imagine the corridor worlds, the older nodes, Veranthos, all of them touched by this same absence of structure. Not death. Worse in some ways. A world where every living thing kept moving and had no internal rail to catch on.

His stomach turned.

Sera was speaking again, though he barely caught the first part. “—if the effect is stable, we need to document the radius and determine whether it’s expanding.”

“Stable?” Kael said. “That implies a lot of optimism.”

“Do you have a better term?”

He gave a one-shouldered shrug. “Catastrophic usually covers most of my day.”

Torin huffed once, the sound half laugh, half exhale, and for a second the team around Ben looked almost familiar. A bad day, a strange world, a mission that might go sideways. They had handled those before.

This was not that.

Ben looked down at his own hands. They were steady. No tremor. No visible sign that the world had stopped offering him the same old map.

His mind kept going anyway. It did not wait for the Force to smooth the edge off the worst ideas, the dangerous rush of them, the easy slide toward certainty that had saved him more than once and tempted him more than once too. The thoughts came naked, one after another.

This is what she wants.

This is what she thinks freedom looks like.

If this can happen here, it can happen anywhere.

If the Force can be pulled apart—

No.

The word came hard, internal and final.

Not if.

When.

That thought did not feel like prophecy. It felt like arithmetic.

Ben drew a breath and held it, not because he needed air but because his body had decided that holding on to something was better than floating. The settlement stood around him with its vines in the shade and its flowers turning toward darkness. The sky stayed blue. The birds did not come.

He could hear Desh speaking to Sera about drift values and timing windows. He could hear Torin answering in a low, steady voice. He could hear Kael, after a pause, say something dry enough to earn a muttered response from Desh.

The sounds were ordinary. Human. Useful.

Ben stood in the silence of the de-structured Force, hears himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts are his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softens.
