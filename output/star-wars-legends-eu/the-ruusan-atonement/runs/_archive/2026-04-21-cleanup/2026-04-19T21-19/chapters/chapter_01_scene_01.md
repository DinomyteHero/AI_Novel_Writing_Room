Rendell’s training blade clipped him across the shoulder, and Ben knew before the sting hit that he’d been late.

Not slow. Late. There was a difference, and he registered it in the half-second gap between what his body had set up and what the Force had given back—a rhythm he’d run ten thousand times coming in a fraction of a count behind, as if someone had tugged the tempo sideways without warning. His guard had been exactly where it belonged. His read had been right. The gap had slipped between them, and Rendell’s blade had taken the opening.

Third time this week.

“Good hit,” Ben said.

Rendell gave ground, breathing hard, training saber still live in his hand. He had the courtesy not to look surprised. Ben appreciated that.

“You all right? That shoulder—”

“Fine.” Ben thumbed off his blade. The training saber’s hum cut out, and the salle settled into a thinner quiet: ventilation cycling overhead, boots scuffing durasteel, the faint traffic of the Temple somewhere beyond the walls. “Long week. I’m calling it.”

“Rematch tomorrow?”

“Sure.” He wouldn’t be. “Get some sleep, Rendell.”

He kept his tone easy, professional. A Knight on an off night, nothing more. Rendell took it the way Jedi took most things—without pressing, without turning it into a discussion—collected his gear, and headed out. The door slid shut behind him with a soft hiss.

Ben stayed where he was and let the silence finish settling.

His palms were damp. His pulse was still up, and not from the sparring. Physically, he’d barely earned the sweat. The extra thud in his chest came from recognition, the same one that had been needling him since the week began: the gap wasn’t going away. He’d been tallying the misses without meaning to. The meditation stumble on Praxeum night. The reach for a door panel that had felt like pushing through water. The spar with Rendell three days ago, where he’d overcorrected and blamed distraction. Tonight.

Four.

Five, if he counted the half-second stall in the solo drill on the first morning, which he’d very reasonably decided not to count.

He was counting it now.

The salle stood empty, late-shift foot traffic reduced to a muffled thread beyond the corridor. Somewhere down the hall a cleaning droid hummed through its patient route. Ben set his training saber back on the rack, then walked to the center of the floor.

Then he lifted his hands around an unignited hilt and started through Shii-Cho Form I.

Not because he needed practice. He’d been running Form I since he was eight; his body knew it the way it knew breathing. He did it now because the sequence was simple enough to strip things down. If the lag sat in his muscles, it would show in the motion. If it lived in his Force-sense, he’d feel the field arrive out of step with what his body expected. One of them was betraying him. This would tell him which.

He moved through the opening sequence. His body was fine. The motions came clean and automatic, footwork landing exactly where it should. He let his awareness reach the way it always did when he ran forms, brushing into the salle’s presence, the familiar living note of a room where Jedi had trained for generations.

He waited.

The lag hit in the third sequence, right where it had been hitting all week—a half-beat behind, the field arriving a fraction late against his body’s expectation. Not absent. Not different in any way he could point to. Just late. Like a note played perfectly and still off the measure, the kind of wrongness that only sounded wrong if you knew how the piece was supposed to land.

Ben stopped in the middle of the sequence.

He stood in the center of the salle with his hands at his sides, breathing slowly, and didn’t bother reaching for an excuse.

He’d spent four days doing exactly that. Fatigue had been a solid option—he’d been running hard since the Abeloth aftermath, and the Order had been stretched thin, and nobody in the Temple was getting enough sleep. Residue had been even better, because it was true in the broad sense: the Force had been unsettled since Abeloth, since Centerpoint, since any of the last crises that had left marks on the field the way a deep bruise lingered in muscle. He’d leaned on that explanation the most. It let him admit something was off while keeping the source safely in recent history, where it was survivable, understandable, and eventually temporary.

Except it hadn’t faded. It had been there during meditation this morning. It had been there in the spar. It was here now, in an empty salle, with no fatigue worth the name and no witness he needed to look competent for.

He reached again, carefully this time, the way you pressed a bruise to find its edges. The field answered. He felt the room. He felt the distant presence of other Jedi spread through the Temple. He felt the churn of the city-planet beyond the walls, Coruscant never sleeping, not really. Everything was there.

Everything landed a half-beat late.

Not him. He knew that now, with the kind of certainty that hit his chest before his mind had anything useful to say about it. He knew what his own misreads felt like—the overreach, the static, the particular warp grief had put into his perception in years he didn’t especially enjoy revisiting. This wasn’t that. This sat outside him. Consistent. Coming from somewhere, somehow, in a direction he couldn’t yet turn toward.

His hands weren’t steady. He noticed that without comment.

Four days of pinning it on himself, on lingering damage, on his own tendency to hear trouble where everyone else heard aftermath. Four days of deciding the gap was his problem and therefore manageable, if he kept his mouth shut and waited it out. The relief of being wrong about that hit fast and hard and did absolutely nothing to improve the alternative. He wasn’t broken. Good. Excellent. Wonderful.

Something was wrong with the Force.

He didn’t ignite the training saber again.

He was still standing there—breathing, thinking, not getting very far with either—when the door slid open.

Luke appeared in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
