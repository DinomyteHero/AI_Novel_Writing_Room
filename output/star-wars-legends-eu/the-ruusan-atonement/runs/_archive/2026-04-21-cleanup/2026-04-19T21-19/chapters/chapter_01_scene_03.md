The preflight checklist finished green across the board, and Ben settled the datapad against his knee.

The Temple hangar was loud the way it always was at this hour—service droids making their circuits, a tech crew pulling inspection panels off the freighter two bays over, the low, chest-deep pulse of some ship farther back in the cavern warming its engines. The air tasted recycled and faintly metallic. Hot durasteel, machine lubricant, a trace of old ozone soaked into the deck plates. Ben had logged enough hours in enough hangars that it barely rose to thought anymore. It was simply the texture of leaving.

He looked down at the datapad.

The Jedi partner roster sat one tap from the top of the screen. He had opened it twice already and backed out both times without scrolling. The names were fine. Better than fine. Two had recent Outer Rim experience; one had worked a Force-anomaly survey the year before Abeloth.

His thumb slid past the roster icon and opened his personal contacts instead.

Desh Rolan. Three entries down, between a fuel broker on Nar Shaddaa and a Jedi archivist Ben had never actually needed. He pressed the name before the rest of his brain caught up.

The comm connected on the third pulse. Long-range lag—maybe a second and a half—and then:

"Ben Skywalker." Desh's voice crackled through the speaker, tinny with distance, warm underneath it. Dry as old leather. "I was wondering when you'd remember I existed."

Ben leaned back into the pilot's seat. "I need a second body. Outer Rim, survey mission, leave window is forty-eight hours." He checked the chrono on reflex. "Thirty-six, actually."

"Generous of you to narrow it down." A beat. "What's the work?"

"Force anomaly investigation. You'd handle logistics, ground support, non-Force situational reading." Ben kept his tone level, practical, the same voice he'd use to file a supply request. "It's not a combat assignment. Probably."

"Probably," Desh said.

"Probably."

Another pause. Ben caught noise behind Desh's voice: a door hiss, distant metal clatter, the indistinct life of somewhere that wasn't a ship and wasn't the Temple. Civilian space. He didn't ask.

"I'll be at the south bay in two hours," Desh said. "You're buying the ration packs."

The channel stayed open a second longer, quiet on both ends, and Ben knew that silence. He had known it years ago, when they were both younger and the GAG patch on their shoulders had meant something different. Desh had never needed the reasons spelled out. Back then that had been efficiency. Now it was something harder to name.

"South bay," Ben said. "Two hours."

He closed the channel.

Hangar noise rushed back in—the droid circuits, the tech crew, the deep thrum of the freighter two bays over. Ben set the datapad on the copilot's seat and brushed his thumb over the activation stud of his lightsaber without igniting it. The hilt sat cool and familiar beneath his palm. He left his hand there a moment longer.

Then he ran through the departure sequence.

---

Desh arrived with ninety seconds to spare, a duffel over one shoulder and a travel mug in hand, and offered nothing beyond a nod as he stowed his gear and folded into the copilot's seat. He glanced at the nav display, took in the route, then looked at Ben.

"Outer Rim," he said.

"Outer Rim," Ben said.

Desh drank his caf.

The ship lifted cleanly, rising through the Temple hangar's upper bay on repulsors as the doors cycled open ahead of them. Coruscant's light washed across the canopy—orange-gray, the color of perpetual city-day—with skylane traffic threading past in ordered streams above and below. Ben tipped the nose toward the upper atmosphere and let the autopilot handle the climb.

The wrongness was still there. It had ridden with him through preflight, through the comm call, through Desh's arrival, the duffel stowed, the departure checklist. It lingered at the edge of his attention like a note played slightly flat—not painful, not sharp, just off, the way a hatch that never quite sealed stayed in your head every time you passed it.

Coruscant dropped away beneath them. The curve of the city-planet edged into view at the bottom of the canopy, the terminator line cutting day from night, and then they were through the upper atmosphere and into black, with the skylanes thinning behind them to nothing.

Desh was studying the nav display. "You want to tell me what we're actually going into?"

"Seventeen flagged anomaly reports along a corridor in the Outer Rim. Force-field disruption. No confirmed source." Ben kept his eyes on the instruments. "Pattern suggests it's localized instead of systemic, but the reports are getting denser."

"Getting denser," Desh repeated. "Meaning it's spreading."

"Meaning more people are noticing it."

Desh turned that over the way he always did—not arguing, just testing the balance. "And the Jedi Order is handling this by sending one Knight and a civilian with a travel mug."

"The Jedi Order is handling it by conducting a survey." Ben flicked him a glance. "You're the logistics support."

"Right." Desh lifted the mug a fraction. "Well-equipped."

Ben laid in the course for the first survey coordinate and let the nav computer chew through the calculations. Jump window in four minutes. He ran the pre-jump checks by hand instead of letting the ship handle them—old habit, less distrust of the instruments than a need to keep his hands busy.

The wrongness was still there. Faint. Directional, the way it had been directional since the training salle, since the sparring match, since the morning he'd stopped blaming himself for it. He was used to it now, the way you got used to a sound that never stopped as long as you never listened too closely.

"Jump in three," the nav computer said.

"Jump in three," Ben confirmed.

Desh strapped in without comment.

The stars stretched.

The hyperdrive engaged with a deep bass shudder. The canopy flashed white-blue, and then hyperspace opened around them—the familiar rush of light-lines, the feeling of the galaxy folding into a corridor. Ben's instruments flickered once, a quick stab of instability, then steadied. The nav computer rolled over to elapsed time. Everything nominal.

He reached into the Force, checking the field the way a pilot checked altitude—reflex, not thought.

The wrongness was still there.

Not faint now. Not ambient pressure, not that sour edge he could almost write off as fatigue. The jump should have broken it. Hyperspace transit scrambled most Force-field distortions, washed them smooth the way clear water left turbulence behind. It wasn't supposed to survive the transition.

This did.

And as Ben let his attention settle on it—fully, without skirting past—it changed. No longer a disruption. A direction. A bearing. Not here but there, specific and insistent, pulling along the exact heading the nav computer had already set.

The instruments held steady. Desh was looking down at his datapad, unbothered. The ship was fine.

Ben went very still in the pilot's seat as the stars streamed past, and the wrongness in the Force shifted as he left Coruscant—not diminishing but acquiring direction, like a compass needle settling.
