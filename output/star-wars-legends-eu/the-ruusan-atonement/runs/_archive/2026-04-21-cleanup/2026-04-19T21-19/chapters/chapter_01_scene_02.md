The datapad sat squarely in the middle of Luke's desk, its blue display already lit, and Ben knew his father's expression before he stepped all the way into the office.

Mission-ready. Decision made.

"Close the door," Luke said.

Ben did. The corridor noise cut off at once, leaving the room quieter without ever reaching silence. Coruscant still pressed at the window, a steady wash of traffic muted by seventeen stories of durasteel and distance. The holocomm on the desk was dark. Luke's chair sat pushed back a little, the stance of someone who had spent too long with a problem and had finally chosen an answer.

Luke slid the datapad across the desk.

Ben picked it up. Seventeen flagged entries. Every one marked with the same anomaly notation—a symbol he didn't recognize, which meant either new or buried in a classification tier he'd never had reason to touch. Survey routes. Outer Rim. The timestamps stretched back six weeks. The earliest entries were thin, cautious. The newest had grown dense with secondary notes. Someone had been watching this for a while.

"Survey entries," Ben said.

"Flagged by three different relay stations over the past month." Luke's tone stayed level. "The notation is ours. I wrote it when the fourth came in. Look at the distribution."

Ben scrolled. The entries lay scattered along a corridor of space running from the Mid Rim outward, each one marking a localized Force anomaly. Not identical readings—Force-sensitive teams never described the same thing the same way—but the shape under the language held. A wrongness in the field. A disruption that matched nothing Ben knew.

He zoomed in on three entries. The most recent ones. The clearest.

His grip tightened on the device.

These three he knew. Not the coordinates, not the crews—the description. The phrases the teams had reached for, the note of bafflement threaded through the notation, the way each entry had edged up to the mouth of malfunction and stopped short. He knew what they had tried to say because he'd been trying to say it to himself for four days and had come up just as empty.

Luke watched him work without speaking.

Ben set the datapad down between them. His shoulders loosened by a fraction—not enough to call it ease, just the release of someone who had been braced for disbelief and hadn't found it.

"How long?" he asked.

"The first report was six weeks ago. My own read—" Luke paused, brief and exact, not uncertain. "Eleven days."

Eleven days. Ben had been carrying it for four, and he'd spent most of that time finding reasons it belonged to him alone. Luke had eleven days and a mission file to show for it.

"You've had this on your desk for eleven days."

"I've had a question on my desk for eleven days. The entries gave it shape." Luke leaned forward, forearms near the desk's edge. "I've felt something in the field. Recent. Directional. It originates in the Outer Rim—I can't narrow it beyond a broad bearing from here, but the pattern confirms it." His gaze flicked to the datapad. "You recognized three of those entries."

Not a question.

"Yes," Ben said.

"Describe it."

Ben's eyes went to the window first—the moving strings of skylane traffic against Coruscant's gray-orange dayglow—then back to his father. Luke's presence in the Force was warm and close, as familiar as it had ever been. Under that, faint and stubborn, the wrongness sat in the room like something gone sour in recycled air. It had come up from the training level with him. It was here too.

"A lag," he said. "Half a beat behind where it should be. The field answers, but late. Like the response is coming from farther away than it should." He stopped there, then added, "You described it as directional."

"It has an origin point. Or the nearest thing to one I can place from this distance."

"The route runs along the bearing."

Luke neither confirmed nor denied it. He picked up the datapad, turned the display toward Ben, and let the distribution map speak for itself. The corridor stretched outward in a narrow arc. Ben traced it automatically, pilot's instinct taking the route apart and putting it back together again—the sequencing was clean, efficient, a practical path through every flagged coordinate. It also pointed, as clearly as a nav arrow, toward the deep Outer Rim.

Not a routine assignment. A vector.

"I want a Jedi Knight on the ground," Luke said. "Someone who can read what the teams are reporting and tell me what they're actually seeing." He set the datapad down again. "You're the right person for this."

Ben heard the register beneath the words. Careful. Warm, but formal around the edges. The voice of someone who had felt something new and was still fitting it into known shapes, building Order procedure around a private unease. Eleven days old. To Luke it was still an anomaly in the field: directional, troubling, enough to justify a mission.

To Ben it was the thing that had been pulling at him since the first morning, the slip that had cost him three sparring matches, the disruption he'd spent four days trying to pin on himself. Luke was reading it from the outside. Ben knew the texture of living inside it.

He didn't say any of that.

What he said was, "When do I leave?"

"Forty-eight hours. I'll have the full packet on your terminal before morning."

Ben nodded and crossed to the window. Below, the skylane traffic moved in steady streams—freight haulers, commuter transports, the endless business of a city-planet that had no idea anything was wrong with the Force. He watched it for a moment. The wrongness lived here too, faint beneath the Temple's usual harmony, and in his mind the route ran outward from this room like a heading laid over a chart.

Somewhere along that bearing, something was wrong. Maybe for longer than eleven days. Longer than four. Long enough to leave tracks in the entries. Long enough to have direction.

He turned back from the window. Luke remained at the desk, watching with that patient attention Ben had known all his life—the look that said I see you and never demanded anything back.

"I'll be ready," Ben said.

"I know you will."

For a moment neither of them moved. Coruscant murmured beyond the glass. Somewhere down in the Temple, a training salle was probably in use, a lightsaber humming through forms in a field that answered on time.

Ben picked up the datapad and tucked it under his arm.

He didn't suggest a partner. Luke didn't offer one. The silence between them held the rest, and both of them let it stay there.
