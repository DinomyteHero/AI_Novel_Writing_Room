The training blade slapped across his shoulder, and Ben was already stepping back, already too late to save the exchange.

Third time this week. Same half-beat. Same lag between intention and the Force answering, like reaching for a handhold in the dark and finding it a finger-width lower than his grip expected.

“Good hit,” he said.

His sparring partner—Reyan, two years a Knight, careful enough to make you pay for every lazy transition—lowered his blade. “You telegraphed the counter.”

“I know.” Ben thumbed off his training saber. The hum cut out, leaving the salle quieter: ventilation whispering through the walls, boots ringing faintly on durasteel somewhere out in the corridor. “I’m done for tonight.”

Reyan studied him for a second—not suspicious, just checking the read—and then let it go. “Early shift tomorrow anyway.” He clipped his saber to his belt and headed for the door. That was the thing about Jedi who’d trained long enough. They knew when not to ask.

Ben waited until the door shut.

Then he stood in the center of the salle and breathed. Standard Temple issue: high ceiling, scarred floor panels, recycled air carrying the stale ghost of a thousand sparring sessions. At this hour the corridor traffic had thinned to the late shift, muffled and uneven. Somewhere down the hall, a cleaning droid bumped along a baseboard with patient determination. Normal. Ordinary. The Temple at eleven at night, the same as it had been for years.

He ignited his training saber.

No partner. No drill. No objective. Just the opening movement of Shien, slow and exact. His body knew it the way it knew breathing—muscle memory laid down before he was twenty, sharpened since until it asked nothing conscious of him. Left foot forward. Weight transfer. The emitter’s hum climbed as the training blade rose from low guard into diagonal extension.

The Force came in late.

Not by much. Not by anything Reyan would have spotted, or anyone watching from the wall. Just the same half-beat lag between physical intent and the ambient field meeting it, a slight wrongness in the way the world received him. His body moved cleanly. His body had always moved cleanly. The problem wasn’t his body.

He lowered the saber and started again.

Same thing on the second pass. Same on the third. He ran the full sequence twice, then a third time, tracking not his footwork or angles but the place where the Force ought to have met him and didn’t—or didn’t quite, didn’t fully, arrived just behind the beat like a musician who’d slipped tempo without noticing.

A cleaning droid chirped in the corridor. Lift chimes sounded somewhere above. The Temple kept going about its ordinary late-evening business while Ben Skywalker stood alone in an empty salle and tried to decide whether he was losing his mind.

He was not losing his mind.

That was the answer his body kept giving him, which was a problem all by itself. If he were exhausted, his technique would advertise it—the tiny tremors, the shortened reach, the way fatigue took depth out of a guard. None of that was there. His form was clean. His breathing stayed even. He’d slept well enough, eaten properly, done all the sensible-Knight things a sensible Knight was supposed to do when the galaxy handed him a rough stretch and expected him to keep moving.

The wrongness was not in him.

On the seventh pass he stopped mid-form, saber raised at the transition between diagonal extension and overhead parry—the point Reyan had caught him, the point where the lag always showed itself most clearly—and held there. Motionless. Waiting.

There.

Just under sound, under sensation, the ambient field answered a half-beat behind his intention. Not flat. Not absent. Just slightly, persistently wrong, the way a room sounds off when one wall is hollow and you still can’t tell which one.

He deactivated the saber.

The hum died again. The salle settled into silence, into recycled air and the distant strike of boots in the corridor, and Ben finally let himself think the thought he’d been refusing to think for a week.

This wasn’t aftermath. The Order had been treating the background disturbance as residue—Abeloth’s ripples still settling, old wounds in the Force closing slowly, nothing that needed immediate action. He’d told himself the same thing. He’d gotten very good at telling himself the same thing. He was his mother’s son that way: if you could classify a problem as manageable, you could keep moving until it stopped being manageable, and then you dealt with it.

The problem had stopped being manageable somewhere around the third time it cost him a transition he’d executed ten thousand times without thinking.

He clipped the training saber to his belt. His shoulder throbbed where Reyan had tagged him—not badly, just enough to keep reminding him. He’d been slow. He was going to keep being slow until he figured out why the Force kept arriving wrong, and he was not going to figure it out by standing in the salle and pretending he’d just had a hard week.

The wrongness had a direction, he realized. Had been realizing, really, without letting himself finish the thought—the way you know where a sound is coming from before you consciously register it as sound. Not Coruscant. Not anywhere in the Core. Something farther out, deep and distant, tugging at the edge of the ambient field the way a current pulls at still water. He didn’t know what it was. He had no name for it, no location, nothing that would survive thirty seconds in a mission briefing.

He knew it was there. He knew it was getting worse.

That was enough to make it real.

His shoulders eased down a fraction, the tension he’d carried all week settling into something heavier and more honest. All right. Fine. The week had not simply been hard, and the wrongness was not residual, and he was not going to sleep this off and run cleaner forms tomorrow. Something was wrong with the Force itself—not with him, not with his perception, not with the aftermath of anything he’d survived—and it had a direction, and he was going to have to tell someone.

He was still deciding who when the salle door opened.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
