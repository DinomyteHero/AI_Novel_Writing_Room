The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Third time this week. Same half-second gap, same missed read on the attack angle—and the same wrong note sounding in the Force a beat before impact, too late to do anything useful.

His sparring partner dropped out of the follow-through stance and tilted her head. She hadn't gloated after the first two, which Ben appreciated, but the question in her posture was harder to ignore each time.

"I'm done." He deactivated his training saber. The hum died. "Shoulder's been tight all day."

She accepted this with a nod and started gathering her things from the bench. Ben kept his expression neutral while she crossed the salle, exchanged a brief word with the Knight coming in for the next session, and disappeared into the corridor. The door hissed shut.

He stood in the middle of the floor and let out a breath.

The salle was empty now except for him. Coruscant's nightside pressed against the high windows, the city's light bleeding through in orange-grey bands. The room smelled of sweat and the faint metallic bite of recycled air. His shoulder ached where the blade had landed—not seriously, just enough to confirm he'd been hit.

That wasn't the problem. The problem was why.

Ben crossed to the far wall and back again, his boots loud in the quiet. He ran the sequence in his head: her feint to his left, the real strike coming high on the right, his guard already moving to compensate—and then the note. Low and off-pitch, like a string pulled slightly too far from true. His concentration had fractured for a fraction of a second, and that fraction was all she'd needed.

He'd felt it twice before this week. Once during a routine Force-assisted jump that had gone six centimeters shorter than his body knew how to make it. Once during a simple object levitation exercise that had stuttered mid-lift, the object dropping and bouncing off the floor while the Knight supervising the initiates had looked at him with careful professional blankness.

The Masters had a theory. Post-Abeloth disturbance, residual turbulence from the Maw events, the Force settling back into its natural channels after catastrophic disruption. Give it time. Meditate. Log the anomalies if they persisted.

Ben had logged three weeks of anomalies.

He picked up his unlit training saber and moved into Shien's opening stance—not to practice the form, but to test the gap between what his body knew and what his Force perception was doing. He moved through the first sequence. Clean. His muscles remembered every degree of every angle, the weight distribution, the pivot points. He transitioned to Djem So. Clean again. His body had no complaints. Seven years of drilling had put these movements somewhere below conscious thought.

He held position at the peak of the third sequence and reached for the Force.

There it was.

Not wrong in the way Jacen had been wrong—that had been a wound with a specific shape, a darkness with edges and intention Ben had learned to recognize. Not wrong the way Abeloth had been wrong, which was less like a wound and more like a door left open onto something that had never been meant to exist. This was different. Quieter. It sat underneath everything else the way a bad bearing sits inside an engine—you don't hear it until you're listening for it, and once you hear it, you can't hear anything else.

He lowered the training saber.

The Masters weren't wrong that the Force had been turbulent. But turbulence was weather. This was geology.

He'd spent enough time around Jacen in the years before Centerpoint—before he'd understood what Jacen was becoming—to know what it felt like when something was wrong at a level deeper than emotion or intent. He'd spent enough time in the aftermath of Abeloth to know the difference between a Force-user making bad choices and something wrong with the Force itself. This had that second quality. Not a presence. Not a will. Just a structural catch, like a catch in a blade's balance, like a note played slightly flat on an instrument that had been perfectly tuned yesterday.

He moved through the rest of the sequence without stopping. His body was fine. His body was exactly where it was supposed to be. The wrongness wasn't in him.

That should have been reassuring.

He finished the form and stood still, listening to his own breathing slow. The Temple's presence pressed around him—dozens of Jedi, the deep familiar weight of the building itself, generations of practice soaked into the stone. He'd grown up feeling that presence as a kind of background warmth. Tonight it felt like trying to hear a conversation through a wall that had developed a crack.

The other Knights weren't logging anything unusual. He'd asked, carefully, in the way that didn't invite follow-up questions. Background noise. Residual disturbance. Give it time.

Maybe they were right. Maybe his years with Jacen and then the Abeloth crisis had recalibrated his sensitivity in a way that made him read signal in static. Trauma had a way of teaching the nervous system that the worst-case interpretation was the correct one.

Except his nervous system had been right before. That was the problem.

Ben set the training saber on the bench and pressed his palm flat against the cool durasteel wall. He wasn't going to log this one. Three weeks of logs had produced nothing except a notation in his file that he was experiencing post-crisis perceptual adjustment and had been encouraged to continue his meditation practice. Which he was doing. Which wasn't helping. Because meditation required him to sit quietly with the wrongness and breathe through it, and the wrongness wasn't the kind of thing that responded to breathing.

He needed a problem he could actually solve.

Boots in the corridor.

He turned.

His father stood in the doorway, a datapad in one hand, wearing the expression that meant he'd already read whatever was on it twice and had a destination in mind.
