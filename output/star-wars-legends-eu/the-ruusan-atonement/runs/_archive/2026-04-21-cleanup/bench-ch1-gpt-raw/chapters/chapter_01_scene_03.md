Ben strapped in, checked the last green status light on the board, and picked up his comlink.

The Temple hangar breathed around him through the cockpit hull: bootsteps on duracrete, a loader repulsor whining somewhere off to port, service-tech chatter flattened into muffled syllables. Recycled air carried oil, hot circuitry, and the faint metal chill of the bay doors.

His thumb hovered over the contact menu.

The roster sat where it was supposed to, clean and official. Three Knights near the top, all competent, all available on short notice if Luke's office had pushed the alert through the right channels. Ben scrolled past them without opening any of them and flipped to personal contacts.

The motion was too smooth.

He stopped on Desh Rolan's code and looked at it for a beat, which was already more hesitation than the choice had earned. Then he keyed the call.

Static hissed. A relay chirped. The signal stretched long-range across a portion of Coruscant large enough to make most people feel properly insignificant.

Desh answered on the fourth pulse. “If this is a social call, your timing is offensive.”

Ben smiled before he could stop it. “Good. I was aiming for professional.”

“Worse. How bad?”

“Outer Rim run. Leave now if you can.”

A short pause. Ben could picture Desh using it to stand up, collect whatever mattered, and start moving before the conversation had finished. Old habits from older work. The kind that stayed with a man after the badge went away.

“Quiet or legal?” Desh asked.

“Both, ideally.”

“Ah. The expensive package.”

“I'll note that on your invoice.”

“You never pay my invoices.”

“I respect them. From a distance.”

Desh made a sound that might have been a laugh under the static. “Send the pad. Pickup?”

“Temple hangar.”

“Thought so.”

That was all. No why him, no why not a Knight, no polite pass at pretending the roster had not existed. Just acceptance, flat and practical, with the shape of the missing question sitting plainly between them.

Ben leaned back a fraction in the pilot's seat. The pull across his chest eased before he could stop it. Useful information. Mildly insulting, too.

“Desh,” he said.

“Mm.”

“Thanks.”

“You're welcome. Try not to make that sound ominous. I'm dressing.”

The line clicked dead.

Ben lowered the comlink and stared at the darkened display for a moment. Then he snorted once under his breath, keyed the mission packet for transfer, and sent it.

They had both been younger when the Guard had put them in the same stream of dirty jobs and called it necessity. Ben had been a teenager in borrowed authority; Desh had been old enough to know better and practical enough to show up anyway. The institution was gone. The shorthand had survived it.

He set the comlink aside and brought the startup sequence back to the main board. No point admiring his own habits when the craft was already asking for clearance.

His hands moved over the controls with practiced economy. Repulsors live. Vector display up. Traffic queue from the Temple spire feeding into the lower ascent lanes. He eased the craft off the deck and felt the familiar lightness as the landing gear cleared.

Outside the cockpit, the hangar opened in slices: floor markings sliding back, stacked crates at the bay edge, maintenance lights washing the hull of a freighter two berths down. A pair of deck crew crossed well clear of his path without looking at him. Temple departures happened every hour. A Knight lifting on assignment did not rate drama.

Fine by him.

He brought the nose out and fed power in. The hum under his boots deepened. The bay mouth widened ahead, framing evening Coruscant in hard angles and streaming traffic.

For a few breaths there was only flying.

The craft slipped from the Temple into layered air lanes, banked between a courier van and a public shuttle, and climbed. Towers fell away in mirrored walls and strips of light. Somewhere below, civilian traffic kept doing what civilian traffic always did on Coruscant: hurrying with conviction toward problems Ben would never hear about unless they grew large enough to become policy.

His board muttered course confirmations. A lane beacon flashed amber and rolled to green. He adjusted heading, nudged speed, and let procedure take over where thought might otherwise have tried to get decorative.

The wrongness stayed with him.

It had been riding the edge of his attention all evening, thin and sour and out of step with everything else. Climbing toward orbit, he waited for the usual wash of motion and task-load to smear it into the background.

It didn't.

Ben's fingers tightened on the yoke by a fraction. He cut through the next traffic layer, cleared the planet's denser lanes, and let the craft accelerate harder. Coruscant spread beneath him now, citylight wrapped over curvature, all that metal and life and heat turning slowly under the dark.

He should have called somebody from the roster. On paper, certainly. On paper, a Jedi partner made the whole thing neater. Better chain of command, better mission optics, fewer questions if he had to send a report that sounded strange even by the Order's standards.

Instead he had called Desh, and the relief of hearing his voice had arrived so fast it was practically an admission.

Ben exhaled through his nose. Useful. Not flattering, but useful.

The terminal vector chimed ready. He keyed the jump solution Luke had handed him, checked the lane again, and gave the navicomputer final commit.

Stars held steady ahead. Controls warm under his palms. Systems stable. The wrongness still there, lingering just out of clear thought like a word at the edge of memory.

“Let's see what you've got,” he muttered, not entirely to the craft.

He pushed into hyperspace.

Starlines snapped long and vanished into the blue-white tunnel. The hull gave its usual brief complaint. Across the main board, instrument lights flickered once in a quick spill of color and settled back before the readouts could mean anything. Hyperdrive status held clean.

Ben glanced over the board anyway, chasing the mechanical answer on reflex. No power dip worth naming. No fault cascade. No sensor surge. Craft sound.

And the wrongness was still there.

That hit harder than the flicker. Hyperspace usually put distance between a Jedi and everything external. Not silence, exactly, but a muffling. The galaxy moved to another room and shut the door.

This did not.

Ben went very still.

The sensation sharpened. Not louder. Not stronger in any simple way. It stopped being everywhere at once and leaned. His body answered before thought did, a minute turn inside his chest, the instinctive correction of balance when a deck shifted underfoot.

He looked at the vector line on the nav display.

The pull matched it.

His hand moved to the instruments again, then stopped above the board. Nothing on the screens showed what he was tracking. The course was the course. The hyperdrive was the hyperdrive. The rest of it lived where no dial could touch it.

For a moment he sat with both hands braced on the controls, listening to the steady craft-noise and the thinner silence under it. Desh was on his way to meet him farther along this line. Luke's assignment sat in the data packet, official and tidy. Ben had told himself that was what this was: a mission with a deniable pilot and a practical reason for every choice.

The pull took another fraction of shape, aligning cleanly down-course.

Not random, then. Not ambient. Not something he happened to be carrying with him while he did his job.

Something was drawing him.

His mouth went dry. He swallowed, eyes on the glowing route line, and felt the last of his neat operational framing fold in on itself without the courtesy of an argument. He had chosen the call, chosen the partner, chosen the clean version that left room for no explanation because some part of him had already been following this.

The wrongness did not fade with distance from Coruscant. It settled onto a bearing and held there, fixed and undeniable, like a compass needle finding north.
