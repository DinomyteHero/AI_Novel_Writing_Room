Luke was already behind his desk when Ben stepped in. He tapped the door panel shut with two fingers, then slid a datapad across the polished surface.

“Take a look at these.”

The pad stopped in front of Ben with its display active, blue light washing over the grain of the desk. Seventeen entries marched down the screen, each tagged with the same notation. Not standard survey coding. Luke’s addition. Ben kept his face still before he touched the screen.

Coruscant traffic muttered through the office window behind Luke, a low, endless wash under the Temple’s quieter mechanical breath. Inside, the usual steadiness of the place held around old stone, recycled air, and the faint heat of electronics.

Under that sat the wrongness. Thin. Sour. It hung in the room even here.

Ben picked up the datapad and scrolled.

Coordinates. Timestamps. Survey team designations. Brief notes in the clipped language Jedi used when they wanted to make the uncooperative fit a report format. A few of them circled the edges of what he had been trying not to name for days.

Field response delay.

Localized desynchronization during meditation.

Directional distortion inconsistent with post-event residue.

His pulse eased by a notch he had not known it had climbed. Not imagination, then. Not fatigue. Not him making a private problem large enough to trip over in public.

“Seventeen?” he asked.

“Over six weeks,” Luke said. “Flagged by relay stations, then rerouted once the pattern started to repeat.”

Ben enlarged the first report, then the second. Most were close. Not exact. Different planets, different teams, different vocabularies layered over the same bad read. He kept scrolling.

On the third row from the bottom, his thumb stopped.

He opened that report and read it through. Then again.

Noting ambient lag relative to action intent. Bearing remains stable through repositioning.

The back of his neck tightened. He flicked to the next entry. Another team, farther out along the same broad line.

Perception suggests external drag rather than user-side impairment.

His shoulders loosened anyway, which was irritating but apparently honest. He had spent the morning doing a fair impression of a man carrying his own dignity in a damaged crate. It turned out shared evidence improved the packaging.

“Three,” he said.

Luke folded his hands. “Three what?”

Ben tapped the screen and angled it slightly. “These. This one, this one, and this.” He moved between them in quick sequence. “The wording’s different, but it’s the same thing.”

Luke leaned forward enough to read, though Ben suspected he already knew exactly which entries would catch him. “What makes them the same?”

Ben looked at the note again. He could have offered a cleaner answer if this were a debrief and he had another hour to pretend language was the problem. Instead he went with the truth available.

“It keeps arriving behind movement.” He tapped the second report. “This team almost says it. This one does, if you translate from meditation-speak.” He glanced up. “And it holds a bearing.”

Luke gave a small nod. Not approval. Recognition.

Ben set the datapad back on the desk between them. The motion came out more careful than he intended. Relief was one thing. Relief with witnesses had edges.

Luke sat back in his chair. The office light caught silver at his temples, and for a second Ben had the unhelpful thought that the galaxy had spent decades trying to make his father into a symbol and had never once asked whether that sounded exhausting.

Probably yes.

“You’ve felt it longer than the reports cover,” Luke said.

Not a question. Ben let that pass for permission. “On and off.”

“How long is on and off?”

Since he was small enough to think oddities in the Force were probably a universal feature and adults simply had better manners about mentioning them. Since his mother had still been alive to give him the look that meant stop circling the problem and state it cleanly. Since Jacen had taught the family very thoroughly what happened when somebody’s private read went unquestioned for too long.

Ben kept his face still. “Long enough that I know those three aren’t false positives.”

Luke’s gaze rested on him for a moment, warm and steady and impossible to hide from if you had grown up with it. He did not push on the missing years. He reached out, turned the datapad back toward himself, and touched the notation marker on the corner of the display.

“I started getting these eleven days ago,” he said. “By the second pair I stopped trusting the standard classifications. This was simpler.”

Ben looked at the symbol. “Simple” was generous. “You invented a new category.”

“I borrowed an old one buried in a tier no one uses and made it work harder.”

“That sounds more like you.”

A corner of Luke’s mouth moved. “I’ll accept that as praise.”

“It was meant generously.”

“I’m relieved.”

There it was, light and brief, not enough to loosen the room but enough to remind Ben that Luke could still do this when he wanted to. A line, a breath of space, then back to the work.

Luke sobered. “It’s getting closer.”

Ben went still.

Luke did not dress the words up. He did not soften them into possibility or theory. “I’ve been tracking the pull for eleven days. Directional. Consistent. Nearer each morning.”

The office seemed to narrow around that. Not in any mystical sense. Just simple geometry: desk, chair, datapad, window, his father’s voice putting shape around the thing Ben had been living beside and making it real in a different register.

Dad, the private part of his mind said at once, startled and young and deeply unhelpful.

Grand Master Skywalker sat across from him and spoke as a Jedi to another Jedi. But beneath the formal cadence was something else, quiet and unmistakable. Not indulgence. Not concern disguised as oversight. Luke was telling him he had seen it too. That the wrongness Ben had carried for years was not some private defect in his own reading. For a brief, disorienting instant, the floor under the conversation leveled.

Ben had not expected that to hit so hard.

He looked down at the reports again, mostly to buy himself a second. The blue glow sharpened the tendons in his wrist. Outside the window, a distant siren rose and faded into Coruscant’s traffic stream.

“You should have told me sooner,” he said.

Luke’s brows lifted a fraction. “I wanted more than my own impression before I put it in front of you.”

Fair. Also annoying. Ben let both truths exist. “And now?”

“Now you have seventeen reports, three that match your read closely, and a bearing.” Luke keyed the display and a route line appeared over a star chart, stretching out from Coruscant toward the Outer Rim. “I want a survey mission run along this vector. Quietly. Officially. A Knight with discretion and a pilot who can get in and out of places without attracting attention.”

Ben followed the route with his eyes. The pull inside him answered at once, a pressure aligning with the line on the screen until the chart and the sensation sat on top of each other. Not a theory, then. A road.

He rose before he quite meant to and crossed to the office window. Traffic lights threaded between towers in red and white streams, speeders banking through lanes as if the whole planet had agreed on a shared impatience and built civilization around it. Far below, somewhere beyond the Temple’s clean heights, people were buying caf, missing meetings, arguing over docking fees, getting on with their day while the Force kept pointing at something out in the dark.

The glass was cool under his fingertips.

Behind him Luke said, “You’ll file under standard survey authority. Minimal profile. Relay updates through Temple channels.”

Ben watched a cargo hauler ease between two faster lanes with all the confidence of something too large to apologize. “Solo survey?”

“That’s the cleanest version.”

Ben almost smiled at the wording. Cleanest version. Not safest. Not simplest. The language of protocol doing quiet work for both of them.

He turned back. Luke had not moved much. He rarely needed to. His father had always had a gift for making stillness feel like a deliberate choice rather than waiting.

There were obvious things a more prudent Jedi might say here. Suggest a second Knight. Open the roster. Turn independent concern into proper team structure and let the Order distribute the load the way it was designed to. Ben knew all of that. He also knew the tight pull under his ribs that came with the thought of explaining this sensation to someone else while it was still slipping under language, and the colder truth under that: he did not want another Jedi between him and the read.

His mother would probably have had an opinion about how unattractive that sounded when stripped down to basics.

He came back to the desk and rested both palms on its edge. “I can leave within the hour.”

Luke studied him. No visible surprise. No visible argument. Just that same steady regard, carrying more than the room’s formal language allowed.

“You’ll need a pilot,” Luke said.

Ben gave a short nod. “I have someone in mind.”

Luke accepted that without asking for the name. He deactivated the star chart and slid the datapad back toward Ben for keeps this time.

“Then this is your assignment, Knight Skywalker. Survey the anomaly corridor, verify the source if possible, and report anything that changes the threat picture.”

Ben took the datapad. “Understood, Master Skywalker.”

The title sat between them exactly where it belonged. So did everything else neither of them said.

Ben inclined his head once. “I accept the mission.”

Luke did not ask about a partner. Ben did not offer one.
