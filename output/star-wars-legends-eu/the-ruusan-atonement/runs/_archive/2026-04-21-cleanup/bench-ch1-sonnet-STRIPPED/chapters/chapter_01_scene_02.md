Luke's office smelled like old flimsiplast and recycled air and something that had been burning in the small brazier on the corner shelf for as long as Ben could remember. Incense, maybe. He'd never asked.

"Close the door," Luke said.

Ben closed it. The office was functional in the way that only spaces belonging to people who didn't think about their spaces could be — stacks of datapads organized by some system Ben had never decoded, a holocomm unit on the desk that looked like it had survived three separate wars, a viewport showing Coruscant's nightside traffic in patient, ordered streams. Luke was already seated. The datapad on the desk between them glowed blue.

Ben sat across from him and waited.

"Seventeen reports," Luke said. "Flagged by survey teams operating along the Outer Rim corridor over the past eight weeks. Independent sources — three different survey rotations, two independent contractor teams, one monastery on Dxun that started filing Force-observation logs six weeks ago and hasn't stopped."

Ben picked up the datapad. The edge was cool against his fingers, and the wrongness was here too, faint and sour beneath the Temple's familiar harmonics, like a fruit that looked fine but had already turned. He scrolled.

The reports were dry reading. Survey notation, coordinate strings, standardized Force-sensitivity assessment scales. But the flagged readings clustered in a pattern he recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd before you've consciously registered why — not from the data itself but from the shape of the gap it described. Places where Force perception had gone briefly, horribly flat. Places where the feedback loop had dropped out for a fraction of a second and then returned, leaving the observer disoriented and unable to explain why.

Three of the readings matched his own logs exactly. Not approximately. Exactly.

He zoomed in on the third one. A survey team near Balmorra, six weeks ago, reporting a momentary disruption during a training exercise. The notation read: *anomalous interference, post-Abeloth residue suspected, no further incidents recorded.*

"Post-Abeloth residue," Ben said.

"That's what the teams are calling it."

"That's what I called it too." He set the datapad down. "For about a week."

Luke's expression didn't change, but his presence in the Force shifted — a slight turn, the way a large, warm thing moves when it directs its full attention toward you. Luke's presence had always felt like that to Ben. Vast and familiar and occasionally overwhelming in the way that standing too close to a heat source could be overwhelming, even when the heat was benign.

"You've been tracking it independently," Luke said. It wasn't a question.

"I logged two incidents with Cilghal."

"She mentioned them."

"I didn't log the other nine." Ben watched his father's face. "Because the pattern didn't match residue. Residue has weight. This is the opposite — it's an absence. Something under the structure, not on top of it."

Luke was quiet for a moment. Outside, a transport lane shifted and the light through the viewport changed fractionally, painting a pale stripe across the desk between them.

"I feel it too," Luke said.

Ben's chest loosened. Dad, he thought, and then the thought was gone, filed away behind the part of him that was a Jedi Knight in a briefing with the Grand Master.

"Since when?"

"Ten weeks. I've been cross-referencing against every documented post-Abeloth signature we have." Luke's mouth pulled at the corner — not quite a smile. "I keep arriving at the same answer you did."

"Then you know it's not residue."

"I know it doesn't match residue profiles." Luke's voice was careful. Precise. "What I don't know is what it does match. Which is why I want a survey run along the corridor. Firsthand observation, Force-perception logs, physical sampling at the three highest-concentration sites." He nodded at the datapad. "The bearing runs almost exactly through the cluster."

Ben pulled the datapad back and looked at the route overlay. The corridor was a long sweep of Outer Rim space, sparse and poorly mapped, running through systems that showed up in historical records mostly as waypoints and nothing else. The flagged readings distributed themselves along it like beads on a string.

"You're not calling this a crisis," Ben said.

"I'm calling it a survey."

"Because if you call it a crisis, you have to tell the Council what you think it is, and you don't know yet."

Luke looked at him. "Yes."

That was the thing about his father, Ben thought — he didn't dissemble when you caught him at something. He just confirmed it and waited to see what you'd do with it.

"My read is that it's structural," Ben said. "Not emotional, not residue, not localized dark-side saturation. The way Jacen's experiments felt — not the specifics, but the category. Something that operates below the surface layer." He kept his voice level. "Abeloth's fractures felt different from this. So did Vestara's training techniques. I've got a decent library of what wrong feels like, and this is a new entry."

"I know." Luke said it simply, without qualification. "That's part of why I'm sending you."

Ben stood. The movement was just there, and he went with it, crossing to the viewport and putting his back to the desk. The city spread below him, indifferent and enormous. The wrongness pulsed once against his awareness, faint and off-key, and the cool transparency of the viewport did nothing to mute it.

"Survey mission," he said. "Three sites. Force-perception logs and physical sampling."

"And whatever else you find."

"Timeline?"

"Departure in forty-eight hours. The *Starhawk* is available — she's small enough for solo operation but has the sensor package you'll need."

Ben nodded, still facing the window. The traffic lanes below were orderly and patient, a million small lights going about their business. He thought about the survey team near Balmorra who had written *post-Abeloth residue suspected* and moved on. He thought about the monastery on Dxun that had been filing Force-observation logs for six weeks and hadn't stopped.

He thought about the shape of the gap in those three reports, identical to the gap he'd been cataloguing in his own perception for six weeks, and what it meant that the gap had a bearing and the bearing had a corridor and the corridor was waiting.

"Anything else?" he said.

"Check in at each site. Daily reports if the readings spike." Luke paused. "Ben."

He turned.

His father was watching him with an expression Ben didn't have a clean name for — something careful and warm and held at a specific distance, the way you hold something fragile that you've learned not to grip too tightly.

"Trust what you're perceiving," Luke said. "Your read on this is good."

Ben held his gaze for a beat. Two. "I'll send the first report from the jump point."

He picked up the datapad from the desk, tucked it under his arm, and walked to the door.

Neither of them said anything else. Ben didn't mention a partner. Luke didn't ask why not.
