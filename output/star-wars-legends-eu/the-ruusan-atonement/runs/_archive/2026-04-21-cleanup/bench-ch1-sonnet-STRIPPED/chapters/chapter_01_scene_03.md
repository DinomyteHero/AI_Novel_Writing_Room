The preflight was done. Had been for ten minutes.

Ben sat in the *Starhawk*'s cockpit with the checklist complete and the engines idling and his thumb hovering over the datapad screen, not moving.

The Temple hangar stretched behind him through the viewport — high vaulted ceilings, hot metal and recycled coolant, a ground crew running diagnostics on a courier shuttle three berths down. The sounds echoed strangely in here, all hard surfaces and distance. He'd always found the hangar indifferent in a way the rest of the Temple wasn't, which he'd decided long ago was probably healthy.

He looked at the datapad.

Three names on the Jedi Knight roster. All good Jedi. All people who would say yes if he called, pack a bag, and be here in an hour without complaint. Lira Donn, who had the sharpest Force-perception he'd worked with outside his own family. Corran Fel, steady under pressure, good instincts. Yela Maris, who'd trained with him for two years and knew his signals before he gave them.

His thumb slid past all three and opened his personal contacts.

He didn't examine the motion. He just made it.

Desh Rolan's name sat near the top of the list, flagged with a private comm address that bypassed standard relay. Ben selected it before he could construct a reason why, and listened to the connection tone cycle twice, three times, four —

"You've reached Rolan Security Consulting." The voice was flat and dry and immediately familiar. "If this is a Jedi, I charge double."

"I'll put it on the Order's tab," Ben said.

"Skywalker." A pause. Not hesitation — Desh didn't hesitate, he assessed. "Long-range ping. You're calling from a ship."

"Sitting in the Temple hangar. Leaving in about fifteen minutes."

"Survey run?"

"Something like that." Ben leaned back in the seat. "Outer Rim corridor, three sites, possibly more. Duration unknown. I need someone who can handle a sensor package and doesn't ask too many questions about what the readings mean."

Another pause. Shorter. "When do I need to be at Pad Twelve?"

Ben's mouth pulled at the corner. He hadn't told Desh which pad. "Fourteen. And you're not already here."

"I'm in the lower city. Give me forty minutes."

"You'll have thirty-five."

"Then I'll run." The line crackled — long-range distance, or maybe just Desh's end of the connection, which had always sounded like it was operating one maintenance cycle past due. "Same rate as last time?"

"Same rate."

"Good." A beat. "I'll bring the field kit."

The line closed.

Ben set the datapad on the console and looked at it for a moment. Desh hadn't asked why he was calling instead of pulling from the Jedi pool. Hadn't asked what the sensor readings were for, or whether this was official Order business, or whether Ben had cleared it with anyone. He'd asked when and what rate, and that was the whole conversation.

There had been a time — a specific night, rain-slicked streets in the lower Coruscant levels, three years ago and a different kind of mission entirely — when Ben had understood exactly what Desh's silences cost him. The Galactic Alliance Guard had been dissolved by then, its files sealed, its methodology quietly buried by people who'd decided that the post-war Alliance needed to forget some things in order to function. Desh had been one of the ones who'd made it function. He knew what it meant to follow orders that didn't survive daylight, and he'd never once asked Ben to justify the company he kept.

The memory surfaced and receded in the time it took Ben to clip the datapad to the console mount.

He keyed the hangar control frequency. "Temple Control, this is *Starhawk*, Berth Fourteen. Requesting departure clearance."

"*Starhawk*, clearance granted. Departure vector delta-seven, orbital ascent lane four. Safe travels."

The repulsors engaged with a low, even hum that Ben felt more in his chest than heard. The *Starhawk* lifted from the deck — a clean rise, no wobble, the ship steady under him the way a well-maintained tool was steady. He liked this ship. It was small enough that he knew every sound it made, and right now it sounded fine.

The hangar fell away beneath him. He angled toward the exit bay and the pale wash of Coruscant's perpetual urban glow.

The wrongness was still there. It had been there since he'd boarded, sitting at the edge of his perception the way a bad tooth sits at the edge of your tongue — present, unavoidable, not quite painful but wrong in a way that made ignoring it work. He'd been ignoring it for six weeks. He was good at it by now.

He climbed through the departure lane, the city spreading below him in its patient illuminated grid, and thought about the three names he hadn't called. Lira would have been the right choice, technically. Operationally. She'd have been an asset and she'd have been professional about it and she would never have understood the part he couldn't explain — that he needed someone whose perception of the Force he didn't have to account for. Someone whose read on the situation would stay clean of whatever this thing was, so that when it got complicated, and it was going to get complicated, he'd have one data point that wasn't contaminated.

That was the practical reason.

He was aware it was the practical reason he'd constructed, and not the first reason he'd had.

The first reason was simpler and less flattering and he didn't particularly want to look at it, so he didn't.

The orbital lane opened ahead of him, Coruscant's traffic thinning as he climbed into the upper atmosphere. He filed the jump coordinates with the nav computer — the first site, a system called Ankhural that showed up in the historical records mostly as a footnote — and set the departure timer for when Desh would be aboard.

Thirty-three minutes, as it turned out.

The airlock cycled. Footsteps on the boarding ramp, the familiar cadence of someone who moved like they were always expecting the floor to shift. A duffel hit the equipment locker. Then Desh dropped into the copilot seat and looked at the nav display.

"Ankhural," he said. "That's deep Outer Rim."

"Yes."

"Anything I should know before we jump?"

Ben considered the question. "The readings we're following are anomalous. I don't have a good framework for them yet."

"Anomalous how?"

"Like a gap," Ben said. "Where there shouldn't be one."

Desh looked at him for a moment, then looked back at the display. "Okay."

That was also the whole conversation.

Ben brought the *Starhawk* around to the jump vector and engaged the drive. The stars stretched and collapsed into the white tunnel of hyperspace, the ship shuddering once through the transition — normal, that was normal, the *Starhawk* always shuddered on the jump — and then the streaking light steadied and the cockpit went quiet except for the drive hum.

The instruments flickered. A single stutter across three readout panels, there and gone.

Ben's hand went still on the console.

The wrongness was still present. It should have dropped away at the hyperspace boundary, or at minimum gone flat — that was how localized Force phenomena worked, how every documented case of dark-side saturation or residual wound energy behaved when you jumped clear of its source. Distance mattered. Hyperspace mattered.

This didn't care about either.

It was there in the tunnel with him, unchanged and patient, and it had a direction now. Not vague. Not ambient. A vector, clean and specific, pulling along the exact bearing of his jump coordinates like he hadn't chosen the destination at all.

Like it had.
