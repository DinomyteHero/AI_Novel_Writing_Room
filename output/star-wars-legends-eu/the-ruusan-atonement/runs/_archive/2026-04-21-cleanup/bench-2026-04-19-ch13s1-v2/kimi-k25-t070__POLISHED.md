The viewport filled with green and blue, the temperate bands of a world that should have teemed with life. Ben's hands tightened on the co-pilot yoke as the Steady Returns broke orbit. From up here, Kolven looked like a thousand other settlement worlds in the Expansion Region—blue oceans, continental forests, the faint thermal signatures of abandoned infrastructure scattered across the northern continent like freckles.

"Surface temperature reads twelve degrees below visual estimate," Desh said from the sensor station. The former GAG operative's voice carried the flat cadence of someone reporting impossibilities as routine. "Vegetation index is inverted. The forests are showing up as dead rock on thermal, and the urban centers are reading as dense biomass."

Ben adjusted the descent vector. "Visual confirmation?"

"Eyes say paradise. Instruments say freezer-burned greenhouse." Desh leaned forward, tapping his chrono against the console. The sound was sharp, metallic, insistent. "Also, my chrono gained four minutes during the approach. Just sitting here on my wrist, not even using the stop function."

Sera stepped up behind the pilot's seat, her hand resting on the headrest. She didn't touch Ben—she never did, maintaining the physical boundaries of a thousand-year-old military protocol—but her presence usually registered at the edge of his Force sense as a bright, focused point. Now she felt distant, or he felt distant, the connection already thinning as they approached the surface. "The Brotherhood had a facility here?"

"Gavran's anchors included a Kolven station," Torin said from the engineering station. He sounded too cheerful, the way he always did before something went wrong. "Secondary node. Veraine would have known the location. She'd have had time to work."

"She's been busy," Kael said. He stood by the viewport, arms crossed, watching the green continent swell beneath them. "Weeks ahead of us. Time to run experiments. Field tests."

The ship settled onto cracked duracrete landing pads that had survived a thousand years better than they should have. The ramp hissed open, releasing a breath of air that tasted like spring on Alderaan—wrong, already wrong, because nothing with this vegetation signature should smell this clean, this alive. Ben stepped down first. His boots hit the surface with a solid thump that should have grounded him. He reached for the Force to steady his balance, the automatic gesture he'd performed since he was old enough to walk.

His perception fired into formless nothing.

Not darkness. Not the absence he knew from ysalamiri or void-stone. The Force was there—he could feel the potential, the energy, the vast presence of life on the world around him—but there was no up, no down, no light, no dark. Just raw, directionless potential that offered no axis to orient against.

Ben stopped walking. The duracrete felt solid beneath his boots, but the ground of sense had dropped out from under him.

"You feel it," Kael said. He hadn't phrased it as a question. The smaller man stood two meters back from the ramp, his blaster held low but ready, his eyes scanning the tree line with the flat assessment of someone who had survived by noticing things first. "It's quiet."

"It's not quiet," Sera said. She had her lightsaber in hand, unlit. Her knuckles were white against the metal casing. "It's empty."

They moved into the settlement proper. Buildings stood intact—low prefab structures with durasteel frames that had resisted the centuries, their windows dark, their doors sealed against time. The architecture was familiar: Republic standard, pre-Ruusan, the kind of modular construction that had followed explorers into the Expansion Region during the centuries before the Brotherhood's defeat. But the vegetation climbed the wrong walls. Vines thick as Ben's wrist crawled upward on the northern faces, where shadow should have kept them stunted, while the southern exposures remained bare and bleaching in the sunlight. Flowers bloomed in alleys where no sun reached, petals open and white, facing the darkness like worshippers.

Ben watched a vine tendril reverse direction while he looked at it. The motion was subtle, a slow curling back against its own growth pattern, the tip withdrawing from the light it had sought a moment before, retreating into shadow.

"There's no birds," Torin said. He was trying to keep his voice light, the paternal warmth cranked up to cover the strain. "Settlement world like this, temperate zone—we should be drowning in avian noise. Instead, we've got..." He gestured at the silent trees. "Botanical gardens from a nightmare. Maybe the local wildlife has better sense than we do."

"Or maybe they left," Desh said. He had his chrono in his palm now, watching the face with the intensity of a man trying to catch a lie in the act. Ben could see the second hand from three meters away. It moved, stuttered, jumped backward a full tick, then resumed forward motion with a mechanical jerk that shouldn't have been possible in a standard chronometer. "Time here isn't keeping the kind of count that supports nesting cycles. Five minutes fast now. Gaining steadily."

Sera moved to a flowering bush growing against a wall. The blooms were white, waxy, opening toward the shadowed stone rather than the open sky. She touched one petal with a gloved finger, then pulled back as if burned. "These are phototropic. They should be facing the light. Basic biology."

"They're facing something," Kael muttered. He stayed near the ship, his back to the hull, his eyes tracking movement that wasn't there. "Just not anything we can see. Not anything that follows the rules."

Ben walked to the center of the landing pad. The duracrete was cracked but level, stained with chemical spills from ancient fuel lines. He widened his stance, planted his feet shoulder-width apart, and deliberately reached into the Force again.

The response came instantly—energy, presence, the living texture of the galaxy pressing against his awareness—but without the lattice, without the structure that gave it shape. It was like shouting into fog and hearing the echo return without direction, or trying to stand on water. His moral intuition, the subtle tug that had guided him since childhood, had gone silent. Not suppressed. Removed.

He stood there, breathing, while the others fanned out around him. Desh checked his chrono again, then holstered it with a sharp click. The second hand skipped, hung at the twelve, then jumped to the two.

"Six minutes," Desh said. His voice had gone flat, the tone he used when calculating odds that didn't favor survival. "Whatever she's doing, it's accelerating. The zone is expanding."

Torin laughed, a short bark that broke wrong against the silence. "Well. Veraine's field test, I suppose. She wanted to know if she could do it to a planet. Here we have our answer."

"She didn't just test it," Sera said. She was staring at the flowers with an expression that Ben couldn't read—her face had gone still in a way that meant she was processing something vast and terrible. "She collapsed the node here. This is what happens when the lattice is pulled out completely. Not degraded. Gone. Removed."

Ben let his hands hang at his sides. Without the Force's structure, he couldn't feel Sera's tension or Torin's fear or Kael's calculation. He could see their body language—Sera's rigid shoulders, Torin's too-wide stance, Kael's constant peripheral scanning—but the empathic resonance was gone. He was alone inside his own skull in a way he had never been, not even in the ysalamiri bubbles his father had used for training when Ben was a child, not even during the worst moments of the Second Galactic Civil War.

A grief pressed under his ribs. It wasn't his.

He knew it was Desh's—the non-Force-sensitive man standing three meters away, checking his weapons with mechanical precision, his face calm but his hands moving too often, adjusting straps that didn't need adjustment. Desh felt the wrongness as a grief without object, a sorrow that couldn't find its shape because the moral framework that would give it meaning had been removed from the environment itself. The man had no Force sensitivity to lose, but he had the human instinct for rightness, and that instinct was screaming into a void where the categories no longer existed.

Ben wanted to say something reassuring. The words wouldn't come. He had no sense of what would be true, no intuition guiding him toward the helpful thing to say. The Force wasn't whispering comfort or warning or anything at all. He was flying blind in his own mind.

He reached again, deeper this time, searching for the familiar axis—the light that guided, the dark that warned, the moral gravity that had always pointed toward the right choice like a compass finding north. Nothing. The Force surrounded him like water without current, air without temperature, space without dimension. Every trained instinct fired into the void and found no purchase, no comparison, no structure to push against.

The ground of sense dropped out entirely.

Ben stood on duracrete under a blue sky, breathing oxygen mixed with pollen from flowers that grew toward shadow, and felt his own thoughts with a clarity that bordered on violence. The Force wasn't softening them. It wasn't amplifying his better nature or quieting his doubts. Those processes had been automatic for so long that he hadn't recognized them as assistance—he'd thought they were him.

Now he heard himself think, unfiltered.

The fear was his. The anger was his. The cold calculation that suggested leaving this place, abandoning the survivors, letting Veraine have her victory if it meant survival—that was his too. All of it. Every petty, frightened, selfish impulse that the lattice had previously rendered as faint whispers against the steady drum of Jedi intuition.

In the silence of the de-structured Force, Ben hears himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts are his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softens.
