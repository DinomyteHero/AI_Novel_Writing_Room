Kolven filled the viewport like a recruitment poster. Green continents, blue oceans, the white swirl of cloud cover over temperate zones. Ben had seen a hundred worlds look exactly like this from orbit. They were usually telling the truth.

Desh's voice cut through the cockpit's quiet. "Surface temperature's reading fourteen degrees at the landing coordinates."

"Fourteen's cold for temperate," Torin said from behind them.

"Fourteen's not the problem." Desh tapped the display. "Fifty meters east of the pad, it's reading thirty-one. Same elevation. Same atmospheric column."

Ben kept his hands on the co-pilot's yoke. The numbers didn't match the view. "Vegetation signatures?"

"All over the spectrum. Deciduous canopy next to tropical bloom next to what looks like tundra lichen. Same grid square." Desh pulled up another readout. His brow furrowed. "Atmospheric composition's within habitable range, but the ratios are shifting as I watch. Oxygen's climbing. Nitrogen's dropping. Slow, but measurable."

"Live readings or instrument error?" Sera asked from the crew compartment doorway.

"I've calibrated twice. The instruments are fine. The atmosphere's drifting."

Ben watched the green-and-blue world turn slowly below. Beautiful. Normal. Lying. "What about the bird analogs?"

"Wrong." Desh pulled up the tracking data. "Migration patterns say they should be heading south for seasonal shift. They're going north. All of them. Every flock on the continent."

"Maybe they know something we don't."

Nobody laughed.

The descent was smooth. No turbulence, no atmospheric anomalies, nothing the instruments could name. The Steady Returns settled onto a landing pad cracked by root growth but structurally intact. Through the canopy, Ben could see the settlement stretching outward—two rows of prefabricated shelters, doors hanging open, windows dark. A standard grid. The kind of place that should have had speeders parked along the curbs and voices carrying from open doorways.

He cycled the ramp. The air that rushed in tasted clean. Vegetative. Humid. Unremarkable.

His boots hit duracrete. Normal. Solid. The street ahead was empty but intact.

The vines climbing the eastern wall of the nearest shelter were moving.

Ben stopped walking. He watched a tendril the thickness of his thumb curl upward, reach the window frame, and reverse. Unwinding itself. Retreating downward against the wall. Another vine followed. Then another. While he watched, a third tendril changed direction mid-curl, looping back on itself like a question being unasked.

"That's not wind," Kael said flatly.

"No," Sera said. She'd stopped beside him, watching. "It's not."

"Directional reversal in real time," Torin said. He sounded more fascinated than disturbed. "The growth pattern is actively inverting. Whatever signal tells the plant which way to grow—it's not holding."

"Torin." Sera's voice carried a warning.

"I'm observing. That's what we're here for."

They moved deeper into the settlement. Sera and Torin fanned out to examine the shadowed spaces between buildings. Ben followed the main street, his boots on duracrete, his balance registering normal gravity on a solid surface.

But something under the surface of his perception had shifted, and his body couldn't locate the source.

"Flowers here," Sera called from around the corner of a building. "Blooming in shadow. Full blooms. On the north face."

"North face gets no direct light," Torin said.

"No."

Kael had drifted to the edge of the pad, arms crossed, watching the vines retreat and advance. "So the plants are growing wrong and the birds are flying wrong. What else?"

"The silence," Sera said.

Ben heard it then. Or didn't hear it. The settlement should have been full of ambient sound—insects, wind through the structures, the rustle of movement in the vegetation. Instead there was a flatness to the air, a pressure that absorbed sound rather than carrying it.

"The silence is wrong," Kael agreed. He didn't elaborate. He didn't need to.

Desh pulled his chrono off his wrist. Ben watched him hold it at eye level, studying the face with the focused attention of a man who trusted instruments more than impressions. The second hand moved. Stuttered. Skipped backward two ticks. Moved forward again.

"Time's drifting," Desh said. His voice was steady—operative steady, the voice of a man reporting a malfunction he could quantify. But his other hand had pressed flat against his sternum, and he didn't seem to realize he'd done it.

"Measurable drift," Sera said. She came back around the corner, her expression tight. "The zone is affecting local chronometry."

"It's affecting more than that." Desh looked at Ben. "Something's pressing on my chest. Like grief, except I can't find what it's for. I've been in bad places, Skywalker. This isn't like bad places. Bad places have a shape. You can feel what's wrong. This one—" He stopped. Shook his head. "There's nothing to push against."

Ben felt it too. A pressure below his own ribs, a weight that wanted to be grief but couldn't locate its object. Not his emotion. Desh's. Except he couldn't be sure anymore, because the moral shape that let him distinguish his feelings from the ambient current had vanished.

He couldn't offer reassurance. The words would have no structure to stand on.

"Spread out," he heard himself say. "Observe. Document. We need to understand the radius."

Professional. Controlled. The right words, delivered in the right tone. He had no idea if they were the right orders.

"Ben." Sera's voice, sharper now. "Are you—"

"I'm fine." Too fast. Too certain. The kind of answer that meant the opposite.

Sera studied him for a moment. Then she nodded once and moved deeper into the settlement with Torin. Kael drifted after them with the deliberate unhurriedness of a man who wanted to keep the ship in sight.

Desh stayed by the ramp, chrono in one hand, the other still pressed to his chest.

Ben widened his stance. Planted his feet on the cracked duracrete. And reached for the Force—deliberately this time, with full attention, the way he'd reached for it ten thousand times since childhood. A reflex. A steadying hand on a rail that had always been there.

The Force answered.

Energy. Presence. The raw current of life and connection that had been the background of his existence since before he could name it. It was there. He could feel it. But the shape of it—the axis that told him where light ended and dark began, the moral intuition that had guided every decision he'd ever made—was gone.

The Force was present and formless.

His perception fired into it and returned no orientation. No up. No down. No this-is-right, this-is-wrong. The ground of his sense dropped out.

Every trained instinct he possessed fired into that absence and echoed without returning. He was standing in raw potential with no direction, no axis, no register. The Force was there. It was not silent. But it offered nothing he could hold.

His hands were shaking.

He pulled back. The physical world reasserted itself—duracrete under his boots, humidity on his skin, the distant sound of Sera's voice calling out a building number. But the absence stayed with him, a hollow where something fundamental should have been.

Veraine did this. She pulled the lattice out of this world. And if she could do it here, as a test—

He didn't finish the thought.

In the silence of the de-structured Force, Ben heard himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
