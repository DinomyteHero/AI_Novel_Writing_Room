The planet looked healthy.

That was the first wrong thing.

Ben stood at the Steady Returns' forward viewport and watched the world turn below them—green continents, blue water, white cloud cover breaking over temperate coastlines. Desh was at the sensor console behind him, reading scan data in the flat voice he used when the numbers didn't make sense and he'd decided not to panic about it yet.

"Surface temperature nominal," Desh said. "Atmospheric composition nominal. No toxins, no radiation, no particulate anomalies." A pause. "Chrono sync is off by four seconds against the nav computer."

"Four seconds," Ben said.

"Four seconds. We've been in orbit eleven minutes."

Ben didn't look away from the viewport. The planet kept turning, blue and green and entirely ordinary. "What's the bird count?"

Desh pulled up the bioscan. Another pause, longer this time. "Avian life signs—" He stopped.

"Desh."

"Zero."

Sera moved up beside Ben, arms crossed, studying the surface with the expression she used for tactical assessments. "The previous nodes showed partial suppression. Localized distortion, wildlife displacement within the radius." She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to.

"This isn't partial," Torin said from the co-pilot's chair. His voice was careful in a way that Ben had learned to read as Torin deciding not to show what he was feeling. "She didn't test the technique here. She completed it."

Kael said nothing. He was watching the planet the way he watched most things—from the back of the group, with his weight on his back foot, like he was already calculating the distance to the exit.

Ben pulled his eyes from the viewport. "We go down."

---

The ramp hit dirt and Ben walked out into wrong.

The air was fine. That was the second wrong thing. His lungs reported nothing unusual—temperature, humidity, pressure, all within normal range. His eyes reported a settlement world in early afternoon light, buildings intact, streets visible between overgrown lots, a sky the right shade of blue.

His skin reported something else. Not cold. Not heat. A kind of pressure that had no direction, like standing at the bottom of a deep pool without the water.

"Vegetation's growing toward the shadows," Kael said, somewhere behind him.

Ben looked. The nearest building had a wall of climbing vines on its north face—the shaded side. Every tendril oriented away from the sun. Across the street, a planter box held flowers in full bloom, petals wide open in the shade of an awning, while the side of the box in direct light was bare soil.

He'd read about this. The Ankhural Codex described it as phototropic inversion—organisms that used the Force's structural layer to interpret environmental gradients losing that interpretive framework and defaulting to the opposite orientation. He'd read it as an academic description of a theoretical effect.

Standing in it was different.

Desh came down the ramp last, datapad in hand, scanning. "Chrono drift is accelerating. We've been on the ground ninety seconds and I'm reading eight-second variance." He looked up. "That's not atmospheric. That's not magnetic interference. I don't have a category for this."

"How do you feel?" Sera asked him.

Desh considered the question with the same deliberateness he applied to everything. "Like I'm missing something. Not a thought—I'm not confused. Like I'm standing in a room where someone used to be and they left recently." He glanced at his chrono again, watched it, put it back in his pocket. "It's not pleasant."

Torin had walked a few meters into the street. He stood with his head slightly tilted, the posture of someone listening for a sound they'd heard once and couldn't place.

Ben reached for the Force.

---

It was there.

That was what he hadn't expected. He'd braced for absence—for the flat, dead silence he'd read about in the Codex descriptions of Nathema, the total severance that left Force-sensitives hollowed out. He'd prepared himself for nothing.

What he got was everything, and none of it meant anything.

The Force was present the way heat was present in a dark room—he could feel it, energy and potential and the faint pressure of something vast, but when he tried to orient himself within it, when he reached for the familiar pull of light and shadow, the sense of living things and their emotional weight, the quiet moral gravity that had been part of his perception since he was old enough to open himself to it—

Nothing reflected back.

He pushed harder. The energy was there. He could feel it moving, or something that moved like it, currents without direction. He reached for Sera's presence and felt—heat, proximity, potential, but no texture. No sense of her discipline or her tension or the particular quality of her attention. He reached for Kael and felt the same formless warmth. He reached for the street, the buildings, the inverted vines, and felt only mass and distance.

He was standing in a lit room where nothing cast a shadow.

"Ben." Sera's voice.

He opened his eyes. He hadn't closed them.

"What do you have?" she asked.

"It's not gone." He heard how his voice sounded and adjusted it. "The Force is present. But there's no—" He stopped. The Codex language came back to him: lattice. Structure. The organizational framework that made the Force navigable, that gave it direction and meaning and the capacity to carry moral information. "There's no structure. It's like trying to read in a room where the light exists but nothing casts shadows. You can see, but you can't see anything."

Torin had turned back toward them. His expression was the most honest Ben had seen it since they'd met—stripped of the warmth, the easy authority, the paternal steadiness. He looked like a man standing in a place he recognized from a nightmare.

"The last time I felt something close to this," Torin said, "was during the Veranthos lattice inversion. That was partial. Localized. We pulled back before it completed." He looked at the inverted vines. "This is what we were trying to prevent."

"She used this world as a test," Sera said. Not a question.

"She used it as proof," Kael said. He'd moved without anyone noticing, was standing at the edge of the planter box, looking at the blooming flowers in the shade. "That it works. That she can do it at scale." He touched one of the petals, not quite gently. "How many people lived here?"

Desh checked his datapad. "Settlement records show forty-two thousand at last census. Eighteen months ago."

No one asked the follow-up question. The bioscan had already answered it.

---

Ben walked to the center of the street and stood there.

The professional part of his mind was still running—cataloguing, assessing, building the operational picture. Veraine had demonstrated a full-scale de-structuring. She'd proven the technique worked at settlement-world scope. Veranthos was larger, more complex, more densely populated, and sat at the convergence of three major hyperspace routes. If she could do this here, she could do it there, and the effect wouldn't stay local. The Codex had been explicit about cascade potential.

He knew all of that. He filed it and set it aside.

Because the other thing was happening, and he couldn't stop it.

With the lattice gone, the Force offered no feedback. No gentle pressure toward patience when his temper moved. No quiet weight of consequence when he considered something he shouldn't. No sense of other people's presence as anything more than warm mass. The layer of perception that had always—not controlled him, he'd never thought of it as control—but informed him, given him context for his own impulses, told him which of his instincts were worth following and which ones to examine first—

Gone.

Not replaced by darkness. Not replaced by anything. Just absent.

And his mind kept running, the way it always did, the assessments and plans and reactions, the things he thought about people and situations and choices, and none of it was filtered. None of it was softened. The thought he'd had about Torin two days ago, the one he'd let the Force gentle into concern before it could become something sharper—it was back, and it was sharp. The calculation he'd made about Kael's reliability and what he'd do if that calculation proved wrong—it was back, and it was cold.

He'd always known those thoughts were his. He wasn't naive enough to think the Force made him good.

But he hadn't known, until now, how much of the time he'd been relying on it to make him careful.

Desh appeared at his shoulder. "Chrono's at fourteen seconds variance." A pause. "You okay?"

Ben looked at the inverted vines. The flowers blooming in the wrong direction. The empty street of a world that had held forty-two thousand people.

"Ask me later," he said.

The thoughts kept coming. All of them. The Force was present around him—he could feel it, formless and vast and entirely unhelpful—and inside that presence, in the silence where his moral intuition had always lived, he was alone with his own mind for the first time in his life.

The thoughts were his.

All of them. Including the ones he'd never had to face without the lattice there to catch them.
