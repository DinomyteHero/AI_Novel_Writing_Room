The preflight checklist glowed green across every panel. Ben sat with his thumb on the datapad and didn't move.

The Temple hangar hummed around him — repulsor wash from a departing shuttle two bays over, the distant clang of a maintenance crew working a hull plate, the particular echo that polished durasteel floors made when a place was too large and too empty at the same time. His ship sat quiet. Everything ready.

He scrolled the roster.

Sera Varik. She'd been running her own anomaly observations for three months — he knew that, had watched her file the reports with the same careful precision she brought to everything. She'd understand the readings before he finished explaining them.

He kept scrolling.

Kael Drenn. Outer Rim experience, good instincts, no political complications. The practical choice, if Sera wasn't available.

He kept scrolling.

Torin Hal. Steady. Reliable. The kind of partner who wouldn't second-guess him in the field.

His thumb moved to his personal contacts.

*Practical,* he told himself. A Force-sensitive partner meant shared perceptions, shared interpretations, the inevitable pull toward consensus on what they were both reading. He needed clean data, not two people calibrating each other into agreement. Solo observation, then Desh for logistics and ground support. Efficient. Clean. The tightness below his sternum had nothing to do with any of it.

He opened the comm.

Three seconds of static, then: "Rolan."

"It's Ben."

"I know who it is." A pause, and Ben could hear the ambient noise of wherever Desh was — the low background drone of a ventilation system, maybe a cantina, maybe a security office. Hard to tell with Desh. "You need something, or are you calling to catch up?"

"Job," Ben said. "Outer Rim survey, duration unknown. Probably two weeks minimum, could extend."

"Pay?"

"Temple rates. Plus expenses."

Another pause. No *why aren't you taking a Jedi*, no *is this official Order business*, no questions about the mission profile or the risk assessment or what kind of trouble a Jedi Knight needed a former GAG officer to help him walk into. The Galactic Alliance Guard had been dissolved years ago, but the men and women who'd served in it carried the work in their bones — the kind of work that happened in the spaces where rules ran thin. Desh had walked away from that. Ben had just changed which organization he was doing it for. Just: "When do you need me?"

"I'm departing in about four minutes."

"Of course you are." The background noise shifted — chair scraping, movement. "Nearest transit hub to your current position?"

The warmth that moved through Ben's chest had an edge to it. He'd felt that particular combination before, back when they'd both been younger and less careful, when Desh had shown up at a safehouse on Nar Shaddaa with forged credentials and a holdout blaster and had not once asked why the mission parameters kept shifting.

He pushed that thought down before it finished forming.

"Coruscant Central Transit, Level Forty-Two," Ben said. "I'll send you the route data."

"Don't bother. I'll find it." A click, and Desh was gone.

Ben set the datapad on the copilot seat and wrapped both hands around the yoke.

He powered up the repulsors.

The ship lifted off the hangar deck with a low vibration he felt through the seat, through the soles of his boots, through his palms. The polished floor dropped away. He pivoted the nose toward the hangar mouth and let the autopilot handle the departure lane — Temple hangar control was particular about exit vectors, and he didn't need the notation on his record today. The echoes changed pitch as he cleared the bay, the acoustics of the hangar swapping for open air and then, almost immediately, for the thin upper atmosphere.

Coruscant spread below him in every direction. The traffic lanes were already threading around his departure vector, the city-planet's automated systems absorbing one more ship into its constant churn without noticing. He'd lifted off from this hangar more times than he could count. It never felt routine.

Today it felt like stepping off something.

He angled toward orbital clearance and let himself watch the Temple complex shrink in the rear display until it was just another structure in the grid.

*Be thorough.*

His father's voice, even and careful. All the things it had meant sitting layered under the two words like sediment.

Ben turned off the rear display.

The jump to the Outer Rim corridor would take eleven hours on the Hydian Way before he'd need to drop back to realspace and recalibrate for the survey route. Long enough to review the sensor suite specs. Long enough to sleep, if he could manage it. He ran the nav calculations twice out of habit, confirmed the exit point, and reached for the hyperdrive lever.

The wrongness was still there. It had been there since the morning — that same flat, pressured quality under everything else the Force was doing, the thing that didn't fit, the note that sat wrong against the rest. He'd grown accustomed to it the way you grew accustomed to a bruise: always aware of it, cataloguing its presence without poking at it directly.

He pulled the lever.

Starlines.

The instruments flickered — one panel dropping to static for half a second before recovering, which was not standard hyperspace behavior and which he logged without letting himself think too hard about what it meant. The blue tunnel of hyperspace stretched ahead, the nav computer ticking down the hours.

The wrongness didn't fade.

That was wrong. Hyperspace dampened Force perception — not completely, not the way a ysalamiri could, but the transition usually softened the edges of whatever he'd been reading in realspace. A background filter. He'd used it before, deliberately, when he needed to clear his head between a difficult mission and whatever came next.

The wrongness came through the jump clean and intact, and it wasn't diffuse anymore.

It had a direction.

Not a heading he could plot on the navicomputer — nothing that precise. More like the difference between standing in a room where something had gone wrong and standing in a room where something was going wrong, the distinction between aftermath and active pull. It was pointing at something. Or pulling him toward something. The distinction felt important and he couldn't articulate why.

Ben sat with his hands loose on the yoke and the blue tunnel running ahead of him and the nav clock ticking down eleven hours.

He'd told himself this was a survey mission. Clean data, solo observation, controlled variables. He'd told himself Desh was the practical choice.

The wrongness pulled steadily toward the Outer Rim, patient and specific, like a compass needle that had finally found north.
