The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Third time this week.

His sparring partner lowered her saber, waiting. The hum of both training blades filled the silence between them — that flat, buzzing tone that never quite resolved into a clean pitch, the way the Temple's recycled air never quite smelled like anything except the absence of outside.

"Again?" she asked.

"No." Ben deactivated his saber. "I'm done for the night."

She accepted that without comment, which he appreciated. She was a good partner that way — didn't push, didn't ask questions he'd have to deflect. He watched her clip her saber to her belt and collect her water bottle from the bench, and then she was gone, boots quiet on the durasteel floor, and the salle door hissed shut behind her.

Ben stood in the middle of the room and turned his deactivated hilt over in his palm.

The shoulder didn't hurt. That wasn't the problem. The problem was that he'd *seen* the strike coming — had read her weight shift, her elbow angle, the slight drop in her left guard that telegraphed the angle of attack. His body had the counter. His muscles had run that sequence ten thousand times. The half-second gap between knowing and moving shouldn't have existed.

It had existed because of the note.

He didn't have a better word for it. A note, played slightly flat, vibrating somewhere below conscious thought. It had spiked exactly as he was committing to the parry, and the spike had cost him the quarter-second of precise timing the counter required. The training blade had connected solidly across his shoulder instead of deflecting into empty air.

Skywalkers weren't supposed to miss beats like that.

He started pacing, hilt still in his hand, unignited. The salle was empty now — forty meters of open floor, impact matting at the edges, target remotes racked along the far wall in neat rows. The Temple pressed in around him through the walls, that constant presence of dozens of Force-sensitives going about their evening routines. Meditation sessions. Lightsaber maintenance. Someone in the upper halls running through katas with more enthusiasm than technique.

Ben walked the length of the room, turned, walked back.

The other Knights called it background noise. Post-Abeloth disturbance, residual static from the Maw crisis, the Force settling after trauma. Master Cilghal had documented it in three separate clinical reports. The consensus was that it was fading.

Ben had read those reports. He'd sat in the debrief sessions and listened to his fellow Knights describe the disturbance as intermittent, manageable, a low-grade interference that sometimes disrupted meditation but rarely affected combat performance.

He stopped walking.

*Rarely affected combat performance.*

His shoulder disagreed. His shoulder had disagreed three times this week, and twice last week, and four times the week before that. The pattern wasn't fading. It was getting more precise — not louder, exactly, but more targeted, like the note was learning where to hit to cause maximum disruption.

He raised his unlit hilt and moved into Soresu's opening stance.

The first sequence came cleanly. Muscle memory, no Force required — his body knew the geometry of the form the way it knew how to breathe. He moved through the second sequence, the third, letting the footwork carry him across the salle floor. His boots found the rhythm. Scuff, pivot, weight transfer, pivot back.

The flatness hit on the fourth sequence.

Not hard. Not incapacitating. Just a half-beat of wrongness that pulled at his balance during the pivot, like the floor had shifted a centimeter in a direction floors didn't shift. He compensated, completed the movement, but the compensation was conscious instead of automatic.

That was the distinction the clinical reports were missing. The disturbance didn't block the Force. It disrupted the *timing* of Force perception — the way combat intuition arrived a half-second before conscious thought, giving a Knight the margin that made the difference between deflecting and getting hit. The wrongness didn't silence that intuition. It delayed it. And a half-second delay in a fight was the same as no warning at all.

He lowered the hilt.

The salle hummed around him. Somewhere deep in the Temple's infrastructure, a ventilation unit cycled, and the air moved in a slow drift from the ceiling vents. Ben pressed the heel of his palm against his left temple and stood still.

The note was there now, low and persistent. Not spiking. Just present, the way a bad tooth was present — not screaming, but impossible to ignore once you'd noticed it.

He'd felt something like this twice before. The first time, he'd been seventeen and watching Jacen across a briefing table, trying to reconcile the brother-figure he'd idolized with the shape of what Jacen had become. There had been something wrong in the Force around Jacen by then — not dark, exactly, not the cold weight of the dark side, but *off*. Like the Force itself was uncertain how to read him. Ben had assumed at the time that it was his own perception, his grief distorting what he sensed.

The second time was Abeloth.

He'd been closer to her than almost anyone else who'd survived the encounter. He knew what that wrongness felt like — the specific quality of a Force presence that wasn't operating inside normal parameters. Not powerful-wrong or dark-wrong. *Structurally* wrong. The Force behaving as though some underlying rule had been suspended.

This wasn't Abeloth. This wasn't Jacen. But the quality was the same.

His palm pressed harder against his temple. The note pulsed once, subsided.

The clinical reports described a disturbance. Ben was increasingly certain he was perceiving something the reports weren't measuring — something that didn't register as a disturbance because it wasn't coming *from* the Force. It was happening *to* it.

He'd tried saying that once, in a debrief session six months ago. Master Sebatyne had looked at him with the particular patience she reserved for Knights who were technically correct but emotionally compromised, and suggested he schedule additional sessions with the Healers. His father had said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

The Healers had found nothing wrong with him.

He was handling it. That was the answer he gave when anyone asked, and it was even mostly true. He was functional. He was mission-capable. He was just also losing sparring bouts he shouldn't lose, sleeping in three-hour increments, spending his evenings alone in empty salles trying to map the edges of something that everyone around him had agreed to call background noise.

Skywalkers handled things alone. It was practically a family tradition.

He lowered his hand and looked at the hilt in his other palm. The salle's overhead lights had shifted to their evening setting, warmer and lower, and the shadows at the edges of the room had deepened.

The note faded to its baseline flatness.

*Structural,* he thought again. Not emotional. Not residual. Structural.

He didn't know what that meant yet. He knew it meant something.

The salle door opened.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad in his hand and an expression Ben had learned to read before he was old enough to have a reason to recognize it — the particular set of his father's mouth and eyes that meant he'd already mapped the problem, already considered the risks, and was now looking for the right person to send.

Ben clipped his hilt to his belt.
