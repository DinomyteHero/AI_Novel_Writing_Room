The datapad landed on the desk between them with a soft click.

"Seventeen reports," Luke said. "Six weeks. Same anomaly signature, same bearing." He leaned back, hands folded, watching Ben the way he always did — not waiting for a reaction, just there. "Survey teams flagged them as instrument error at first. Then the second team came back with the same readings."

Ben picked up the datapad.

The reports were tidy, formatted in standard survey notation — grid coordinates, Force-reading indices, and a classification tag Luke had apparently created fresh because nothing in the existing system fit. Seventeen entries, each marked the same way. He scrolled past the first six without slowing.

The office smelled of recycled air and old caf, and somewhere below the window the Coruscant traffic moved in its perpetual river, the sound of it softened to a low wash by the transparisteel. The room had the lived-in quality of a space where someone actually worked — mission files stacked to one side, a holocomm unit with a blinking relay light, two chairs that had seen better decades. Ben had sat in one of them for the first time when he was nine years old, waiting while Luke finished a transmission.

He reached the third cluster of readings.

His thumb stopped.

The coordinates were Outer Rim, unremarkable. But the index values underneath — the specific register of the disturbance, the precise quality of the Force-disruption the instruments had caught — were exact. He knew that pattern. Not something like it. That. The same sourness under the harmonics, the same wrongness he had been carrying since the salle three days ago, since longer than that if he was honest, since a version of it that had lived at the back of his awareness since he was twelve and had not yet learned its name.

He set the datapad down and moved to the window.

"The route runs along this bearing the whole way?" he asked.

"Directly along it." Luke's voice stayed even, all briefing-room precision. "The readings don't cluster — they're consistent. Whatever's causing it isn't a point source."

Ben looked at the traffic below. A cargo hauler was threading between two transit lanes, moving with the patient stubbornness of something that had been making this run for years and intended to keep doing it. The city went about its business. The wrongness sat at the back of his awareness like a stone in a boot, present even here, even in his father's office in the heart of the Temple.

"I can run the survey," Ben said.

A pause. Not long. Just enough to show Luke was choosing his next sentence.

"There's something else." Luke picked up the datapad, looked at it once, then set it back down. "I've felt it too. Eleven days." A beat. "Directional."

Ben turned from the window.

Luke's face stayed composed, careful — the Grand Master's face, the one that gave nothing away unless he chose to give it. But his Force presence was warm and familiar at this range, the way it had always been, and beneath the careful neutrality Ben could feel the weight of what Luke had not said. Eleven days. Luke had been sitting with this for eleven days, running reports, creating new classification tags, and saying nothing.

"Anomaly," Ben said. "That's how you'd describe it."

"Yes."

The word sat there.

Ben heard what it did not say. Accurate, and not enough. Luke was describing a phenomenon. An anomaly had edges you could map, a deviation from a baseline you could calculate, a problem you could approach with instruments and patience. What Ben had been living with was older than instruments. It had texture, a specific kind of wrongness that lived below the level of analysis — not a deviation from the Force's normal register but a sour note in it, one that only mattered if you'd spent years listening for that particular chord.

He had spent years listening for that particular chord.

He did not say any of it.

"What's the priority level?" he asked instead.

"Survey and report. Anything that changes the threat picture, I want to know." Luke paused. "You're a skilled pilot. The route is Outer Rim — no significant political complications, no active threat assessments. It's a practical assignment."

A practical assignment. Ben almost smiled. Luke was very good at this — the framing that was technically accurate, that gave them both somewhere to stand without naming the ground beneath their feet. The survey was real. The assignment was real. And Luke had handed it to him specifically this morning, the morning after the salle.

"Understood," Ben said.

He crossed back to the desk and picked up the datapad, and Luke did not say *take someone with you*, and Ben did not say *I'll check the roster*, and the silence around that point settled between them with the weight of a decision neither of them was making aloud.

Luke's warmth was still there in the Force. Steady. Slightly formal, the way it got when they were doing this — when they were being a Grand Master and a Knight instead of what they actually were. Ben thought *Dad* and felt the distance between that thought and the room, and did not close it.

"Anything else on the route I should know?" he asked.

"Nothing flagged." Luke met his eyes. "Trust your read. If something changes, you call it in."

There it was. Trust your read. His father knew what he was sending him into — not the assignment, not the Outer Rim coordinates, but the thing underneath all of it, the wrongness Ben had been carrying since before he could name it. Luke knew, and Luke was handing him the mission anyway, and neither of them was going to say why that made sense, because saying it would require naming what Ben had lived with and what Luke had only felt for eleven days, and that gap was too wide to cross in a briefing room.

Ben tucked the datapad under his arm.

"I'll file departure clearance this afternoon," he said.

Luke nodded. "Good hunting."

It was the kind of thing you said to a Knight you trusted with a mission. It was also the kind of thing a father said when he was letting go of something he wasn't sure he should let go of, and the two meanings sat in the same words and neither of them reached for the difference.

Ben walked to the door. He did not look back.

He accepted the mission. Luke did not push. Neither of them said what the silence meant — that Ben was walking into the thing that had haunted him since childhood, that Luke was letting him, and that somewhere in the space between those two facts lived everything they had not learned to say to each other.

The door slid shut behind him.
