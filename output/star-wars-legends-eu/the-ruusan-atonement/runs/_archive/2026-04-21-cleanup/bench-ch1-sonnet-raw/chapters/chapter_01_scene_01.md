The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

Ben stepped back, absorbing the hit, and knew before the sting even bloomed that it was the wrongness again. His guard had come up late. Not his muscles — they had moved correctly, cleanly, the way they always did. The lag was in the field itself, the ambient layer that should have pressed against him a half-beat before Rendell’s follow-through, the way it always had, the way it had since he was twelve years old and training in this same Temple with his father standing two meters away, telling him to stop thinking and listen.

The field had come too late. Rendell’s blade had not waited.

“Good exchange,” Rendell said, which was the polite version of *What was that?*

“Your footwork is better.” Ben deactivated his training saber. The hum died. “I’m calling it.”

Rendell lowered his own blade. He was a solid Knight, careful and patient, the kind who noticed things and didn’t always say them. He was noticing something now, his gaze flicking once to Ben’s shoulder and back to his face. “Rematch?”

“Early morning tomorrow.” Ben rolled his shoulder, letting the motion carry the rest of the excuse — fatigue, a long week, nothing worth discussing. “I’m done.”

A beat. Rendell accepted it the way Knights accepted things they didn’t believe: quietly, professionally, without pressing. “Good night, then.”

His footsteps crossed the salle floor and faded into the corridor. The door slid shut behind him.

Ben stood in the sudden quiet and breathed.

Third time this week. The first two he’d managed to bury without making a spectacle of it — a delayed parry he’d turned into a feint, a stumble in footwork he’d disguised as a repositioning step. Recoveries. The kind of small adjustments that looked like choices if you were watching from the right angle. Tonight the angle had been wrong. Rendell had been watching from exactly the right place, and Ben had been half a beat slow, and there was no recovery for a training strike that had already landed.

His palms were still sweating. He could feel his heartbeat in the muscle above his shoulder.

The salle settled around him: climate controls whispering overhead, the distant percussion of boots in the corridors beyond, the recycled-air smell of the Temple at this hour, sweat and durasteel and something faintly metallic from the equipment racked along the far wall. Normal sounds. Normal smells. The Temple on the late shift, carrying on as if nothing here had gone wrong.

The wrongness was still there.

He knew it the way you know a sound has stopped — not by hearing it, but by the space where it should be. Except this was the reverse. The Force was present. It had always been present. What was wrong was the timing. The ambient pressure his body had learned to read before conscious thought — the half-beat that arrived before the moment that needed it — was arriving after. Barely. Consistently. Like a musician playing just behind the pulse, technically accurate, technically present, wrong in a way that only mattered when he moved fast enough to need it.

Ben started moving.

He walked through the opening sequence of Shii-Cho without igniting the blade, wrists loose, letting muscle memory run without the distraction of an active weapon. His body knew the form. His body had always known the form. That was the point. He wasn’t testing his muscles.

Pivot. Step. The field should have arrived.

It arrived late.

He ran the sequence again. Different form — Makashi, the second variation, the one that demanded the most precise timing in the field-read because the footwork was deceptive and the parries depended on knowing that half-beat before the body needed to know. His feet moved correctly. His hands moved correctly.

The field arrived late.

He stopped.

His breathing was steady. His hands were not shaking. He looked at them anyway, because he needed to check, because there was a version of this where the problem was him — where the week had been long enough and the sleep short enough that he was misreading his own fatigue as something external, and the thing he needed most right now was honesty about which problem he actually had.

His hands were steady. The form had been clean. The lag was in the field.

Not him. Not fatigue. Not residual anything.

The ambient field was arriving late.

Ben stood in the empty salle and let that settle. The foot traffic in the corridor moved past without pausing. A cleaning droid hummed somewhere beyond the east door, its progress marked by the faint squeak of wheels on Temple tile. The late shift was indifferent to whatever a Jedi Knight was sorting out in training room four.

He had been managing this for a week. He’d told himself it was background noise — the Order was still recalibrating after the Abeloth crisis, the Force was still settling, everyone was still adjusting, and a half-beat lag in his field-sense was the kind of thing that happened when the galaxy had been through what it had been through. He’d told himself that, and he’d half-believed it, and he’d kept his sessions private and his exchanges controlled and his excuses ready.

Rendell had seen through the last one.

Ben rolled his shoulder again. The sting was fading. The wrongness wasn’t.

He ran the Makashi variation one more time, just to be certain, just to give himself the chance to be wrong about being right. His wrists moved through the angles. The field read exactly the way it had read every time this week: present, functional, late.

He stopped pretending it was fatigue.

The relief was smaller than he’d expected. Somewhere in the back of his mind, he’d thought that if he finally admitted the problem was real and external, the pressure of it would lift. Instead it shifted — from the kind of weight he carried when he wasn’t sure if he was broken to the kind he carried when he was sure something else was. Different weight. Not lighter.

He was still standing there, blade dormant in his hand, when the door slid open.

Luke appeared in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
