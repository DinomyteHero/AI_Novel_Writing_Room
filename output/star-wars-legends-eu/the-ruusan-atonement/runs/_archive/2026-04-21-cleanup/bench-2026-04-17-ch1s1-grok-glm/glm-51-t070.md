The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

Ben registered the impact before he registered the miss—a hot stripe across his deltoid that would bruise by morning. His guard had dropped. Not early. Late. A half-second too slow to bring his saber up, and the Knight opposite him had done exactly what any competent opponent would do with an opening that wide.

"Good hit," Ben said.

She lowered her training saber, breathing hard. "You hesitated."

Fatigue. That was the word for it. The word he used. "Long week."

A nod, accepting the explanation he hadn't really offered. Three times now. Three sessions where the Force had stuttered under his grip like a transmission slipping gears, and three times he'd walked away with some variation of the same excuse. Tired. Distracted. Off his game.

Ben deactivated his training saber and set it on the rack along the salle wall. The hum of its power cell died, and the silence left behind pressed against his eardrums.

"Run it again?" the Knight asked.

"Rain check." He rolled his shoulder, testing the damage. Manageable. "Need to cool down."

She gathered her things and left without pressing. Jedi gave each other space. One of the Order's virtues and one of its blind spots—nobody asked follow-up questions when you said you were fine, because asking implied doubt, and doubt implied judgment, and the path between judgment and the dark side was shorter than anyone liked to admit.

The salle door sealed behind her.

Alone in the quiet. The Temple's training spaces were built for acoustics—high vaulted ceilings that let the crackle of sabers dissipate without echo, walls lined with panels that absorbed the percussion of boots on hardwood. Late evening, the chamber held a stillness that settled against his skin.

He started pacing.

The wrongness was there. It had been there for weeks, lurking at the edge of his perception like a note played slightly flat—not wrong enough to be dissonant, just wrong enough to throw off every instinct he'd trained into his body since childhood. The Force flowed. It always flowed. But somewhere in the current, a subtle drag. A hesitation that didn't belong.

At the center of the salle, Ben settled into opening stance. First form. No blade this time—just his body, his breath, the physical memory of a thousand repetitions.

Block. Redirect. Counter. His muscles knew the angles without consulting his mind. His feet found their positions by habit. The form was clean, precise, exactly as he'd learned it from his father and his father's father before him.

No stutter. No lag. His body was fine.

Second form, faster now, pushing his balance and recovery. The Temple's living presence hummed around him—dozens of Jedi scattered through the building, their signatures as familiar as the sound of his own breathing. The Force was here. It was always here. Not absent or diminished or corrupted in any way he could name.

Just off.

Third form. He drove his weight forward into a lunge that would have ended with a blade through an opponent's guard. His timing was perfect. His body obeyed. And yet the Force that should have sung through the movement carried that same flat quality—present, powerful, and subtly misaligned with itself.

Ben stopped. His breath came hard. Sweat cooled on his temples and the back of his neck.

He knew what wrong felt like. Grown up in the shadow of his cousin's fall, spent his formative years watching Jacen Solo become Darth Caedus one rationalization at a time. Stood against Abeloth when she'd twisted the Force into something predatory and hungry. He knew the taste of the dark side—bitter, corrosive, seductive in its certainty.

This wasn't that.

This was structural. The difference between a song played badly and a song played in the wrong key. The notes were all there, but the framework that gave them meaning had shifted a degree off true.

And no one else felt it.

He'd asked. Carefully, casually, in the way you ask about the weather when you're trying to figure out if you're the only one who noticed the sky is the wrong color. The other Knights reported the expected post-Abeloth disturbances—pockets of residual darkness, the occasional eddy in the Force where reality had been bent too hard and too long. Normal. Expected. Background noise that came with living through a galactic crisis and would fade with time.

Ben's wrongness wasn't fading. It was spreading.

Boot soles whispered against the salle floor as he resumed his circuit. The rational explanation was trauma. Too close to too many catastrophes, and his perception was scarred. Skywalker luck—survive everything, carry the bill afterward. His mother would have told him to stop catastrophizing. His father would have told him to trust his instincts. Both of them would have been right, which meant neither answer was sufficient.

The problem with being a Skywalker was that everyone assumed you had a direct line to truth. What they didn't understand was that the line worked both ways—when something felt wrong, you couldn't dismiss it as anxiety, but you also couldn't assume it was prophecy. Sometimes a headache was just a headache. Sometimes the galaxy really was ending. Distinguishing between the two required more than instinct.

It required evidence. And all he had was a flat note in the Force and three lost sparring matches.

Another circuit. The windows along the far wall showed Coruscant's skyline at night—endless towers lit against the dark, traffic lanes threading between them like luminous veins. The city never slept. The Temple did, or tried to. Somewhere above him, Jedi were meditating, resting, preparing for tomorrow's duties with the quiet discipline of an Order that had survived worse than the current peace.

His shoulder ached where the training blade had caught him. A good ache. Grounding. Physical problems had physical solutions.

The wrongness in the Force didn't.

At the window, Ben pressed his palm flat against the cool transparisteel. The city hummed beyond the glass—speeders, climate systems, ten trillion lives generating a subsonic vibration you could feel if you stood still long enough. He reached for the Force underneath the noise.

There. Again. That drag. A fraction-of-a-second delay between intention and flow, like water encountering an obstruction downstream that he couldn't see but could feel in the current's pull against his palm.

Something similar, once before. Not after Abeloth. Not during the war. Before that—or underneath it. In the moments when Jacen had stopped being Jacen and started becoming something else, when the Force around his cousin had developed a quality of absence, as if the light was there but bending around something that refused to be illuminated.

Not the dark side. Something adjacent to it. Something that made the dark side look like a symptom instead of a disease.

Ben pulled his hand back from the glass.

The movement behind him was subtle—a shift in the air pressure, a change in the room's acoustic signature as the salle door opened. He turned.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway. Datapad in one hand, posture carrying the particular stillness that meant he'd been watching for longer than Ben had noticed. His expression was the one Ben had learned to read before he'd learned to read words: calm, focused, and carrying the weight of something that needed doing.

A mission.
