The training blade slapped across his shoulder, and Ben was already stepping back before the contact fully registered.

Third time this week.

His sparring partner lowered his weapon and waited—professional, patient, wearing the kind of stillness that passed for courtesy when you’d just scored on someone who should have seen it coming. Ben rolled his shoulder out of reflex more than need. The strike hadn’t been hard.

That wasn’t the point.

The point was the half-beat. The gap between his body and the Force had no business existing in a Knight two years past his trials, no business existing in someone who’d trained the way Ben had. It kept showing up like a missed stair in the dark—that tiny fraction where the field answered from too far away, as though what should have been instant had to cross a distance first.

He had felt it again an instant before the hit. His guard had started to come up.

The Force had come late.

“Call it,” Ben said.

The other Knight deactivated the training saber. “You want another round?”

“Long week.” Ben lifted one shoulder in a shrug meant to end the subject. “I’ll run some forms. You go ahead.”

A pause. Concern, maybe. Or just the ordinary calculation of whether this was worth pushing. Then boots on the salle floor, heading for the door. The brief hum of a saber clipped back onto the rack. The hiss of the door.

Silence, or near enough.

That was convincing, at least.

Ben stood in the middle of the salle and drew a slow breath. At this hour the Temple settled around him the way it always did—the faint resonance of meditation chambers a couple of floors up, distant foot traffic moving through the corridor outside, a lift chiming somewhere down the east wing. A quick burst of laughter. Then quiet again. The late shift finding its own rhythm. The building doing what buildings did.

He had been telling himself it was fatigue since the start of the week. It was a decent explanation. It had been a decent week to be tired, and the Order had been treating the background disturbance as leftover static from the Abeloth crisis, the field settling back into itself like water after a thrown stone. Reasonable. Plausible. Ben had managed plausible right up until the moment the lag stole a block he’d been doing on reflex since he was sixteen.

He thumbed off his own training saber and laid it on the bench.

Then he started the form.

No blade. He had never meant to ignite it. This wasn’t about the saber. It was about the other thing—the thing he was testing without quite admitting he was testing it. Shien opening. Footwork first, then the transition into upper guard, the part that depended on the Force being there the way balance was there, the way breathing was there, the way a second hand was there. Not a tool you reached for. Just part of occupying your own body correctly.

The footwork came clean. The upper guard rose clean. He moved through the first sequence and felt nothing out of place, and for one brief second his chest loosened.

There. Fatigue. See.

Then he went into the second sequence—the half-turn, the weight shift, the instant where intention and the Force should have been the same motion—and the lag caught him again.

It wasn’t pain. Not quite vertigo, either. It was reaching for a handhold in the dark and finding it a half-breath after his body had already committed, enough to jar his balance, enough to make him stumble without actually falling. His next movement turned conscious. Deliberate. He was thinking about his weight, thinking about the angle of his arm, thinking the way a student thought.

That was wrong.

A Knight two years past his trials did not think his way through Shien footwork. He did it. The thinking was the failure.

Ben stopped.

He stood in the center of the salle with the unlit hilt hanging at his side, breathing a little harder than he should have been, while the Temple’s presence flowed on around him, utterly indifferent.

The meditation chambers hummed overhead. Out in the corridor, someone was keeping a quiet conversation to scheduling. A cleaning droid scraped along the far wall, clipped a corner, then corrected and kept going.

Everything else was where it belonged. The sounds were fine. The boots in the corridor, the lift chime, the droid’s faint mechanical grind—all of that landed where it should. It was the current beneath it that came in a half-beat late, the texture of the building and the settled calm of the Order’s evening arriving at a slight remove, like an echo that had taken the long way around.

He had been calling it fatigue because fatigue was manageable. Fatigue made sense. Fatigue did not require him to walk into the Jedi Council chamber and say something is wrong with the Force itself two years after his cousin had said something was wrong with the Force itself and had been right, and the Order had not understood that until it was Jacen Solo standing across a lightsaber from Luke.

Ben knew perfectly well that was not a comparison he could use.

He also knew that knowing better was doing nothing for him.

So.

He let the breath out. Not fatigue.

He didn’t know what it was. He didn’t have a name for it, and he wasn’t going to invent one now. Naming it would turn it into something he’d diagnosed, something he’d pinned down, and all he really had was the lag—the half-beat where the Force should have been and wasn’t. He had no idea whether it was local or distant, personal or spread wider than that, getting worse or holding steady. He had only the fact that he had felt it four times this week, and each time it had cost him something small.

Small things added up.

He also had the direction.

He had been careful not to look at that too closely. But it was there anyway, the way some things sat in the current before you were ready to face them straight on—a bearing, a pull, the sense of a sound coming from one side of a room and not the other. The lag had direction. He had noticed that the first time and filed it away, and had spent the week very deliberately not thinking about it.

Outer Rim. About that specific, and no more. Something far out, and getting less far. Or less quiet. Maybe both.

Ben clipped the unlit hilt back to his belt and stayed where he was a moment longer in the empty salle, inside the Temple’s steady hum, with the knowledge that he was alone in this and the flat, grim sense that alone was probably how this was going to stay for a while.

The corridor door opened.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben had learned to read before he was old enough to shave—the particular set of his father’s shoulders that meant a mission, and meant it was already decided.
