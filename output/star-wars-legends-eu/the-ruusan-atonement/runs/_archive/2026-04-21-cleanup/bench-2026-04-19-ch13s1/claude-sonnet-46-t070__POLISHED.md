Kolven looked like a postcard.

Ben held that thought while the *Steady Returns* dropped through the cloud layer, the planet filling the viewport in gradients of green and blue that some colonial surveyor had probably described as *temperate and promising* before filing the settlement report. Intact continent. Reasonable axial tilt. No obvious reason to quarantine an entire world.

"Temperature gradient on the eastern settlement grid is wrong," Desh said from the sensor station. He didn't sound alarmed. He sounded like a man reading a document that had been printed upside down. "Forty-three degrees at the shaded face of the main structure. Nineteen in direct sunlight."

"Reversed," Sera said.

"Reversed," Desh confirmed. He scrolled through the readout. "Vegetation biomass signatures are inconsistent with the visual. The canopy looks like late summer. The sensors say early spring. At best."

Ben kept his hands on the co-pilot yoke and watched the settlement resolve through the viewport — rooftops visible now, streets still legible between standing buildings, the whole place intact the way an exhibit is intact. Preserved. He pressed the heel of his palm against the yoke and felt the durasteel, solid and real, while something behind his sternum coiled once and settled into watchfulness.

"Any life signs?" Torin asked from the jump seats.

"Insects." Desh paused. "No birds."

No birds. Ben filed that away.

"There are no birds on Nathema either," Kael said. He was looking at the viewport, not at anyone. "No birds, no vermin, nothing that isn't rooted to the ground. Things that can leave, leave."

"Things that can leave had a thousand years to do it," Ben said. "Veraine was here weeks ago."

Kael turned his head. "Right. Weeks. That's very reassuring."

The *Steady Returns* settled onto the landing pad with a groan of metal on cracked duracrete — the pad intact, the surface fractured in a pattern Ben associated with thermal stress, not blast damage. Whatever had happened here had not been violent. It had been quiet.

---

The air outside was temperate. That was the first thing. Perfectly, precisely temperate, the way a climate-controlled room is temperate — not weather, but the absence of weather. Ben stood on the pad and breathed it in and felt his lungs work normally and his skin register nothing, and that nothing was its own kind of wrong.

"Check your chrono," Desh said, already looking at his.

Ben glanced at his wrist. The second hand was moving. Then it stuttered, backed up three ticks, and moved again.

"Mine's drifting," Desh said. He held it up without looking at any of them, watching the face. "Not much. Enough to measure."

Sera stepped off the ramp and stopped. She stood very still in the way she went still when she was listening through the Force — not tense, just present, every sense directed inward. After a moment she said, "It's here."

"The zone?" Ben asked.

"We're in it." She turned to look at the nearest building. The wall facing them was in shadow, and along that shadow, flowers grew. Dense, healthy, turned toward the shade the way flowers don't. "We crossed the boundary on descent."

Torin was already moving toward the street, reading the space with the practiced eye of someone who had cleared a lot of ground in his life. "Structural integrity looks sound. Whatever she did here, she wasn't interested in demolition."

"Field test," Ben said. "She wanted to know the radius. How long it holds. Whether it propagates."

He said it like a professional assessment and it landed like one. Nobody disagreed.

Then he reached for the Force.

His perception went out the way it always did — the familiar extension, the widening of attention beyond skin and bone into the living world around him. He felt it go. He felt the Force respond. Energy, presence, potential, the whole enormous fact of it, real and immediate and — 

No axis.

He reached for the light and his hand closed on air. He reached for any direction at all, any register, any sense of which way was which, and there was nothing to compare against. The Force was present. Completely present, full and available. And completely without shape.

His balance went first. Not physically — his boots were flat on the duracrete, his weight distributed correctly, nothing wrong with his inner ear. But the ground of sense that had been underneath every moment of his life dropped out, and he was standing in a room with no walls, no floor, no ceiling, with everything still there and nothing to tell him where he was in it.

He planted his feet wider. Breathed.

"Ben." Sera's voice, close and level.

"I'm fine," he said. The words came out steady. He was a little surprised.

"No you're not," Kael said. He was standing a few meters away, watching the vines on the eastern building face. As Ben looked over, one of them moved — a slow reversal, the tip curling back against its own direction of growth, visible in real time. "Nobody's fine. We're just not saying it."

Desh had his chrono open now, the case flipped back, watching the mechanism. "Four seconds of drift in the last two minutes," he said. "Measurable and accelerating." He snapped the case closed and looked up at the street ahead. His shoulders had dropped, not from relaxation but from something pressing down on them from inside. A weight that had no source he could name. Grief without an object. Loss without a shape.

Ben knew that weight. He was carrying it too. It pressed under his ribs like something that should be present and wasn't, something he should be able to reach for and couldn't.

He wanted to say *it's the zone, it's not real* — the standard Jedi reassurance, the framing that turned sensation into data and made it manageable. He reached for the framing and found only the sensation.

"The flowers," Torin said. He was standing by the shadowed wall, looking at the blooms turned toward darkness. His voice was quiet. "They're not diseased. They're growing correctly. Just in the wrong direction."

"Everything here is growing correctly," Sera said. "That's the point. The Force is present. It's just — " She stopped.

"No axis," Ben said.

She looked at him. He'd said the thing she'd stopped before. She nodded once.

Kael finally turned away from the reversing vine. "So she collapsed an entire settlement world to run a calibration test." He paused. "I want to say that's excessive, but I've been awake for three weeks and I've stopped having strong feelings about scale."

"The settlement was already evacuated," Torin said. "She chose it for that reason."

"That's not as comforting as you think it is," Kael said.

Ben took three steps down the street, stopped, and deliberately reached again — wider this time, the full extension he used when he needed to read a space. The Force came up to meet him completely. He held the contact and turned his attention in every direction he knew, looking for any gradient, any differential, any sense of *here* versus *there* in moral terms.

Nothing. Not degraded. Not muffled. Just absent, the way a compass is absent in a world with no magnetic poles. The needle spins freely. The needle works perfectly. The needle tells you nothing.

He stood with that for a moment.

"She can do this to Veranthos," he said. Not a question.

"If she reaches the primary anchors," Sera said, "yes."

"Then we have the radius." Ben turned back to the group. "We have the propagation rate. We have proof of concept at planetary scale." He looked at the reversed vines, the shadow-blooming flowers, the intact empty streets. "We also have the only complete picture of what full de-structuring looks like from the inside."

Desh looked at him steadily. "Are you going to be all right in here?"

"I'm going to have to be," Ben said.

Desh accepted that with a nod. It was a soldier's nod — not reassurance, just acknowledgment that the answer was sufficient and they were moving forward anyway.

They moved forward.

Ben walked the empty street with his perception still extended and still returning nothing he could use, and he listened to his own thoughts the way you listen to a room after the noise stops — surprised by what was there all along. No intuition smoothing the edges. No moral gravity pulling the difficult ones toward resolution. Just the thoughts, unmediated, his.

He'd always assumed the hard ones were the Force testing him.

Some of them were just his.
