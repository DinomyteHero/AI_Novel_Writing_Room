Ben watched the planet swell in the viewport, its blues and greens sharp against the black. Kolven looked like any number of settled worlds—temperate, inviting, ordinary. The kind of place where people raised families and complained about the weather. Desh sat beside him at the co-pilot station, eyes fixed on the sensor readouts.

“Surface temps jumping between twelve and thirty-eight degrees in the same grid square,” Desh said. “Vegetation signatures don’t match any known pattern. Half the biomass reads as growing backward.”

Ben’s hands tightened on the yoke. The Steady Returns shuddered once as it shed velocity. “Eyes say different.”

“Eyes are lying.” Desh tapped the screen. “Or the instruments are. Take your pick.”

From the rear compartment Sera’s voice carried forward, clipped and precise. “Veraine chose this place on purpose. A test bed. Small population. Isolated. Easy to contain the variables.”

Torin’s laugh followed, low and edged. “Easy. That’s one word for it.”

Ben kept his gaze forward. The ship’s autopilot painted a landing vector across cracked duracrete pads that should have been overgrown after a thousand years. Nothing looked ruined. Streets still ran straight between buildings. Roofs held their lines. Only the vegetation refused to behave—vines snaking up walls in spirals that hurt to track for long.

“Birds are wrong too,” Kael muttered from the hatchway. He rarely spoke first. When he did, it usually meant the rest of them had missed something obvious.

Ben glanced back. “No birds at all, you mean?”

Kael shrugged. “Exactly.”

The Steady Returns settled with a soft groan of landing struts. Ben cut the repulsors. The sudden quiet pressed in. He unstrapped and stood, rolling his shoulders against the flight suit’s stiff seams. The others followed him down the ramp without needing orders. Old habits from older wars.

His boots met duracrete that felt solid enough. The air carried a faint metallic tang, like recycled starship atmosphere left too long in the tanks. Wind moved through the streets, stirring dust that settled in odd patterns—piling against walls on the leeward side one moment, then reversing direction the next. Ben narrowed his eyes. A cluster of pale flowers bloomed in the shadow of an overhang, petals open to darkness instead of light.

Sera moved left, Torin right. They swept the area with the economical caution of people who had once commanded armies. Kael stayed near the ramp, watching everything and nothing. Desh crouched a few meters away, pulling a chrono from his belt pouch. The device looked ordinary—scratched plasteel casing, battered numerals.

Ben took one step, then another. Balance felt off, as though the ground had decided to shift its mind about which way down was. He planted his feet wider. The motion should have helped. It didn’t.

He reached.

The Force answered with raw presence—energy, vast and immediate. No direction came with it. No lattice of light or shadow to shape the surge. His trained instincts fired and met nothing they could grip. The ground of sense dropped away beneath him. He stood in open air with every muscle locked against a fall that never arrived.

A flower on the wall nearest Sera unfurled another petal while he watched. The new growth pushed toward the shade.

“Time’s drifting,” Desh said. He held the chrono up so everyone could see. The second hand stuttered, jumped backward three ticks, then resumed its crawl. “ measurable. Not my imagination.”

Sera’s head snapped toward him. “Show me.”

Desh passed the device over. She studied it with the same intensity she brought to every tactical problem. Torin joined her, one hand resting on the hilt of the lightsaber he refused to call his own anymore. The weapon hung at his belt like an accusation.

“Kolven had twelve thousand residents when the seal went up,” Torin said. His voice stayed warm, but the paternal tone carried strain now. “Gavran’s people evacuated most of them. The ones who stayed—” He stopped. Shook his head. “We thought the wound would heal if we walled it off. Looks like the wall had other ideas.”

Kael drifted closer to a patch of vines that climbed the side of a nearby hab-unit. The tendrils moved against gravity, reversing their spiral mid-climb. He didn’t touch them. “Silence is wrong too. No insects. No wind noise in the leaves even when the leaves are moving. Like the world forgot how to make background noise.”

Ben tried to breathe around the pressure building under his ribs. The sensation wasn’t his. He recognized the shape of it—Desh’s practical mind encountering something it couldn’t catalog. Grief without an object. The kind that arrived when the moral shape of things had been stripped away and nothing remained to mourn properly.

He wanted to say something reassuring. The usual Jedi words rose—about centering, about trust, about the Force providing what was needed. They died before they reached his tongue. No intuition told him whether reassurance would help or harm. The absence left his thoughts loud in his own skull.

Sera returned the chrono. “This matches the fragments we recovered from Veranthos. Local suspension of structure. She scaled it up.”

“Scaled it up and walked away,” Kael said. “Left the test running. Nice of her.”

Torin’s jaw worked. He scanned the empty streets as if expecting ghosts to appear. “She believes she’s curing the galaxy. We’re the disease.”

Ben kept his stance wide. The duracrete felt normal under his boots. His body insisted the world remained solid. Every other sense argued otherwise. He deliberately widened his stance further, planted his heels, and reached again. On purpose this time. The others watched him do it.

Presence flooded in. Potential crackled at the edges of perception. Nothing organized it. No axis aligned the energy toward light or dark. His moral intuition—the quiet voice that had guided him since childhood—produced only silence. The Force existed here. It simply refused to tell him what anything meant.

The drop hit him physically. Not in his stomach. Deeper. In the place where certainty had always lived. His knees locked to keep him upright. Sweat broke along his hairline despite the inconsistent temperature. The thoughts in his head sharpened, each one stripped of the softening buffer he had never noticed until now.

*This is what she wants for everything.*

The idea arrived unfiltered. No gentle correction followed it. No sense of perspective or compassion arrived to blunt its edges. Just the raw recognition that Darth Veraine could spread this absence across the galaxy and call it freedom.

Ben exhaled through his teeth. The sound seemed louder than it should. Birds—no, the lack of them—made every noise carry.

Desh pocketed the chrono. “We should mark the boundary. Come back with better instruments. Whatever this is, it’s accelerating.”

Sera shook her head once, sharp. “Instruments won’t measure what’s missing. Only what remains.”

Torin laid a hand on Ben’s shoulder. The contact felt distant, as though transmitted through thick armor. “You all right, kid?”

Ben nodded. The motion felt like a lie. He wasn’t all right. He was hearing himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
