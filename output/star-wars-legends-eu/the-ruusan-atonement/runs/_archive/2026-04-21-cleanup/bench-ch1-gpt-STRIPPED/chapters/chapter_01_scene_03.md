The cockpit was ready to go.

Preflight checks sat green across the forward display. Fuel, life support, nav buffer, hyperdrive motivator, all clean. The Temple hangar's guide lights striped the canopy in white bars. Somewhere outside, repulsors whined, then cut. The bay always carried sound oddly—too much stone under too much durasteel, every clank and hiss thrown back at the ships waiting to leave.

Ben held the datapad over his thigh and watched his thumb pass the first name on the Jedi partner roster.

Then the second.

Then the third.

He knew all three. Competent. Sensible. One would have asked careful questions. One would have offered help before he finished the briefing. One would have looked at him for half a second too long and guessed this was not only about survey work.

His thumb slid to the corner and switched screens.

Official Temple roster vanished. Personal contacts opened in its place.

There. Practical.

Long route. Sparse support. A survey that might stop being a survey. Bringing a non-Jedi with intelligence training and enough good sense to shoot, patch, lie, and improvise without turning it into a debate with the Council was just mission planning.

He set the datapad on the console and keyed the long-range comm. The cockpit felt tighter while the signal searched, all hard edges and instrument glow and the faint metallic tang of conditioned air. His fingers drummed once against the yoke, then stilled.

Static scratched.

A voice came through thin with distance. "If this is a social call, Skywalker, your timing is criminal."

Ben smiled before he meant to. "Good. I was aiming for disruptive."

"Then you've succeeded. I was in the middle of teaching a client that paying on time builds character."

"Did it take?"

"Not yet. Want to save me from honest work?"

Ben brought up the route and started locking the first leg into the nav computer. "I need a second set of hands."

A beat of static. Not surprise. Just distance.

"How ugly?" Desh asked.

"Unknown. Which is a polite way of saying potentially annoying."

"Travel time?"

"Outer Rim run along the Hydian side. We move fast."

"Temple job or Ben job?"

Ben keyed in a corridor marker and watched it slot into place. "Both."

"That usually means Temple job with extra bruises."

"Your gift for clarity remains inspiring."

Desh snorted across the comm. "Where do you want me?"

Ben sent the pickup coordinates to Desh's comm ID, then adjusted the departure vector by two degrees to clear traffic faster once he had launch. His hands stayed busy while his head worked around what Desh had not asked.

Not why me.

Not why not a Jedi.

Desh had learned a long time ago where Ben's silences lived. Back when the Galactic Alliance Guard still existed in all but name and too many assignments came stamped urgent and deniable at the same time, Desh had been one of the operators who understood that "containment" often meant "clean up whatever the people above us don't want attached to their names." Ben had crossed paths with him in the years after the war, when the uniforms changed faster than the habits. You learned who would push for truth, who would settle for survival, and who could tell the difference at three in the morning with blaster smoke in the air.

Desh had never asked for a clean version of Ben.

Ben finished the nav entry. "Can you make the lower city pad in forty minutes?"

"I can make it in thirty if Coruscant traffic develops a healthy respect for me."

"It won't."

"No, but I enjoy giving it chances."

The easy rhythm settled in, familiar enough to loosen something between Ben's shoulders. Dangerous, that. Familiar could look a lot like safe if a person wasn't paying attention.

"Bring field gear," Ben said. "Cold-weather too."

"That narrows it down to half my locker."

"Travel light."

"Now you're just being hurtful."

Ben checked the departure queue and bumped his clearance request to transmit. "Desh."

The other end went quiet.

"Thanks."

More static. Then, "If you get sentimental on me before we've even left orbit, I'm hanging up."

"Professional as ever."

"It's why you keep me around."

Ben looked at the personal contact list still open on the side display, Desh's name highlighted where the Jedi roster should have been. "Yeah," he said. "That's why."

Desh let that sit for a moment. He was good at that too—letting a line stay on the table without trying to open it up and examine the parts.

"I'll be there in thirty," he said. "Try not to save the galaxy without me."

Ben cut the comm before Desh could hear the short laugh that got out anyway.

Silence folded back into the cockpit, broken by the steady thrum under the deck plates and the clipped ping of incoming launch authorization. He set the datapad aside and brought both hands to the controls.

There were cleaner choices. Safer choices, if anyone upstairs wanted the official version. A Jedi partner would understand the survey protocols, the reporting chain, the things left unsaid whenever the Force was involved. A Jedi partner would also notice every hitch in his concentration, every time his attention snagged on that sour blank spot brushing the edge of awareness. They would ask what he had felt in the salle. Ask what he had sensed in Luke's office. Ask whether he thought this linked to Jacen, to Abeloth, to any of the old names that still put grit in his mouth.

Desh would ask whether he needed him to bring breaching charges.

Ben settled for the answer that let him launch.

The Temple hangar acknowledged his clearance with a green lane across the display. He eased the ship off its struts. Repulsors took the weight with a low, familiar hum. The cockpit trembled once and steadied.

The floor dropped away.

His fighter rose between guide beacons and service gantries, past stone ribs and metal catwalks cut into the bay's high walls. The place had the same stubborn mix as the rest of the Temple—ancient bones wrapped around modern hardware, as if the building still hadn't accepted that starships belonged inside it. Technicians moved below in small knots, all business, reduced to flashes of color by distance and canopy glare.

Ben angled the nose toward the opening field.

Coruscant night met him at once: traffic streams layered in red and white, tower lights burning through haze, the city spread beneath him without edge or mercy. The ship's engines pressed him back into the seat as he climbed. Temple control fed him vectors. He answered, clipped and automatic, while the city unwound below.

For a minute the work of flying shoved everything else aside. Altitude. Bearing. Stack traffic. Clearance. A transport drifted half a lane where it shouldn't and Ben slid around it with room to spare.

Then the wrongness touched the edge of him again.

Not stronger. Not closer. Just present. The same dead patch under the skin of things, a stale taste where the Force should have answered cleanly. It had followed him from the salle to Luke's office to the cockpit, which was already rude. It should have thinned with distance from the Temple's reports and archives and old stone.

It didn't.

Ben checked his instruments on reflex, as if a bad sensor could explain what his nerves had already tagged as real. Readouts remained steady. Engine temperatures nominal. Shields balanced. Hyperdrive status green and patient.

He broke atmosphere and rolled the ship onto its departure heading. Coruscant curved below him now, all cloud bands and citylight, beautiful in the impersonal way dangerous things often were. The stars ahead sharpened against black.

Comfort lingered from the comm call, enough to make its own point. Desh would show. Desh always showed. No lectures, no careful Jedi courtesy around old damage, no need to pretend Ben's reasons were better than they were. That reliability came with its own price, and Ben knew it. Choosing Desh meant choosing a man outside the Order, outside the chain, outside the reflex that said another Jedi ought to stand at his shoulder when the Force started acting wrong.

He locked the hyperlane solution and set his hand on the lever.

Control choice disguised as practicality. Nice work.

"Too late now," he muttered.

The stars stretched. White lines speared forward across the canopy as he pushed the ship into hyperspace.

Blue swallowed the viewport.

The jump hit cleanly. For half a heartbeat the instruments flickered—one brief ripple across the sensor board, a blink in the nav readout, then stability returned. Ben's hand tightened on the yoke.

The wrongness stayed with him.

That stopped him colder than any alarm would have. Hyperspace usually changed the texture of Force perception even on a good day. Distances folded wrong. Living presences blurred. Threads went thin. This did not blur. It held.

Worse, it sharpened.

No longer a scattered irritation brushing past his senses. No longer a patch of absence drifting at random. It pulled. Faint, steady, off his port forward quarter relative to the line of travel, a pressure with orientation.

Ben sat very still, eyes on the blue tunnel, while that pull settled into place.

Like a compass needle finding north.
