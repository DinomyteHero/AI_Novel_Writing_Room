The planet looked fine.

That was the problem.

Ben watched Kolven fill the viewport as the *Steady Returns* dropped out of its descent arc — green-blue, temperate, the kind of world that showed up in settlement recruitment holos. A continent of mixed forest and open plain, a coastline visible through thin cloud cover, sunlight catching on what looked like an intact agricultural grid. Nothing about it suggested anything had happened here at all.

"Vegetation density reads at ninety-three percent," Desh said from the sensor station. "But the spectral signature on the canopy is off. Chlorophyll absorption is inverted in the northern quadrant — plants are processing light on the shaded faces."

"That's not possible," Sera said.

"No," Desh agreed. "It isn't." He scrolled through the readout. "Chrono drift in the lower atmosphere. Point-four seconds per standard hour. Temperature variance at surface level is running eight degrees inconsistent with the solar angle." He paused. "No bird activity on any frequency. Kolven had a breeding population of twelve native avian species at last census."

Ben kept his hands loose on the co-pilot controls. His mouth tasted faintly of copper, which he put down to the recycled air recycling less efficiently than usual. He told himself that.

"How many people were here?" Torin asked from the jump seats behind.

"Eleven thousand, four hundred," Kael said. He'd pulled the settlement registry from the nav computer an hour ago and hadn't mentioned it until now. That was Kael. "Before."

Nobody asked *before what*. They all knew.

Ben brought them down on the settlement's primary landing pad — duracrete, intact, with faded direction markings still legible. The *Steady Returns* settled with a normal thud of landing struts. Everything was normal. The ramp extended. Warm air moved in, carrying the smell of green growth and turned soil and something underneath that Ben couldn't place — not unpleasant, not recognizable, like a word he'd forgotten how to pronounce.

He went down the ramp first.

---

The streets were intact.

That was the second problem. Eleven thousand people didn't leave intact streets. They left broken things — overturned furniture, scattered personal effects, the evidence of panic or departure. Kolven's central settlement looked like everyone had simply stopped being present. Buildings stood with their doors closed. A market stall's awning had half-collapsed, not from violence but from the slow failure of unsupported weight. The street grid was traceable. Nothing was burned.

"There," Sera said.

The vine on the eastern face of the nearest building was climbing the wrong wall. The sun was behind them, putting the eastern face in morning shadow, and the vine was growing toward that shadow, leaves angled to catch the absence of light. Ben watched a tendril move — not in wind, there was no wind — and track slowly toward the darkest point of the wall's upper edge.

"It's moving," Torin said.

"They're all moving," Kael said. He was looking at the buildings across the square. Vines on every shadowed face, flowers open in the dark angles between structures where sunlight hadn't reached in months. Growth patterns that had forgotten which direction was toward. "They've been doing it long enough to get that far up the wall. This isn't new."

Ben pulled his attention from the vines and tried to get a read on the place. Force-sense was habitual, automatic, the way breathing was habitual — he didn't usually notice himself doing it. He noticed it now because it returned something strange. Not nothing. Not a wall. The Force was here, present, available in the way electricity was available in a live wire. He could feel potential. He could feel energy.

He could not feel anything else.

The pressure behind his sternum had been building since they crossed into the lower atmosphere. He'd logged it as tension, mission focus, the normal tightening before something difficult. Walking down this street, he understood it wasn't tension. It was his body registering an absence it couldn't name.

"Desh," he said.

Desh had his chrono out already. He held it up without turning around. The second hand was skipping — not smoothly sweeping but jumping, half-second gaps appearing and closing in irregular rhythm.

"Started when we crossed the ridge," Desh said. "Point-seven seconds per hour on the ground." He pocketed the chrono. "I also have a grief I can't account for. Sitting right here." He touched his sternum, not dramatically, the way you'd locate a pulled muscle. "No object. No reason. Just there."

Kael looked at him. "Same."

Sera's hand had moved to her lightsaber without her appearing to notice. Ben watched her consciously move it away.

"The Force is present," Sera said. Her voice was steady and careful. "But I cannot locate north."

Ben didn't know what she meant for a moment. Then he did. Force-sense had a quality of orientation, of *this way* and *not that way*, of responses that had weight and direction. What he was receiving from the Force right now had neither. He could reach into it and feel energy respond to his attention, and that response had no axis. It wasn't pointing anywhere. It wasn't telling him anything. It was simply *present*, the way static was present.

He walked to the center of the square.

The vines on all four sides were tracking toward shadow. Flowers he didn't recognize bloomed in the deep angle where two buildings met, their petals open to darkness. The market awning's shadow fell at the wrong angle for the time of day. Desh's chrono was in his pocket but Ben could feel the drift the way you felt a ship's gravity plates cycling slightly off — not pain, just wrongness that accumulated.

He stopped in the square's center, planted his feet, and reached.

The Force came.

Raw and immediate, the way a live current came when you closed a circuit. Energy, presence, potential — all of it there, all of it responsive to his attention. His training fired. Instinct reached for the familiar registers: the warm pull toward light-side clarity, the cooler edge of dark-side warning, the moral texture that had always been there the way gravity was always there, the baseline by which everything else was measured.

Nothing.

Not absence. Not silence in the way a room was silent. The Force had no axis. His trained instincts fired into the energy and returned with responses that had no direction, no valence, no comparison. Every skill he had for reading the Force was a skill built on the assumption that the Force had a structure to read. That structure was not there. The ground of his perception had dropped out from under him and the Force was still there, the energy was still there, and he could not tell up from down in it. He could not tell anything from anything.

He pulled back.

His arms felt hollow. Not painful — hollow, like the bones had been replaced with something lighter than they should be. His own heartbeat was suddenly loud, and behind it, his thoughts.

That was the part he hadn't expected.

Jedi trained in the Force for years before they understood how much of their mental quiet came from it. The background orientation, the gentle dampening of the thoughts that were loudest and worst, the sense that moral intuition was available if needed — it was infrastructure you stopped noticing. You stopped noticing it the way you stopped noticing ship gravity until it failed.

His thoughts were loud. Specific. Unmediated.

*Eleven thousand people*, he thought, and the thought came with nothing to soften it. No Force-sense context, no trained equanimity, no available distance. Just the number and what it meant, at full weight, with no cushion.

"Ben." Torin's voice was even. The kind of even that took effort.

"I'm fine," Ben said. His voice came out normal. He was grateful for that.

"You reached," Sera said. Not an accusation. An observation.

"I did."

"And?"

He looked at the flowers blooming in the dark angles of the square, the vines tracking toward shadow, the market awning casting its wrong-angled shadow across the duracrete.

"It's there," he said. "All of it. And none of it."

Torin put a hand briefly on his shoulder — warm, present, the simple weight of another person — and then moved to study the building faces with Sera. Kael stayed where he was, watching the vines move. Desh pulled out his chrono again, noted the drift, put it away.

Ben stood in the center of the square.

The Force moved through him and around him, responsive and present and structureless, and his thoughts were his own, all of them, including the ones he'd never had to hear clearly before. Including the thought that Veraine had done this to eleven thousand people as a test. Including the thought that Veranthos had a population of two million.

Including the thoughts the Force usually softened.

All of them, loud, in the silence where the lattice had been.
