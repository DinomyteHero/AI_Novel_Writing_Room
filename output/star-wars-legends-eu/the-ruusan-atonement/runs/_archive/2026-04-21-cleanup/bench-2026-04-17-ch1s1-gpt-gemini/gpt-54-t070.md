His guard came up late.

Not slow. Not sloppy. Late by half a beat, which in a training salle full of polished stone and humming blades might as well have been a kilometer.

The practice saber cracked across Ben’s left shoulder with a sharp sting that bit through tunic and sweat. He hissed, pivoted away on instinct, and brought his own blade around in a tight blue arc that should have covered the opening.

Should have.

His partner had already stepped clear.

Ben reset his stance, weight balanced on the balls of his feet, blade high. Across from him, the other Knight lowered his training saber a fraction, waiting. Respectful enough not to press. Smart enough not to assume the hit was luck.

Ben rolled his shoulder once. The contact point burned.

Three times this week.

He hated patterns like that.

“Again?” the Knight asked.

Ben gave him a thin smile. “You asking or gloating?”

A snort. “Asking. You looked distracted.”

Distracted. Sure. Better than unstable, haunted, or apparently unable to manage the basic trick of not getting tagged in his own Temple.

He circled left, boots whispering over the salle floor. The overhead lights cast clean white bars across the room. Along the walls, inactive remotes sat in their racks, and beyond the broad archways the Temple’s evening quiet pressed in—distant footfalls, ventilation, the faint layered presence of hundreds of lives under one roof.

Usually that steadied him.

Tonight it only gave the wrongness more room to stand out.

The Force dipped around him again, not weaker, not absent. Off-key. A note bent low enough to pull his timing with it. His body expected one thing. The current under it gave him another.

The Knight moved.

Ben met the first strike, caught the second on a short parry, and slid inside for a body shot. Their training blades snapped and hummed in quick blue flashes. Ben drove him back two steps, turned his wrist, and cut low.

Then the flat note hit again.

For an instant his sense of distance went wrong. Not by much. Just enough.

His opponent’s blade tapped his ribs.

Then his thigh.

“Point,” the Knight said.

Ben let his saber drop. “You’re very committed to my education tonight.”

“That’s two.”

“I can count.”

The Knight angled his head. “You sure you’re all right?”

Ben thumbed off his blade. The hum died, and the room got larger in the silence. Sweat slicked his palms. His shoulder throbbed where the first hit had landed.

“Long day,” he said. “If I let you embarrass me much more, people will start talking.”

“People already talk.”

“Then I’m protecting your reputation. Generous, really.”

That got a brief grin. The Knight deactivated his own saber but didn’t clip it to his belt yet. “Want to call it and try again tomorrow?”

Ben did not want to try again tomorrow. He wanted the Force to stop sounding wrong in his head, wanted his reflexes back, wanted one day lately where he wasn’t measuring himself against glitches no one else seemed to hear with the same volume.

Instead he flexed his left hand and made a show of working the shoulder. “I’m done. If I lose a rematch, I have to fake a serious injury, and that’s paperwork for both of us.”

The Knight laughed once and clipped the saber to his belt. “Get some sleep.”

That was the standard advice now. Rest. Meditation. Centering. As if all Ben needed was an extra hour and a calmer attitude.

“Bold diagnosis,” Ben said.

“Worked for me.”

Lucky you.

He kept that part behind his teeth. “Thanks.”

The Knight gave him a last look—curious, not prying—then turned and headed for the door. His boots faded down the corridor. A moment later the salle belonged to Ben and the thin mechanical whisper of the ventilation system.

He stood still until he was sure the other man was gone.

Then he exhaled through his nose and rubbed a hand over his face.

Long day, he’d said.

Technically true. In the same way a hull breach was a draft.

Ben crossed to the edge of the mat, set his deactivated saber on a bench, and rolled both shoulders. The left one complained. Good. Pain he understood. Pain had edges. You could work around edges.

He stepped back into the center of the floor and settled into Soresu opening stance without igniting the blade.

Again.

His hands moved through the first sequence. Guard high, turn, cut, recover. The motions came clean. No hitch. No lag. He pivoted into the next form, then the next, feet placing themselves by long habit, knees loose, spine straight. Years of drills in this room and rooms like it had burned the patterns in deep enough that he could run them half asleep.

He pushed faster.

The empty hilt traced silent lines through the air. Elbow close. Wrist aligned. Step, turn, stop. He reversed into a Djem So transition, then broke it apart and repeated the entry three times, looking for the flaw.

Nothing.

No missed line. No stumble. No drag in the shoulder beyond the hit he’d earned.

Ben blew out a breath and started again, harder. Sweat cooled on his back, then returned. His pulse climbed. The salle floor took each step with a soft thud. The muscles in his forearms tightened and released in exact sequence. Body working. Training intact.

He came out of the final turn and froze.

His body was fine.

That should have been reassuring.

It wasn’t.

He lowered the hilt slowly. The Temple beyond the walls pressed at his awareness in layers he’d known since childhood—meditation chambers, transit shafts, durasteel bones under polished stone, Jedi at study, Jedi at rest, all of it woven through the old currents that usually made this place feel anchored. Safe. Familiar.

Tonight the same currents carried that bent note underneath everything.

Not loud. Constant.

Ben closed his eyes.

He had spent enough years around Jacen to know the difference between danger and mood. Enough years around Abeloth to stop dismissing wrongness because everyone wanted normal back. Those crises had left marks on the Order. On the Force too, maybe. That was the line people used. Residual disturbances. Echoes. Background noise after too many galaxy-scale disasters in too few years.

Background noise didn’t make him miss.

Background noise didn’t slide under trained reflexes and tug them off by half a heartbeat.

He opened his eyes and stared at the far wall, at the pale stone scored with a thousand harmless training strikes.

Jacen had always carried distortion with him, though Ben had needed years to name it. Not darkness in the storybook sense. Something misaligned at the base of things, hidden under discipline and certainty. Abeloth had been worse. Not bent, but unmade. Being near her had taught Ben that the Force could go wrong in ways the old lesson texts didn’t prepare anyone for.

This wasn’t either of those.

Too thin. Too wide. Less like a presence than damage in the supports under the floor.

His fingers tightened around the hilt.

And there it was. The part he hadn’t wanted to say even in his own head. This wasn’t memory riding him. Not nerves. Not old scars deciding to pick a bad week. He knew what trauma looked like in himself. It usually arrived with faces, with places, with the sharp little hooks of memory waiting to catch.

This had no face.

Just that flat, dragging note under everything, and the ugly fact that he was the one hearing it loud enough to lose sleep and points in a sparring match while most of the Order treated the post-Abeloth static as weather.

He laughed once, without much humor.

Great. Either he was uniquely perceptive, or he was finally becoming the cautionary tale every overworked Jedi secretly expected to become. Ben Skywalker: too much family history, not enough sense to stop before the cracks showed.

He set the hilt on the bench, then picked it up again a second later. Restless energy crawled under his skin. Sitting with this would mean thinking, and thinking would mean the same useless loop he’d run every night for the past week. Meditate. Listen. Find nothing he could hand to someone else in a report without sounding like he needed mandatory leave.

So he moved instead.

A slower set this time. Basic forms. No saber. Bare hands cutting through the air, each strike ending where it should. Heel planted. Turn from the hips. Keep the line tight. The repetition pared his thoughts down to motion and count, and for a few minutes that helped.

Then the wrongness brushed him again.

Not stronger. Closer.

He stopped with one hand half-raised, every muscle keyed. The room stayed empty. White light. Stone. Benches. His own breathing. The taste of salt at the corner of his mouth where sweat had run.

Nothing to fight. Nothing to point at.

That was almost the worst part.

He wiped his palm on his tunic and paced once across the salle, then back. His boots echoed now that the room was still. He tried, out of stubborn habit, to lay the standard calming pattern over his breathing. Four counts in. Hold. Four out.

The note remained.

Fine.

If he was alone in this, then he was alone in it. He could keep testing. Keep narrowing it down. Keep his mouth shut until he had something better than I think the Force is misbehaving in a way that reminds me of two of the worst experiences of my life.

That sounded healthy already.

Ben turned toward the door as footsteps approached in the corridor. Not the drifting pace of someone passing by. Measured. Direct. Boots on stone, familiar enough that his body marked them before his mind finished catching up.

He straightened and reached for the towel draped over the bench, more to have something in his hands than because it would help.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
