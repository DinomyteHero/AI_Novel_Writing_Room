Ben’s shoulder met the training blade a half-second late.

The strike still landed with enough force to sting through the padded tunic, a flat smack that jolted him sideways on the mat. The saber in his hand came up a beat after it should have. Too late. Again.

His opponent stepped back into guard, blade humming a clean, bright line in the salle’s dim light. Ben caught the motion, but not fast enough to matter. He took one breath, reset his feet, and told himself the delay was nothing. Fatigue. A bad angle. One more bad habit stacked on top of too many late nights.

The problem was that he knew the beat he’d missed. He knew it the way he knew the difference between a cut and a bruise. The world had gone one note wrong, just enough to throw off the sequence before his body could correct it.

Third time this week.

That thought came with a dry edge of mockery that made the hit sting worse. Three times, Skywalker. Try to keep up with your own reflexes.

He lifted his blade again. “Again.”

The Knight obliged without comment. Respectful. Efficient. No need to make it awkward by asking whether Ben was off his game, which meant the answer was already obvious.

They moved. Step, parry, pivot. Ben caught the first exchange cleanly, then the wrongness slid through the back of his awareness again—thin and sour, not a sensation so much as an absence where timing should have been. A note played flat in the middle of a familiar tune.

His next block arrived half a heartbeat late.

The training blade kissed his shoulder again.

Ben swore under his breath and broke off the sequence before frustration made him sloppy. He deactivated his saber with a hiss and clipped it to his belt before the Knight could offer another round.

“Done?” the Knight asked.

“For now.” Ben rolled his shoulder once, feeling the protest spread under the fabric. “I’m starting to think your aim is improving.”

The Knight snorted, and that was almost a laugh. “Or yours is getting worse.”

“Careful,” Ben said. “I’m still armed with embarrassing statistics.”

That earned a brief grin. The Knight lowered the practice blade and saluted him with the hilt, then turned toward the door. Ben watched the figure go, boots sounding dull on the polished floor, and let the salle swallow the last of the company with visible relief.

He waited until the door sealed behind the Knight before he let his expression drop.

The room held its own steady noise: the low reverb of the ventilation, the soft buzz from inactive training emitters, the faint scrape of his boot when he shifted his weight. The Temple always made its own presence known, even when the corridors outside were quiet. Here, in the training level, that deeper current of other lives and other minds pressed at the edges of his awareness like a crowd murmuring behind a closed bulkhead.

Tonight it all sat wrong.

Not louder. Not darker. Wrong.

He flexed his hand around the saber hilt, then set the weapon on the floor at the edge of the mat. Sweat had gathered at his palms and made the grip tacky. He wiped his hand on his tunic, crossed the salle, and started pacing.

No blade. No opponent. Just the floor beneath his boots and the memory of the sequence he’d just botched.

One step.

Pivot.

Guard high.

Counter low.

He moved through the form in clean, practiced motions. Empty-handed, the pattern should have been simple. He’d trained these passes until they lived in muscle and bone. The body knew them. The body always knew them.

That was the irritating part.

He cut a tight arc, shifted his weight, and snapped his forearm into the line of a block that wasn’t there. His foot landed exactly where it should. His shoulders tracked. His breathing stayed even. His muscles were fine.

The beat that failed was elsewhere.

Ben stopped in the middle of the mat and stared at his own hands.

He hated that this was where his mind went first: not injury, not lack of sleep, not the dozen banal excuses the Temple medics would probably offer if he walked in and complained. Those would have been easier. Human. Temporary. Fixable with rest and less pride.

Instead he had the wrongness in his head again, that hard little hitch in the Force that had started a week ago and only sharpened since. It didn’t carry shape or color or any of the dramatic nonsense people loved to assign to mysteries. It was just there, a structural drag in the current, like a span that should have held and didn’t. He moved through it. The Force moved through him. Between those two things, timing snagged.

Jacen had felt like this at the edge of something impossible. Abeloth had felt like this too, all pressure and fracture and a sense that the rules were being tugged loose at the seams. Ben had enough experience with both to know his memory wasn’t being theatrical.

That comparison made him swallow hard enough to taste copper at the back of his throat.

No. Not comparison. Recognition.

He started moving again before the thought could settle. Faster this time. He drove through the opening drill, hands and feet snapping from one position to the next. Then he repeated it. And again.

The salle grew warm around him. Sweat darkened the collar of his tunic and ran under the band at his wrist. His boots squeaked faintly when he pivoted too hard on the polished floor. He ignored the protest in his shoulder and kept going until his lungs worked harder than his irritation.

Still the same delay.

Not every time. That would have been easier to name. Instead, the failure came on the edges, when his focus touched the Force for timing and balance. If he stayed entirely inside his body, the sequence held. If he let the Force in, the beat slipped.

Ben slowed, then stopped.

That was new. Bad new.

He bent forward, hands on his thighs, and dragged in air that tasted faintly of recycled filter and sweat. The Temple’s background presence brushed at him again, patient and distant, but the drag inside it didn’t belong to fatigue. It didn’t belong to bruised ribs or a shoulder hit or lack of sleep.

It belonged to the Force itself.

He straightened slowly, and for a moment he just stood there with the empty mat under his boots and the humming walls around him.

The room felt too large.

Not because it had grown. Because the part of him that usually filled it had gone thin.

Ben dragged a hand over his face, then let it fall. He could hear himself breathing. He could hear the faint clink of his saber where it rested on the floor. He could hear the distant movement of the Temple above and below, the layered life of a place built to hold a thousand quiet emergencies and one or two catastrophes at a time.

No one else had said anything. No one else seemed to notice. That was the part that made his teeth want to grind. He’d tried bringing up the disturbance once, carefully, in the way that made it sound like a question rather than a complaint. The answers had been mild and sensible and useless.

Recent crises. Residual strain. Post-Abeloth echoes. Keep meditating. Get more sleep.

As if sleep could organize the galaxy back into working order.

He pushed off the mat and started pacing again, this time with a sharper purpose. If the Force was the variable, then strip it out. Test the machinery. Check the body, check the reflexes, see what remained.

Form one.

He ran the opening without calling the blade.

Form two.

The turn came clean.

Form three.

His left foot landed half an inch inside the line, corrected, and carried him through.

He kept going. Faster. Harder. A sequence built for blade work and a partner who wanted to hit him. He made the blocks, the feints, the transitions, every one of them with the precision of a drill manual and none of the help he usually drew from the Force when he crossed the threshold from thought into action.

The body held.

The body held.

The beat still came late when he reached for that other layer, the one that ought to have folded timing and instinct together without effort.

He stopped so abruptly that his boot skid a fraction on the mat.

There.

Not him. Not his muscles. Not his training. The gap sat somewhere outside all of that, a stutter in the thing he touched when he moved as a Jedi instead of as a man with a stick and good balance.

He stared at the empty space in front of him and let the conclusion settle where it wanted.

The wrongness was structural.

Not emotional residue from too many losses. Not grief. Not stress. Not another scar from the business with Jacen or Abeloth lingering in his head and making patterns where there were none. This was deeper. Older-feeling. The sort of failure that sat under the floorboards and made the whole room lie to anyone standing in it.

He disliked how fast his mind accepted that.

He disliked even more that part of him had already known.

Ben exhaled through his nose and looked down at his hands again. One palm was slightly reddened from the saber grip. The other had a smear of sweat across the knuckles. Ordinary. Solid. Real. It was a relief to have something that did not argue back.

“Great,” he muttered to the empty salle. “Nothing wrong with me. That’s comforting.”

The words sounded thin, but they kept the unease from tightening around his ribs. Dry humor had always worked better than panic. Panic was noisy and made promises. Humor at least admitted the situation was ugly.

He bent, picked up the saber, and clipped it back to his belt.

The training floor had gone quieter. Not silent. The Temple never quite managed that. But the far-off thrum of traffic through the levels below seemed more distant now, and the air had cooled enough to slick his sweat against his skin. He wiped his hands on his tunic again and started walking a slow circuit of the room, giving his thoughts something to do besides circle the same bad question.

How long had he been missing it?

Three failures this week meant three chances for someone to see the pattern and three chances for everyone to decide he was pushing too hard. The Order had enough actual problems without Ben Skywalker wandering in to announce that the Force had started acting up around him like a bad power coupling. He could already hear the patient concern, the practical advice, the careful dismissal tucked inside both.

He was not in the mood to be managed.

That thought brought a brief, crooked edge to his mouth. Of course he wasn’t. He was a Skywalker. Managing himself poorly was practically a family tradition.

The humor faded as quickly as it came, leaving the salle bare again. He crossed to the far wall, turned, and looked back at the mat. The hit mark on his shoulder still ached under the fabric. He could feel it every time he rolled the joint.

The ache was honest. The wrongness wasn’t. That was what made it infuriating.

His pace slowed. The air carried the faint scent of heated equipment and old polish, and his damp shirt clung between his shoulder blades. He had just started another circuit when the Temple’s background hum broke under the sound of boots in the doorway.

Ben’s head came up.

Luke stood framed in the entrance, datapad in hand, posture already set in the easy, purposeful way that said he hadn’t come to watch a training session. He had come with somewhere else to be. His face held that expression Ben knew too well for comfort, the one that meant the day had just acquired a mission.
