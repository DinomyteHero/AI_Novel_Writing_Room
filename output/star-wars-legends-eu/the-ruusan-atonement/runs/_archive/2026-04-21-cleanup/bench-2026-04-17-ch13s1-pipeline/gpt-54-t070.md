The world filled the viewport in clean bands of blue and green, cloud systems curled over broad seas, and the landmasses showed the soft temperate spread of a place people settled because it made saying yes easy.

Ben kept one hand on the rail below the transparisteel and watched the image swell as *The Steady Returns* dropped from high orbit. Behind him, Desh sat at the sensor console with a frown that had been deepening for the last six minutes.

“Surface reads stable,” Desh said. “Atmosphere’s breathable. Water tables intact. Urban grid still present. No major tectonic disruption.”

“So we got lucky,” Torin said.

Desh looked over his shoulder. “No.”

That single word sat in the cockpit harder than a paragraph would have.

Sera had both hands braced on the back of the copilot’s chair, eyes fixed on the data stream. “Orbital returns are ghosting.”

Ben glanced at her. “Ghosting how?”

“Redundant structures where none should exist. Thermal bleed in dead zones. Mass echoes delayed by fractions that do not repeat cleanly.” Her voice stayed level, which for Sera meant things were bad. “The instruments disagree with themselves.”

Kael, slouched near the hatch with his arms folded, said, “That seems rude of them.”

Ben almost smiled. “Good. I was worried the collapse might start respecting procedure.”

No one laughed. Fair enough.

He looked back out at the world. Seen from orbit, it was almost offensively normal. Sunlight flashed on water. Wind pushed a veil of cloud over a mountain range. A settlement near the terminator line resolved in pale geometric blocks and dark ribbon streets.

Then he saw the birds.

A flock lifted from a stand of trees, wheeled, and split into two matching arcs that mirrored each other too perfectly, each turn held a fraction too long. Not natural flight. Not even bad natural flight. Like someone had watched birds once and rebuilt them from memory.

His grip tightened on the rail.

“You seeing that?” he asked.

“I’m seeing several versions of that,” Desh said. “Visual feed is clean. Scan correlation says it shouldn’t be.”

Torin pushed up from his seat and came to the viewport. “Still no life signs?”

“Nothing reliable.”

Torin exhaled through his nose. “A settlement world with weather, water, standing structures, and no one home. That narrows it down to about twelve awful options.”

“Eleven,” Kael said. “One of them would smell worse from orbit.”

That got a short sound from Ben, not quite a laugh. His stomach stayed tight.

He reached for the Force out of habit, just enough to brush the world ahead of them, expecting the usual first impressions—life, motion, the broad orientation of things.

He got static at the edge of reach. Not absence. Refusal.

Ben straightened.

Sera’s head turned toward him at once. “You touched it.”

“Barely.”

“And?”

He kept his eyes on the viewport. “Let’s land.”

The ship broke through the lower cloud deck. The settlement came up fast: low buildings, open square, narrow streets, roofs half claimed by vines. No fire damage. No crater scars. No clear sign of battle. It looked abandoned the way a holodrama set looked abandoned, with all the details in place and none of the life.

Desh settled them into a broad street between two rows of intact structures. Repulsors whined down. For a moment the cockpit held the muted tick of cooling systems and nothing else.

Ben rolled his shoulders once and headed for the hatch.

The ramp lowered with a hydraulic hiss. Daylight spilled into the cargo bay.

Air moved in from outside, mild and damp, carrying turned soil, wet stone, and a floral sweetness that hit half a beat late, as if scent had needed to make up its mind.

Ben stepped to the top of the ramp and stopped.

The street ahead traced a clean line through the settlement square. Permacrete slabs sat cracked but not broken apart. Balconies clung to second stories. Window glass flashed in unbroken panes. Ivy draped one wall in a dense green curtain.

On the north face. In full shadow.

Flowers opened beneath an overhang where the sun never touched. Across the square, another vine had threaded itself upward, then bent back down toward the ground, tendrils burying into a planter box on the second floor as though gravity were a suggestion under review.

No birdsong. No insects. Wind stirred leaves, and the leaves answered with a dry whisper that sounded out of time with the motion.

Torin came up beside him. “I miss ordinary ruins.”

“Give it a minute,” Ben said. “This place may still get creative.”

He took one step off the ramp onto the street and opened himself to the Force.

The reach went out.

And went nowhere.

Not nowhere as in empty. Nowhere as in no purchase. He touched power, immense and immediate, a whole world’s worth of living current and dormant weight and motion waiting in all directions—and none of it arranged into anything a mind could hold. No grain. No pull. No distinction. Light and dark had been categories his whole life, as basic as up and down.

Gone.

His chest jerked with a sharp, ugly lurch, like gravity had shifted sideways and his body had received the message before the street did. He caught himself with one foot back on the ramp, hand snapping to the side rail.

Sera inhaled hard behind him.

Torin swore under his breath.

Kael said nothing at all, which was louder than usual.

Ben kept his face still because Skywalkers were apparently required by law to look competent in public. He tried again, more carefully this time, the way he would test a damaged bridge.

Same result. Energy without direction. Presence without texture. He could touch it, but touch meant nothing when there was no shape to touch.

The handrail was gone.

“What happened?” Desh asked from behind them.

Ben looked back. Desh stood on the ramp with one hand near the blaster at his hip, eyes moving over the square, reading people instead of currents in the Force.

Ben let go of the rail. “You ever been on a stair and expected one more step than there was?”

Desh frowned. “Yes.”

“It’s that,” Ben said. “For everything.”

Sera moved past him onto the street. Her posture locked at once, every line of her body braced against something invisible. “This is total.”

Torin came down after her, slower than Ben had ever seen him move. “No containment edge. No partial integrity.”

“Field test,” Kael said from the foot of the ramp.

Ben turned. Kael was crouched by the nearest wall, studying a thick vine that had grown from a crack in the permacrete, crossed three meters of open air, and disappeared into a drain grate. He touched one leaf with two fingers.

The vine recoiled.

Not from touch. From him. It pulled back in a quick rippling motion, then stopped and sagged, as if it had forgotten why it moved.

Kael looked at his hand. “That’s encouraging.”

“No,” Sera said. “It is not.”

Desh stepped off the ramp and reached into a pocket. “Encouraging or not, I’d like numbers.”

He flipped open his chrono. The tiny projected dial sprang up over his wrist. Ben watched the second hand sweep, pause, jump ahead two marks, hold, then slide back one.

Desh’s face changed.

That hit Ben harder than the skipping chrono. Desh wasn’t Force-sensitive. He had no frame for the missing handrail, no lifetime of instinct punching into blank space. But something had found him anyway. His shoulders lowered a fraction, and the calm practicality he wore like armor took a dent, as though some private loss had crossed the room and put a hand on him.

“You all right?” Ben asked.

Desh kept watching the dial. “No.” He swallowed once. “No, and I can’t tell you why.”

Torin turned toward him. “What do you mean?”

Desh snapped the chrono shut, then opened it again immediately, checking whether the problem had obeyed him out of respect. It had not. “I mean my watch is lying to me, the street feels wrong under my boots, and I’ve got…” He searched for the word and failed. “Something missing. I don’t know what.”

Sera’s stare stayed fixed on the square ahead. “The lattice was not only for us.”

Ben looked at her. Under normal conditions he would have caught more than the words—intent, balance, all the tiny cues the Force sanded into clarity. Here he had to do it the hard way. Tight shoulders. Careful breathing. Voice under control by effort. She was holding the line because that was what Sera did when the ground failed.

Torin folded his arms, then unfolded them. “Veraine did this on a settled world.”

“Looks settled,” Kael said. “Past tense seems safer.”

Ben scanned the buildings again. Doors shut. Windows dark. A child’s repulsor toy lay half buried in creeper growth near a fountain rim. Nobody had cleared it. Nobody had stepped around it. The square had the frozen order of a place vacated all at once, except the plants had kept working under rules no one had approved.

“This wasn’t collateral,” he said.

“No,” Sera said. “Calibration.”

The word landed cold.

Ben started toward the center of the square. The others moved with him, though the spacing between them had opened without anyone discussing it. Cohesion frayed at the edges here. Not panic. Something simpler and meaner. Every irritation showed up with the padding stripped off.

He stopped beside a broken chunk of permacrete the size of a cargo crate and hooked his fingers under one edge. Ordinarily he would have flicked it aside with the Force to test local resistance, maybe followed with a physical lift to compare. Here he used both hands and his legs.

The fragment rose, but the weight changed halfway up. First light, then dragging, then nearly nothing again. The shift was enough to throw him off balance. He set it down harder than intended. It struck the street with a flat crack and skidded two meters, though it should have stopped at once.

Desh watched it slide. “That wasn’t friction.”

“Nothing here is,” Ben said.

Torin gestured at the square. “She needed to know how far the effect propagated. What it touched. What kept working.”

“What broke first,” Sera added.

Kael stood, dusting his fingers on his trousers. “People.”

Silence.

Ben’s first response to that should have been the usual one. Compassion before calculation. The instinctive recoil that kept strategy from turning into arithmetic. Instead his mind moved in a hard clean line: Yes. People first. Because if Veraine could do this here and walk away, Veranthos was next, and at full scale there would be no contained failure, no test boundaries, no lucky distance. Entire populations caught in this blankness. Force-sensitives crippled. Non-sensitives stripped by something they would never have words for.

Then another thought came right behind it, just as clean.

The old Order built the prison. The old Order used living beings as parts. And now everyone else got to inherit the bill.

It had edge on it. More than edge. Anger without the usual pressure from the Force asking if he wanted to sharpen that before using it. He looked at the empty windows so he wouldn’t look at Sera or Torin. They were not the Jedi Lords who had done it. They were what survived them.

He heard his own voice before deciding to speak. “She was checking the dose.”

Torin’s head came around. “Ben—”

“She wanted to know how much she could remove without tearing the place apart physically,” Ben said. “How long matter holds. How life behaves. What the dead zone does to everyone inside it.”

Desh nodded once, curt, because practical truth was still practical truth. “That tracks.”

Sera’s expression hardened. “Then Veranthos is not a target of opportunity. It is the objective.”

Kael looked up at the flower box blooming in shadow. “That’s a cheerful thought.”

“No,” Ben said. “That’s the useful one.”

Torin took a step closer. “Useful and dangerous often travel together.”

“Thanks,” Ben said. “I grew up around Solos. We had warning labels.”

A brief snort escaped Desh. Torin managed half a smile and lost it again.

The square pressed in around them. Wind pushed through the street, carrying damp earth and the thick sweet smell of flowers growing where flowers should have failed. Somewhere metal tapped metal in an irregular pattern from inside a building, a loose sign or pipe shifting with no rhythm Ben could trust. The Force remained there, vast, immediate, waiting. No comfort in it. No correction.

He looked at his companions one by one and had to read them as if he were anyone else. Sera, rigid with discipline and probably furious at the shape of this place. Torin, already leaning toward the old reflex—take the burden, make the decision, spare everyone else the choice. Kael, alert now, survival instincts fully awake because the rules had stopped pretending to exist. Desh, bearing a wound with no source he could point to.

Ben stood in the middle of the square and listened for the usual internal nudge that sorted impulse from warning, anger from judgment, fear from caution.

Nothing came.

Only his own mind, clear and unsheltered.

In the silence of the de-structured Force, Ben heard himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
