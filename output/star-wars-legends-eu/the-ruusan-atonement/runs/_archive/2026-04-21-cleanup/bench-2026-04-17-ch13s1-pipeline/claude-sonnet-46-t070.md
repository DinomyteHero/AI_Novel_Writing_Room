The settlement world looked exactly like somewhere people still lived.

That was the problem.

Ben stood at the forward viewport as the *Steady Returns* dropped through the last of the cloud layer, and the planet spread below them in patient greens and blues — temperate, unhurried, the kind of world that showed up in colonization brochures. Farmland in careful squares. A river catching the afternoon light. He could see the rooftops of the main settlement from here, orderly rows of them, the streets between them geometrically sensible.

"Bioscan's giving me conflicting data," Desh said from the co-pilot's chair, not looking up from his board. "Atmospheric composition reads nominal. Thermal signatures consistent with a living settlement — heat differentials, cooking fires, power draw." He paused. "No individual life signs."

"Orbital sensors are ghosting," Sera said. She was standing behind Ben, her reflection doubled in the viewport glass. "I've run the sweep twice. The readings exist, then they don't. The instruments aren't malfunctioning — they're registering something that keeps changing."

"The birds," Kael said.

Everyone looked at him. He was pointing at the viewport.

Ben followed the line of his finger. A flock of birds — dark-winged, large — moved in a slow arc over the settlement's outskirts. Normal enough. Except there was another flock behind them, moving in the exact same arc, the same interval spacing, the same wing-beat timing. And another behind that. Identical. Mirrored in triplicate against the sky.

"Huh," Ben said. "That's new."

"That's not possible," Torin said.

"And yet." Ben turned from the viewport. "Take us down."

---

The ramp lowered onto a street that smelled like cut grass and warm permacrete and nothing else. No food smells, no machinery, no people. The settlement had been mid-sized — a few thousand residents, agricultural support infrastructure, the kind of place that existed to feed somewhere larger. The buildings were intact. Windows unbroken. A child's wheeled toy sat on the step of the nearest doorway, waiting.

Ben went down the ramp first.

The street was quiet in a way that pressed against the ears. Not the quiet of an empty place — the quiet of a place that had recently stopped. He could see where someone had been repairing a gutter. The tools were still laid out on the duracrete below the ladder, in order, as though the worker had simply stepped away.

He took three steps off the ramp and reached for the Force.

The reach fired. He felt it go out from him, the familiar extension of perception, the way it usually spread through a space and returned textured with information — life, intent, the faint moral weight of recent events. He felt it go.

Nothing came back.

Not emptiness. Not a void. The Force was *there* — he could feel the raw energy of it, present and enormous, like standing next to a generator that was running but producing no output. Potential without direction. Presence without shape. He reached again, harder, and got the same response: energy, yes, but no grain to it, no current to read, no suggestion of where anything was or what it meant.

The lurch in his chest was physical. His hand went to the doorframe of the ramp without his deciding to do it.

"Ben?" Sera's voice, two steps behind him.

"Give me a second."

He breathed through it. The Force was still there. He kept telling himself that. But the lattice — the organizing structure that turned raw Force into *information*, into moral intuition, into the quiet voice that said *left* or *careful* or *not this one* — was simply gone. Pulled out cleanly, like a support beam removed from a structure that was still, somehow, standing.

He let go of the doorframe.

"It's a full collapse," he said. "Not degraded. Not damaged. Gone."

Sera came down the ramp and stopped beside him. He watched her face go through three separate things in quick succession — professional assessment, recognition, something harder to name that she controlled before it finished forming.

"The others were partial," she said. "Even the worst node failures retained some structural coherence."

"This one didn't." He looked at the street. The tools laid out below the ladder. The child's toy. "She tested it here first."

Torin descended the ramp and stood in the street, turning slowly. "There's something wrong with the air."

"No there isn't," Desh said, coming down last, scanner in hand. "Air's fine. It's you." He was looking at his chrono. He held it up and they could all see it: the second hand moving in its sweep, then stuttering, then moving again, then stuttering. Not stopping. Stuttering. "Chrono drift. I've been tracking it since we hit the lower atmosphere." He looked up. "Whatever she did here, it's not just affecting the Force."

"The lattice isn't separate from physical space," Sera said. "The node architecture is embedded in—"

"I know," Ben said.

He didn't mean it to come out flat. It came out flat. He heard it and didn't apologize, which was itself unusual — normally the Force smoothed that kind of edge before it reached his mouth, the way it smoothed a dozen small frictions per day without him ever noticing. He noticed now.

Kael had moved off toward the nearest building. He was examining a climbing vine on the wall — dense, dark-leafed, growing across the shadowed face of the structure in long horizontal lines. He reached out and touched it. The vine contracted. Not dramatically, not like something with a nervous system recoiling — just a slow, deliberate withdrawal from his fingers, the way a plant will sometimes turn from heat.

"It's growing on the wrong side," Kael said. "All of them are. Vines on the shadow faces, flowers where the light doesn't reach." He didn't touch the vine again. "The settlement's been like this for weeks, minimum. This isn't new."

"She did this weeks ago," Torin said. "Before the cascade. Before we woke up."

"Field test," Ben said. "She wanted to know if a full collapse was stable. Whether the effect would hold or whether it would spread or collapse on itself." He looked at the rooftops, the orderly geometry of the streets, the intact windows. "It held. The settlement's still here. The buildings are still standing. The chrono drifts and the vines grow wrong and the birds fly in triplicate, but nothing exploded." He paused. "From her perspective, this worked."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Not Force-mediated weight — he'd spent his whole life reading emotional weight through the lattice, the way it amplified group feeling into something almost tangible. This was just silence. Air pressure. The absence of sound.

It felt heavier.

"If this worked," Sera said, "then Veranthos is the application."

"Yes."

"We need to—"

"Yes." Ben picked up a chunk of fallen permacrete from the street. Fist-sized, should have been light. It wasn't — or rather, it was exactly the right weight, but his hands expected the Force to make it lighter the way it always did, the unconscious constant adjustment he'd never known he was making. He held it for a moment and set it back down. "We're not going back to report this. We're going forward."

Torin looked at him. "The Order should know."

"The Order will know when we tell them after." Ben kept his voice level. "We know where she's going. We know what she can do. We know she's already done it once and it worked. If we pull back to report, she gets more time at Veranthos." He looked at Torin. "You know I'm right."

Torin was quiet for a moment. "I know you're not wrong," he said, which wasn't the same thing, and they both knew it.

The group spread out across the square in ones and twos, each of them processing it differently — Desh checking his scanner readings against the chrono drift with the focused attention of a man who wanted data instead of feelings, Kael moving along the building faces with his hands in his pockets, Sera standing still with her eyes closed in what Ben recognized as an attempt to reach through the lattice and finding nothing to reach with.

Ben stood in the center of the square and didn't try to reach anymore.

The absence was enormous. He'd expected it to feel like blindness — the loss of a sense. It didn't feel like that. It felt like the removal of a filter he'd never known was there. Every thought arrived at full volume, unedited, without the gentle redirection the lattice provided when something wasn't worth dwelling on.

His anger at the ancient Jedi Lords — the ones who'd built this seal and never told anyone what it cost — came in clear and hot and uncomplicated.

His fear for Sera, for Torin, for Kael, for Desh, for what Veranthos would do to all of them — came in without the Force's usual softening toward acceptance.

His willingness to use the survivors as tools if that was what it took, his cold calculation of their capabilities against the need — came in without the lattice's quiet suggestion that he examine that impulse more carefully.

The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones he'd always assumed the Force had helped him be better than.
