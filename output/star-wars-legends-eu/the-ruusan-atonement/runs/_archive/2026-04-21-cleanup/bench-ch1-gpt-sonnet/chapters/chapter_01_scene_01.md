Rendell’s training blade clipped Ben across the left shoulder—a sharp sting, a buzz of impact that rang through the padded tunic.

Ben was already moving. Or he should have been. The parry came a half-second late, precise and useless, cutting empty air where the strike had already passed.

He stepped back on reflex and killed his blade. The low hum died, leaving only the echo of the hit in his arm and the slap of boots on the mat.

Rendell lowered his own saber. “Point.”

“Obviously.” Ben rolled his shoulder once. The sting sharpened, then settled into a hot line through the muscle. Third time this week. A fine trend, if his goal was to start dropping exchanges to people he usually handled without breaking a sweat.

Rendell waited, practice blade angled down, perspiration darkening the collar of his tunic. “Again?”

Ben glanced at the hilt in his hand, at the scuff marks on the floor, at anything but the hollow in his own timing where the exchange had gone wrong. “Not tonight.”

Rendell tipped his head. “You sure?”

“Pretty sure,” Ben said. “Unless the Order’s revised lightsaber doctrine to include collapsing from lack of sleep as a defensive form.”

A short huff of amusement—courtesy, offered and accepted. Rendell thumbed off his training saber. “Praxeum night runs long.”

“Apparently it wins, too.”

That earned the smallest hint of a smile. “Get some rest, Skywalker.”

Ben lifted a hand. He didn’t add anything about having slept. Didn’t mention that sleep wasn’t the problem, and neither was the shoulder, and neither was the week. Rendell let the story stand, because Jedi were good at that when they chose to be.

The salle door slid open with a whisper of servos. Corridor noise spilled in—distant foot traffic, a lift chime, the faint whir of a cleaning droid somewhere down the hall—then cut off again as Rendell stepped through and the door sealed behind him.

Silence settled back over the room.

Ben drew a slow breath and dropped his arm to his side. The muscles between his shoulders loosened all at once, and the relief was immediate, quick, thin. Then the restlessness came back underneath it—needling, insistent—and standing still lasted maybe two seconds.

He paced once across the mat, then back.

The salle smelled of sweat, worn fabric, and the dry metallic tang from the emitters built into the practice racks. Familiar. His body knew the dimensions without looking: three strides to the wall, pivot, three back. He’d been moving in places like this since he was small enough to need patient instruction and repeated demonstration.

And under all of it, the rhythm kept missing.

Not gone. Late.

Ben stopped by the nearest rack and stared at the inactive sabers lined in their slots. He didn’t need one. This wasn’t about the hilt, and it definitely wasn’t about getting hit by Rendell, however much his shoulder wanted to make that the headline.

“Fine,” he muttered to the room. “Let’s do this the stupid way.”

He took position in the center of the mat, feet settling into stance by habit older than thought. His palm closed around the hilt, but he left the blade dark.

The first motions came clean. Guard high, turn, cut, recover. The hilt moved through his hands with familiar weight, as known as his own breathing. No crackle of energy, no humming blade to mark the pattern for him. Just muscle and timing and the map his body carried.

That was the point.

He flowed into the next sequence and let it run. The shoulder still stung. Sweat cooled under his collar. Somewhere beyond the salle walls someone laughed, brief and muffled, and the Temple went on being a place people lived in while Ben stood in the middle of a problem he couldn’t punch into simplicity.

The rhythm lagged.

He adjusted without thinking—every duelist did; timing was never perfect—but this was different. His body set the beat and waited for the answer that should have come with it, the support under instinct, and the answer arrived just late enough to trip over what he’d already done.

He corrected and kept moving.

Turn. Step. Guard across the centerline. The old butterfly pattern, stripped of the blade’s geometry but still present in the wrist and shoulder. He had it. For a few breaths he had it.

Then the wrongness tugged at the edge of his attention.

Not hard. It never had to be hard. Just enough to pull the next motion a fraction off, and the one after compensating for the one before, and by the third he was chasing the beat instead of riding it.

The hilt snagged against his own forearm.

Ben stopped so fast the last motion died halfway through. His fingers tightened on the grip until the ridges bit into his palm.

“No,” he said to the empty room.

His pulse was running too high for a man standing still. He reset his footing and started again at the midpoint—because going back to the beginning meant admitting this had gotten into his head, and he wasn’t in the mood to lose an argument to himself.

Cut. Recover. Pivot.

Late.

He forced the pace slower.

Late.

He tried matching his breath to it, surrendering his preferred tempo, meeting the thing where it was instead of where it belonged. For half a second the form clicked into place and the relief hit hard enough to feel like something.

Then the next beat slipped.

It was like reaching for a note he’d known all his life and discovering the song had moved it.

His hand dropped.

The room went very quiet. Not actually quiet—the Temple still breathed around him through walls and floors and distance—but the space immediately around him narrowed down to sweat cooling at the back of his neck, the ache in his shoulder, the ridged grip pressing into his palm.

Ben looked at the dark emitter. Then at his free hand.

His hands knew this form. His body knew it. Rendell hadn’t beaten him because he was tired, and he hadn’t clipped his own forearm because he was distracted, and he hadn’t been off all week because post-crisis exhaustion had finally decided to collect with interest. That excuse had been thinning for days. It had just run out entirely.

He set the hilt on the mat beside his boot and scrubbed a hand over his face.

Great. Excellent. Fantastic work, Skywalker. If he was going to have a problem with the Force itself, at least it had the decency to become undeniable in a training room instead of somewhere with something armed pointed at him.

He gave a short laugh that carried no humor.

Then he paced again, because sitting with it meant accepting it, and accepting it was going to open a list of questions he didn’t want yet. The route carried him to the edge of the mat and back, boots whispering over the floor. The wrongness stayed with him—not loud, not dramatic, just present. Half a beat off. Enough to keep every instinct braced for a stumble that kept almost arriving.

He should report it.

The thought came fully formed and unwelcome. Ben let it pass without answering. Report what, exactly? That the Force had been turning up late to his life for longer than he cared to count, and only now had it gotten rude enough to interfere where someone else could see? Very reassuring. No possible follow-up questions there.

He stopped in the center of the room and folded his arms, then unfolded them because the shoulder objected.

What bothered him most wasn’t the loss. Not even the witness. It was that part of him had still been trying to make this his fault right up until his own hands failed him on a form they could run half-asleep. Pride was annoying. Useful, sometimes. Mostly just annoying.

A soft hiss at the door cut across the thought.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
