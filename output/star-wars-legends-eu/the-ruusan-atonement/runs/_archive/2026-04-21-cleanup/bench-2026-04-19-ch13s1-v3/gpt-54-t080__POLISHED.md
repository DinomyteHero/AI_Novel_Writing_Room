Kolven filled the forward viewport in clean bands of blue and green, the sort of world travel brochures used when they wanted to suggest peace without promising boredom. White cloud streaks. Broad inland seas. Temperate belts. From orbit, it looked settled, healthy, alive.

Desh sat behind Ben with one boot hooked under the console brace, datapad angled to catch the cockpit glow. "Surface thermal spread is wrong," he said. "North continent reads twelve degrees warmer in the mountain shadows than in open lowland. South coast has frost signatures under direct sun."

Ben kept one hand on the co-pilot yoke and watched a river delta slide under them. "Maybe Kolven just likes variety."

"No," Desh said. "Variety still has patterns."

Sera stood behind the pilot seats, rigid even in descent. "Vegetation?"

Desh flicked through the scan returns. "Present. Dense in the settlement zone. But the chlorophyll response is inconsistent. Species clustering doesn't match the climate bands." He glanced up from the datapad. "Also, there should be avian movement at this altitude."

Ben looked for it. A world this green should have streaks over the forests, flocks lifting from water, something wheeling over the old fields.

Nothing crossed the sky.

He kept his voice light because everyone else in the ship had already packed enough grim determination for a small campaign. "Good. Birds make judgmental eye contact."

Kael, strapped in by the bulkhead, cracked one eye. "You say that like it's a joke."

"It is a joke."

"It's also true."

Torin laughed from the jumpseat, warm and easy. "Then for your peace of mind, Skywalker, perhaps the birds have all chosen to judge someone else."

Ben smiled, but his fingers stayed tight on the yoke. The world below looked too composed, too readable. The scans kept refusing the picture. His eyes said temperate colony world. The instruments said broken rules.

The Steady Returns banked toward the old settlement grid. Through the viewport Ben caught the geometry of streets under the green, pale lines and rectangles still holding their places after years of abandonment. Landing pads sat near the center like old coins pressed into dirt.

Desh leaned forward. "Pad integrity reads stable."

"Reads," Ben said.

Desh snorted. "That's the word that worries you?"

"Today? Top five."

The ship dropped through a thin layer of cloud. Buildings came up under them—low duracrete structures, a taller civic block near the square, storage sheds, a collapsed greenhouse dome at the edge of town. Most were still standing. Vines draped them in thick green curtains.

Ben frowned. One wall on the square wore a burst of white flowers all along its shaded side, while the sunlit face stayed bare concrete.

Sera saw it too. "There."

"I see it," Ben said.

"No," she said, voice flattening. "Watch."

He did. The vine nearest the corner moved.

Not in wind. There was barely any. It unwound one tendril from the sunlit edge, drew it back across the seam in the wall, and laid it into shadow. Slow enough to track. Fast enough to kill any comforting lie about tired eyes.

The ship touched down with a soft jolt.

No birds scattered.

Ben listened through the settling whine of the engines, waiting for some ordinary answer to present itself—the hiss of insects, the creak of branches, distant animal noise, the ambient life of a temperate world.

The silence sat over the landing pad like a sealed room.

He ran through shutdown checks by habit, because habit still worked. "Desh?"

"Atmosphere breathable," Desh said. "Trace particulates low. Pressure normal. Radiation normal." He paused. "Normal is getting insulting."

Torin unbuckled and rose. "Then let us go be insulted in person."

The ramp dropped. Air entered the hold, cool on Ben's face and carrying damp soil, old stone, and growing things. Real smells. Nothing ghostly about them. He unclipped his harness and headed down first.

His boots hit duracrete.

Solid. Cracked, but solid.

His body disagreed a half second later. Balance shifted under him with no movement in the ground. A tiny wrongness, enough to bring his shoulders tight and his stance wider. He reached for the Force on instinct, the same way another person might throw out a hand toward a rail in the dark.

Something answered.

Not nothing. Worse.

Power was there. Presence. Vast and immediate.

No axis.

His step checked hard enough that Sera nearly walked into his back.

"Ben?" Torin said.

He looked down at the pad beneath his boots, at a tuft of green punching through a fracture line. "I'm good."

That was close enough to true to use.

The others came down around him. Sera moved left at once, eyes on the nearest building front. Torin took the opposite side of the square with the loose readiness of a man entering a battlefield he refused to let frighten him. Kael descended last, paused at the bottom of the ramp, and scanned the street without touching any of the obvious sightlines. Desh came after him carrying the portable scanner and wearing the expression of a man whose equipment had become personally offensive.

The settlement spread out in stillness. Streets traceable under moss. Doorways gaped open. One transport shell lay nose-down against a wall and had been swallowed to the windows by ivy. The air was cool in the sun and colder in the shade, then warm again by Ben's right hand for no reason he could map. Sweat prickled once at the back of his neck and vanished.

He turned toward the wall with the flowers.

Up close, the wrongness got meaner. The blossoms opened in dense white clusters deep in shadow where no bloom should have wanted to be. On the sunlit side, leaves curled tight and pale. While he watched, a thin runner drew itself away from the light, not quickly, not dramatically, just with the calm persistence of something following a rule none of them knew.

Sera crouched beside it, careful not to touch. "Growth bias inverted."

Torin laid a hand on the wall, then withdrew it. "Cold here," he said, looking at the shaded flowers. He crossed to the sunlit face and blinked. "Warmer."

"By enough to feel through a glove," Desh said. He aimed the scanner, checked it, checked it again, then looked annoyed at the machine for agreeing with his skin. "That isn't local weather drift. That's—" He cut himself off and reached into a pocket. "No. Let's use something dumber."

He took out a chrono, thumbed it live, and held it where all of them could see.

The second hand advanced.

Paused.

Jumped two marks.

Held there.

Ben stared at it. Mechanical time, plain and stubborn, betraying itself in Desh's palm.

Kael looked up the empty street. "I liked this better when it was theoretical."

"Nobody liked it when it was theoretical," Sera said.

Kael's mouth twitched. "I stand corrected. You disliked it with tremendous discipline."

Torin let out a soft breath that might have been a laugh on any other world. Here it died early. He looked toward the row of houses at the far edge of the square. "No scavengers. No carrion birds. No nesting. This happened and life stepped back."

Ben listened again. Nothing. Even the wind moving through leaves seemed to stop before it turned into sound.

Under his ribs, pressure gathered. Grief, clean and heavy, but for nobody. No face rose with it. No memory. No loss he could name. It sat there anyway, blunt as a fist.

He looked at Desh.

Desh was watching the chrono with his jaw set and his shoulders held too still. Not panic. That wasn't his style. The grief was in him too, stripped of story and target, a wound without a point of impact.

Ben opened his mouth to say something useful. Reassuring. Grounding. Jedi enough to steady the room.

Nothing came with the usual shape. No clear line. No quiet internal pull toward the right words.

Just his own mind, reaching.

Desh looked up. "Don't."

Ben blinked. "Don't what?"

"Don't give me the calm Jedi version." Desh slid the chrono back into his pocket. "If this place is making me crazy, I'd rather hear that than get fed comfort on principle."

Ben almost said, You're not crazy.

It would have been reflex. It would also have been a guess.

So he went with the truth he had. "Your chrono skipped."

Desh nodded once. "That it did."

Torin stepped in before the silence could harden. "Then the question becomes whether the world is unstable in pockets or all the way down." He looked at Ben. "Can you take a reading?"

Sera's head came up sharply. She knew what he meant. So did Ben.

He didn't answer at once.

Every lesson he'd ever absorbed, every drilled instinct, every mission where the Force sat in his awareness as context and current and warning, all of it pressed forward. Reach. Center. Listen. Find the line through the chaos.

He planted his feet on the cracked pad and rolled his shoulders once. The breeze touched the side of his face, cool and damp, and carried the smell of crushed green things from somewhere deeper in town. A vine rasped over concrete behind Sera with the faint scratch of living tissue changing direction.

Kael folded his arms. "I hate this plan already."

"You hate all plans," Torin said.

"I reserve the right to rank them."

Ben let their voices fall away. The square stayed in view: shadow-bloom flowers, empty windows, green crawling where green should not have wanted to crawl. Desh stood a few meters off with the scanner in one hand and the other curled near his side, like he was resisting the urge to reach for a weapon against a problem that didn't care. Sera watched Ben with open concern she probably thought she had hidden. Torin watched him with trust.

That trust landed heavier than the grief.

He reached.

Not halfway. Not cautiously. He opened himself to it the way he had since childhood, the way breathing opened the lungs.

The Force was there.

Full. Immediate. Endless.

His perception fired into it and found nothing to brace against. No light. No dark. No pull toward one and warning from the other. No grain to read. No direction. Every trained response went out and came back empty of shape.

The ground of sense dropped out.

His knees unlocked. He caught himself before he went down, boots scraping on old duracrete.

"Ben," Sera snapped, moving one step toward him.

He threw up a hand without looking at her. Wait.

The Force remained—raw, vast, usable in the cruelest technical sense—but there was nothing inside it that told him what anything meant. No moral pressure. No instinctive edge between mercy and violence. No steadying current under thought. Just capacity.

A room with no walls and no markers. Motion without reference.

His pulse hit hard in his throat.

He had known fear with the Force before. Pain with it. Doubt. Temptation. Grief. Those had all happened against a living backdrop that answered back. Even silence had shape.

This had none.

He drew in air that tasted of wet stone and leaves and let it out through his nose, forcing the breath slow because his body wanted speed and speed would put him on the ground. His hands had gone cold. Somewhere to his right, Desh said something low and clipped. Ben didn't catch the words.

He tried again, because failing once and stopping had never been a useful family trait.

The second reach hit the same abyss and stripped the last excuse away. This was not interference. Not distance. Not injury. Nothing blocked the Force.

The structure he knew was absent.

His head rang with the effort of instincts that had nowhere to land. Reach for intent. Reach for balance. Reach for warning. Reach for the small internal resistance that kept ugly thoughts from sounding clean.

Nothing answered in that register.

He opened his eyes and discovered he'd closed them.

The square looked too bright. The white flowers in shadow were obscene now, flourishing where they shouldn't. A strand of vine withdrew from sunlight across a window frame with insect patience. Desh's scanner chirped and then emitted a flat error tone. Torin had shifted closer without crowding him. Sera stood taut as a drawn cable. Kael had gone very still, which on him meant alarm.

"What did you get?" Torin asked.

Ben swallowed. His mouth had gone dry. "It's there."

Torin waited.

Ben searched for the next words and found only blunt ones. "Nothing in it points."

No one spoke.

Desh glanced toward the empty sky over the settlement. "So this was a test."

Ben looked at the streets, the standing houses, the intact pads, the world left functional enough to make the violation clearer. Not ruin for ruin's sake. A demonstration. A proof of method.

"Yes," he said.

The word sat in the square and didn't echo.

He stood in a place where the Force offered no shelter from himself. No nudge. No soft pressure toward restraint. No quiet correction between impulse and action. Only his own training, his own judgment, his own choices, bare and unsupervised.

The silence pressed closer.

In it, Ben heard himself think without the safety net of Force intuition for the first time. The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softens.
