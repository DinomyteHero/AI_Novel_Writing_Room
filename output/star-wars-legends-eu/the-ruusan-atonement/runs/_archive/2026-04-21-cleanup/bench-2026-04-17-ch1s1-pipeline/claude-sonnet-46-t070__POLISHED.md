The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben's first coherent thought was *third time this week*.

He stepped back, weight dropping to his heels, and deactivated his saber. The hum died. His partner — Rendal, one of the newer Knights, good footwork, nothing memorable — held his own blade at rest and waited for the call.

"I'm done," Ben said.

Rendal glanced at the chrono on the wall. They had twenty minutes left in the session.

"Fatigue," Ben added, which was the kind of answer that closed conversations, and Rendal accepted it with a nod and a brief salute before heading for the door. Ben watched him go. He didn't feel guilty about the lie. Fatigue was close enough.

He stood alone in the salle for a moment, rolling his shoulder where the blade had landed. It wouldn't bruise. That wasn't the point.

The point was that he'd known the strike was coming. He'd read Rendal's weight shift, tracked the angle of the blade, already started the counter — and then the wrongness had spiked. That flat, off-key vibration threading through the Force for months now, and his guard had dropped a half-second behind where it needed to be. Not because he was tired. Not because Rendal was fast. Because something in the Force had gone briefly, horribly out of tune, and his body had hesitated before his mind could override it.

Third time this week.

He set his training saber on the equipment rack and paced to the center of the salle. The room was standard Temple issue — padded floor, mirrored walls, overhead panels running at evening intensity, the kind of space where you could spend six hours and feel nothing except the work. He'd spent a lot of hours here lately. More than usual.

Ben drew a breath and settled into the opening stance of Soresu, blade unignited. He moved through the form slowly, watching his own reflection without quite seeing it, tracking the internal geometry of each position. His muscles knew this. They'd known it since he was fifteen. The question he was testing wasn't whether his body remembered — it was whether the wrongness disrupted the memory itself, or just the timing.

The answer, as near as he could tell, was timing. Muscle memory held. Soresu's footwork, the defensive angles, the weight transitions — all of it was intact. What the wrongness disrupted was the Force component, the half-breath of prescience that told a Jedi where the blade was going before it moved. The wrongness didn't erase that. It *stuttered* it. Like a transmission hitting interference, the signal dropping out for a beat and then returning, leaving a gap where certainty had been.

He finished the form and started it again.

The Order had a name for what he was experiencing, more or less. Post-crisis residual disturbance. The aftermath of Abeloth, layered over everything else that had happened in the last decade — the Vong War's scars, Centerpoint, Malachor, the slow healing the Force had been doing since before Ben was born. Master Cilghal had a chart somewhere, tracking disturbance levels across the Temple population. The chart showed a gradual decline. Things were getting better.

Ben had looked at the chart. He'd nodded. He'd said nothing.

Because the chart was measuring something real, and the chart was also missing something, and he didn't know how to explain the difference without sounding like he was claiming to be more perceptive than everyone else. Which — maybe. He was a Skywalker. The family had a history of perceiving things other people missed, and an equally long history of that perception being a warning sign rather than a gift.

He stopped in the middle of a guard transition and stood still.

That was the thing he kept not looking at directly. The wrongness had a quality he recognized. Not from the post-Abeloth disturbances, which were raw and emotional, the Force equivalent of a city still smelling like smoke after a fire. This was different. Structural. Like the underlying architecture of the Force itself was — not damaged, exactly. More like something had been removed and the surrounding structure hadn't compensated yet.

He'd felt something adjacent to that twice before in his life. Once around Jacen, in the months before everything collapsed, when Ben had been young enough to dismiss it as his own instability. Once at the edges of Abeloth's influence, before they'd understood what she was — that sense of the Force's organizing principle being locally absent, raw ability persisting while everything else that made the Force *the Force* went quiet.

He hadn't told anyone. Both times, he hadn't told anyone, and both times the thing he'd felt had turned out to be real.

The Temple's evening hum pressed in around him — the distant pulse of hundreds of Force presences, the low resonance of the building itself, the barely audible ventilation cycling recycled air through the corridors. He focused on it the way Master Cilghal had taught him to focus on a patient's vital signs: not listening for anything specific, just taking inventory.

The wrongness was there. Quiet right now, below the threshold that disrupted his combat timing, but present. Like a note held just slightly below pitch, sustained so long that you'd almost convinced yourself it was the right note — until you heard the correct one and understood, retroactively, that something had been wrong all along.

He could write it up. File a formal report, request a consultation, let the Council route it through whatever process they used for anomalous Force perception reports. He'd done that once, two years ago, with a different concern, and the consultation had been thorough and professional and had concluded that his readings were consistent with documented post-crisis patterns.

Which was true. They were consistent with those patterns. They were also consistent with something else, and the Council's instruments didn't distinguish between the two, and Ben wasn't certain enough to push harder without evidence. That was the problem. He wasn't certain. He was a Skywalker with a history of Force trauma and a tendency to perceive darkness before it fully manifested, and there was no clean way to separate *reliable perception* from *pattern recognition shaped by damage*.

He exhaled through his nose and dropped the meditation stance.

The honest answer was that he didn't know which one it was. He suspected both. He was handling it alone because that was what you did when you weren't sure — you gathered more data before you asked someone else to take on the weight of your uncertainty. That was practical. That was responsible.

That was also, he was aware, exactly what a Skywalker told himself right before something went badly wrong.

He picked up his training saber from the rack and turned toward the door.

Luke was standing in the doorway. He had a datapad in one hand and the expression Ben had been reading since childhood — the one that meant he'd already mapped the situation and was waiting for Ben to be ready to hear it.
