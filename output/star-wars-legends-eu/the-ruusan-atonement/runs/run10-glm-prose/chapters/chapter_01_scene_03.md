The cockpit of the *Steady Returns* reeked of recycled air and the faint sweetness of fresh coolant. Ben had run pre-flight checks twice. Fuel cells at full charge. Shields calibrated. Navicomputer loaded with eight survey coordinates. The PARTNER SELECTION prompt still blinked amber on his datapad.

His thumb hovered over the Jedi Knight roster.

He swiped past Valin’s name. Too many lectures on proper procedure. Past Seha’s—she’d ask questions he couldn’t answer. The Bothan Knight, diplomatic escort by trade? Wrong mission profile. His fingers moved with mechanical precision, each rejection punctuated by a flicker of relief he refused to name.

The roster snapped shut.

Outside the sealed canopy, mechanics shouted across the Temple hangar. Their voices dissolved into the cavernous space, swallowed by acoustics that had swallowed a thousand briefings and a thousand departures. Inside, just instruments and silence.

Ben opened his encrypted contacts. Desh Rolan’s name sat third from the top.

He tapped the comm icon.

Two rings. Static crackled through the cockpit speakers, then a voice—tinny over the long-range channel, dry as Tatooine sand.

*“If this is about the cantina tab, I paid my half.”*

Ben’s grip on the datapad loosened. The tension in his shoulders bled out before he could stop it.

*“It’s not about the tab.”*

*“Then this is either a job or a funeral. You don’t call for anything in between.”*

*“Job. Outer Rim survey run. Eight systems, six to eight weeks. Terminus Station in two days.”*

Silence. Ben could picture Desh—leaning back in some cramped quarters, running calculations with the same methodical precision he’d once used to clear buildings on Corellia. The pause stretched long enough for doubt to creep in. Had he asked for too much? Assumed too much?

*“Survey,”* Desh said. *“For the Jedi.”*

*“For my father’s mapping initiative. Standard astrogation confirmation and anomaly logging.”*

*“Standard.”* The word landed flat, heavy with all the times Ben had used it to mean classified, dangerous, or both.

*“Meeting at Terminus. You in or out?”*

Through the canopy, a shuttle descended into the hangar, repulsors whining against the deck plating. Ben counted the seconds until—

*“I’ll be there.”*

No follow-up questions. No probing. Desh Rolan operated on a simple rule: if Ben called, the job was real. Details would come when they came. Or they wouldn’t. Either way, fine.

*“Two days,”* Ben said.

*“Two days. Don’t die before then—I’d hate to waste the fuel.”*

The comm clicked off.

Ben set the datapad aside. The PARTNER SELECTION prompt still blinked, but he ignored it. He’d file the amended roster after launch. By the time anyone noticed he’d listed a civilian consultant instead of a Jedi Knight, he’d be three sectors away.

His hands moved over the console. Repulsorlift controls, green. Thruster array, green. Navigation lock, confirmed. The *Steady Returns* sat in her berth, systems warm and waiting.

*“Control, Steady Returns requesting departure clearance.”*

*“Steady Returns, clearance granted. Vector seven-one-mark-four for orbital insertion.”*

*“Seven-one-mark-four, confirmed.”*

Gripping the yoke, Ben engaged the repulsors. The hum vibrated through the deck plating and into his boots. The ship lifted—smooth, controlled—and the Temple hangar fell away below. Mechanics scattered from the wash. A cargo loader beeped in protest. The cavernous space shrank to a rectangle of light, then a sliver, then nothing as the *Steady Returns* punched through the hangar’s magnetic seal and into Coruscant’s sky.

Towers clawed upward, their surfaces catching the late afternoon light. Traffic lanes split and merged in patterns memorized by every pilot who’d ever navigated the approach. Ben threaded the ship through the chaos without thought, hands steady on the yoke, eyes tracking the orbital markers.

Coruscant’s gravity released him in stages. The planet’s curve appeared at the edge of the viewport—a thin band of atmosphere giving way to black. Stars emerged, hard and cold, as the planet’s light fell behind.

Ben exhaled. Quieter here. No hangar echoes. No minds brushing against his in the Force. No weight of expectation pressing against his thoughts. Just instruments, the void, and the simple physics of flight.

He pulled up the navicomputer. Eight coordinates glowed on the display, strung along the Outer Rim like beads on a wire. The first lay six hours out at standard hyperdrive speeds. He entered the calculations, locked the trajectory, and reached for the hyperdrive levers.

The stars stretched.

Blue light flooded the cockpit—the impossible tunnel of compressed space-time no pilot ever truly got used to. The *Steady Returns* shuddered once, then steadied as the hyperdrive settled into its rhythm. Mottled walls of the hyperspace corridor streamed past, constant and hypnotic.

Ben closed his eyes.

He reached for the Force.

The wrongness had clung to him for weeks. Pressure behind the eyes. A drone in the back of his skull, unresolved. On Coruscant, it lived at the edge of perception—present but diffuse, like the sound of a crowd through several walls.

In hyperspace, Force perception changed. Distances warped. Connections stretched thin. Local phenomena blurred into the transit’s white noise. Every Jedi learned this early. The Force didn’t travel well through hyperspace. Whatever you felt on one end of the jump tended to fade by the middle.

Ben braced for the fade.

It didn’t come.

The wrongness remained—sharpened, not diminished. The drone in his skull resolved into something with edges. Something with direction.

His eyes opened.

The blue tunnel streamed past. Instruments read normal—hyperdrive stable, hull integrity nominal, no anomalies on any sensor band. The ship didn’t know anything was wrong. Only Ben could feel it, and what he felt made his hands still on the yoke.

The pressure wasn’t ambient anymore. Not a wound leaking into surrounding space. Not an echo bouncing off the galaxy’s walls. It was a vector. A bearing. The wrongness pulled him toward something specific—some point beyond charted coordinates, where the survey route ended and the maps went blank.

A compass needle snapping to true north.
