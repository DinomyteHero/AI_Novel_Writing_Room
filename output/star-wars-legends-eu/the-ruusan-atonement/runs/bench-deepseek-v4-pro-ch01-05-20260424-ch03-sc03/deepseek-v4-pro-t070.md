Ben's fingers found the delete key for the third time.

The cursor ate the line backward — *Master Skywalker, I'm filing this preliminary report to document an anomaly encountered during the survey of* — and the screen went blank again. The nav computer hummed its idle cycle. Somewhere aft, the Steady Returns' environmental system clicked through a pressure adjustment. Ben's shoulders had been climbing toward his ears for the past twenty minutes and he hadn't noticed until the muscle at the base of his neck started to ache.

He flexed his hands once and started typing again. Same first line. Same pause after *anomaly*. The word sat there on the screen, doing no work at all.

The wrongness pressed against the edge of his awareness — not the report, not yet. The other thing. The thing that had been tugging at him since the survey stop, directional and insistent, like a hand on his collar pulling him toward coordinates he hadn't entered. It didn't fade when he focused on the comm system. It didn't fade when he tried to write around it. It just sat there, patient, waiting for him to stop pretending the report was the priority.

He typed *encountered during the survey of* and stopped again.

The cockpit hatch slid open behind him. Desh didn't announce himself — he never did. The man moved like someone who'd spent too many years in places where doors made noise and had learned to compensate. Ben heard the soft scuff of boots on deck plating, then the faint creak of Desh leaning against the copilot's chair.

"You've been in here an hour," Desh said.

"Navigation review."

"Uh-huh." Desh's voice was flat in the way that meant he was being polite about not calling someone a liar. "And the comm buffer showing three deleted drafts?"

Ben's hand stopped above the keypad. He hadn't heard Desh pull the logs. He should have. The man was good, but Ben was a Jedi, and he'd been deep enough in his own head that a non-Force-sensitive had walked up behind him and read his screen.

"Four," Ben said. "That one's about to be the fourth."

He hit delete. The screen blanked.

Desh didn't move. Ben could feel him standing there — not hovering, exactly. Waiting. The man had a patience that didn't come from meditation or discipline. It came from stakeouts. From hours in places where moving too soon got people killed. Ben had learned the same kind of patience in the GAG, and recognizing it in someone else made it harder to pretend he wasn't being watched.

"You're not sending that," Desh said.

It wasn't a question. Ben's jaw tightened — not at the observation, but at the register. Desh had said it the way you said *that intel's bad* or *this op's blown*. The voice of someone who recognized a pattern because he'd run the same pattern himself, in the same machine, under the same institutional silences.

"Not yet," Ben said.

The words landed in the cockpit air and stayed there. First time spoken aloud. First time the withholding had a name.

Desh leaned forward, one hand braced on the back of the copilot's chair, and read the blank screen for a long moment. Then he reached past Ben to the nav computer. His fingers moved across the interface — coordinates Ben hadn't given him, a destination Ben hadn't named. The fortress. The source of the wrongness.

"You pulled these from the survey data," Ben said.

"You left them in the buffer." Desh's tone was mild. "Figured you'd want them handy."

The nav computer accepted the input and began its calculations. The display cycled — probability matrices, course corrections, a route stitching itself together through space that wasn't on any chart Ben had ever seen. The system was mapping blind, extrapolating from stellar drift models and gravimetric shadows, building a path through unmapped territory one probability at a time.

Desh straightened and looked at Ben. Not at the screen. At Ben.

"This the part where you tell me to stay behind?"

Ben considered it. The offer was there, unspoken — the out Desh was giving him, the chance to make this a solo operation and spare someone else the consequences of a choice that hadn't even made it to the transmission queue. It would be cleaner. It would be the kind of thing his father might have done, once. The kind of thing his mother would have called him an idiot for.

"No," Ben said. "This is the part where you strap in."

Desh nodded once and dropped into the copilot's seat. He didn't say *good* or *about time* or any of the things someone else might have said to fill the silence. He just pulled his harness across his chest and started running the pre-jump checklist, his hands moving through the sequence with the ease of long practice.

Ben watched the nav computer work. The route was resolving — thin lines of probability hardening into a course, the display showing a destination that had been waiting for him since the moment he'd felt the wrongness on that survey stop and decided not to put it in the report. The decision felt less like a choice now and more like gravity. Like the coordinates had been pulling at him all along and the only thing he'd been choosing was how long to resist.

The doubt surfaced — brief, sharp, a tightening in his chest that had nothing to do with the harness. Desh had seen the pattern. Desh had named it. And Desh had reached past him to enter the coordinates anyway, which meant he'd decided to follow Ben into a choice he knew was being made for the wrong reasons. Loyalty enabling avoidance. The words formed at the edge of Ben's awareness, clinical and precise, and he pushed them back down before they could become a thought.

The seal was degrading. The survivors were waiting. The urgency was real, and urgency was a better thing to focus on than the shape of what he was doing.

"Course plotted," Desh said. "Such as it is."

"Will it hold?"

"Nav computer gives it seventy-three percent confidence. Which means it's guessing, but it's guessing with math." Desh glanced at him. "We doing this?"

Ben reached for the hyperdrive controls. The wrongness pressed against his awareness — not pulling anymore, but pushing. Spatial. Directional. Like pressure building against the bulkheads before the jump even started.

"Yeah," he said. "We're doing this."

He engaged the hyperdrive.

The Steady Returns stretched into the jump — that familiar moment of elongation, the stars smearing into blue-shifted light, the deck plates humming through the transition. Ben had made hundreds of hyperspace jumps. The wrongness always faded. Distance always worked. Whatever he'd been feeling at sublight, the jump smoothed it out, put light-years between him and the source, let him breathe.

This time it didn't fade.

The pressure built instead. Not in his head — in the ship. Against the bulkheads. Against the viewport. The wrongness didn't diminish with distance. It intensified, as if the jump had carried them deeper into something rather than away from it. The deck plates creaked under a weight that wasn't gravity. The blue of hyperspace seemed to press inward, the light itself behaving wrong, closing around the ship like a fist testing its grip.

Ben's hands locked on the armrests. His spine went rigid — not fear, not quite. Recognition. The wrongness had been directional before. Now it was *here*. They were inside it. They were heading toward the source, and the source was big enough that hyperspace couldn't put a margin between them and whatever was failing.

Desh was watching the instruments. His face was calm in the way that meant he was cataloguing everything wrong and filing it for later. "Bulkhead pressure's reading normal. Hull integrity's fine. Whatever you're feeling isn't mechanical."

"I know," Ben said.

"Is it going to be a problem?"

Ben didn't answer. The wrongness coiled around his spine, heavy and certain, pressing the doubt down into something harder. Not acceptance. Not quite resolve. Bracing — the way you braced before a jump you couldn't see the bottom of.

The Steady Returns flew on through the blue, and the pressure didn't let up, and Ben sat with the weight of a choice that had been named but not examined, heading toward coordinates that had been pulling at him since before he'd known what they were.
