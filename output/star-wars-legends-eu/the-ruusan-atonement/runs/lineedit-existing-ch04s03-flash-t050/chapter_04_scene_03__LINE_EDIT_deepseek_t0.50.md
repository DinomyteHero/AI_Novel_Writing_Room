Level 3 opened into a ring.

The stairwell emptied into a circular chamber built around a central shaft that dropped into darkness. Twelve durasteel panels lined the walls at regular intervals—Ben counted them like hours on a chrono—and most were dark. Emergency lighting ran along each panel's base in the same pale blue they'd followed down from the command post, but the panels themselves were dead, surfaces dull, indicator lights extinguished. The air was warmer here than it had been on the upper levels. The hum in the walls was louder.

Ben stopped at the threshold. The Force pressed against him—the disturbance he'd been tracking since the plateau, sharper now, more focused, as if the fortress had been funneling his perception toward this room. But beneath that disturbance, moving through it with a clarity that made his chest tighten—

Three signatures. Living. Distinct.

One was disciplined—steady, controlled, its rhythm unchanged by the centuries. One was heavy—slower, deeper, carrying a weight that Ben's senses registered as pressure rather than information. One was clipped—abbreviated, its cadence cut short in a pattern that suggested restraint rather than damage, someone who had learned to hold still and was still holding.

"Twelve chambers," Desh said behind him. "Most of them are dark."

Ben didn't answer. He was already walking, following the curve of the ring, counting. The first chamber was dark. The second was dark. The third, the fourth, the fifth—each panel dead, each indicator light extinguished, each stasis field collapsed. He could see the bodies through the transparisteel viewports: preserved, still, faces visible in the blue emergency lighting. Human. Near-Human. One Twi'lek whose lekku had gone pale with age and stasis. All of them dead, sealed in chambers that had failed one by one over the centuries, their Force signatures absent—not hidden, not muted, but gone.

Ben's hand found the railing along the inner edge of the ring. The metal was warm.

Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Nine dark chambers. Nine dead anchors—the word surfaced without context, without definition, just the shape of what he was looking at—and then the tenth chamber, and the field was active.

Not bright. Flickering, unstable, the blue stasis glow dimmed to barely illuminating the transparisteel. But active. The indicator lights blinked amber along the panel's edge. The hum was stronger here, vibrating through the railing into Ben's palm, and the Force inside the chamber was alive—disciplined, steady, unchanged. A woman's silhouette was visible through the flickering field, her features indistinct but her posture upright, her hands at her sides. Waiting.

Eleven. The field was active here too—heavy, slow, the signature inside pressing outward against the stasis with a weight Ben felt in his teeth. A man's shape, broader than the woman's, his head bowed. The field around him flickered more than the tenth chamber's, its blue light stuttering in a pattern that Ben's instincts read as strain.

Twelve. Active. Clipped, restrained, the signature inside quick and sharp—another man, smaller than the eleventh, his posture coiled rather than still. The field held, but barely. The indicator lights on this panel were amber too, but one of them was flashing—the same urgent frequency Ben had seen on the network display upstairs, the same BREACH warning translated into a single chamber's monitoring system.

And the twelfth chamber was empty.

Not dark. Not dead. Empty—its stasis field gone, its transparisteel viewport shattered from the inside, its interior scored with gouges that radiated outward like something had clawed its way free. The damage was systematic. Deliberate. The scoring traveled in lines that converged on the center of the chamber and then burst outward, and the durasteel around the viewport was bent in a pattern that suggested pressure applied from within over a long period of time.

Desh was already moving toward it. Ben saw him step past the eleventh chamber, his glow rod trained on the empty one's interior, his free hand tracing the edge of the shattered viewport without touching it.

"Someone broke out," Desh said. His voice was flat, operational—the register Ben had learned to recognize as Desh processing a threat. "Recently."

"How recently?"

Desh's fingers found the monitoring panel beside the chamber. The display was still active—ancient, flickering, its readouts in the same unknown notation Ben had seen upstairs, but the timestamps were clear enough. Desh read them, his jaw tightening.

"Weeks. Maybe a month. The flags are still logging."

Ben's attention sharpened. The awe at the three living signatures—the disciplined woman, the heavy man, the clipped presence in the flickering field—tightened into focus. These three were still here. Still sealed. Still dependent on systems that were failing, on a fortress whose power was degrading, on a network whose nodes had been dying for a thousand years. And whatever had been in the twelfth chamber had had weeks to move.

Weeks to act.

Weeks to dismantle.

"The damage," Ben said. "From inside."

"Systematic scoring. Radiating outward." Desh's voice was quiet, the operational flatness giving way to something harder. "Whatever was in there didn't break out in a panic. It worked at it. Methodically. Over time."

Ben's hand moved toward the monitoring panel before he'd decided to move it. The display flickered under his touch—the same amber indicators, the same flashing BREACH warning, the same timestamps counting backward from the moment the chamber's field had collapsed. He didn't need to read the notation to understand what he was seeing. The breach was old. The escapee was loose. And the three people still sealed in their chambers had no idea.

"We need to see what's below," he said.

"Ben."

"I know."

"You don't know. That's the problem."

Ben turned from the empty chamber. Desh was watching him, his glow rod still trained on the shattered viewport, his weight settled onto his back foot in the stance Ben had learned to read as *we should leave*. But he hadn't moved toward the exit. He was waiting.

"Whatever was in there," Ben said, "had weeks. If it wanted to hurt us, we'd already be dead. It wanted out. It got out. The immediate threat is what happens next—to them." He nodded toward the three active chambers. "And to whatever's below us that Gavran was monitoring when he died."

Desh held his gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded—not agreement, but acknowledgment, the nod of a man who had decided to follow someone into a situation he didn't like and was making his peace with it.

"The stairwell continues," he said. "After you."

---

Level 4 was smaller than the ring above it.

The stairwell ended in a single chamber built around the base of the central shaft, its walls lined with conduits that converged on a control nexus at the room's center. Most of the conduits were dark, the power that had run through them long since failed, but a few still glowed with the same amber light that had flickered through the monitoring stations upstairs, their energy cycling through systems designed to persist. The hum was strongest here, rising through the floor and the walls and the air itself, a vibration that Ben felt in his chest and his bones and the base of his skull.

Gavran was seated at the nexus.

His body was preserved—the dry atmosphere of the fortress had mummified him, his skin drawn tight over the bones of his face, his robes intact but faded. His hands were still on the control surfaces, the fingers resting on the interface with the precision of a man who had been working when he stopped. His posture was not peaceful. It was settled—the way a man settled when he had finished what he could and then stopped moving, the way a body settled when the will that had held it upright finally let go.

Ben stopped at the edge of the control nexus. The Force around Gavran's body was quiet. Not deadened—the disturbance was still there, still pressing, still straining against whatever containment the fortress had been built to maintain—but quiet, the way a room was quiet after a note had finished sounding. The signature that had once been here was gone, and what remained was the shape of where it had been.

The monitoring log was still active on the console beside Gavran's right hand. The display flickered—amber, unstable, the text in the same unknown notation Ben had seen on every console in the fortress—but the final entry was legible enough. The sentence trailed off mid-word, the characters dissolving into a string of incomplete glyphs that suggested the writer had stopped typing before he'd finished the thought.

He held this together until he couldn't.

Desh was silent behind him. Ben didn't turn. He stood at the edge of the nexus, looking at Gavran's hands on the controls, at the conduits that converged on the center of the room and rose upward toward the ring of chambers above, at the body of the man who had designed this system and sealed himself inside it and held it for a thousand years while the anchors died one by one.

The grief arrived not as sentiment but as weight. Gavran had chosen this. Had stayed, had held, had kept the seal intact even as his people failed around him, even as the power degraded, even as the network screamed into a silence that no one was alive to break. He had held until he couldn't. And now Ben was standing in the room where he'd died, looking at the three survivors who were still sealed above him, and there was no one else to do what Gavran had done.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet. "The chambers upstairs. The three that are still active."

"I know."

"What do we do with them?"

Ben's jaw tightened. The question was the right one—practical, immediate, the thing Desh always asked when the situation stopped being theoretical and started being a problem that needed to be solved. But the answer wasn't his to give. Not yet. The three signatures were still pulsing above him—disciplined, heavy, clipped—each one a person who had been sealed for a thousand years and did not know the galaxy had moved on. Waking them was a decision that would change everything. Waking them without knowing who they were, what they believed, what they would do when they understood what had been done to them—

"Not yet," Ben said.

"Ben."

"I know. I know what you're asking. But I need to understand what we're dealing with before I wake three people who've been in stasis since the Ruusan Reformations and tell them the galaxy forgot they existed."

He turned from the nexus. Gavran's hands stayed on the controls. The monitoring log stayed frozen mid-sentence. The conduits hummed, and the disturbance pressed, and the three signatures pulsed in the chambers above—waiting.

"Come on," Ben said. "I need to see something."

---

They climbed back to Level 3 in silence. The emergency lighting flickered along the risers. The hum followed them up.

Ben stopped in front of the tenth chamber—the disciplined signature, the woman's silhouette visible through the flickering blue field. The indicator lights blinked amber along the panel's edge. The stasis field hummed at a frequency that Ben felt in his palms.

He touched the control surface.

Not hard. Not the sharp, directed nudge he'd used on the monitoring station upstairs. This was gentler—the Force equivalent of knocking on a door and waiting to see if anyone knocked back. The field hummed in response, its blue light steadying for a moment before resuming its flicker, and the signature inside the chamber pulsed—once, faintly, like a heartbeat answering a question.

The other two pulsed with it. The heavy one in the eleventh chamber, pressing outward. The clipped one in the twelfth, quick and sharp. Three signatures, distinct and alive, acknowledging the touch without waking.

Ben's hand stayed on the control. The hum carried a question he wasn't ready to answer. The three signatures were people—not infrastructure, not anchors, not components in a system that had been designed to fail. People. Sealed here by an Order that had decided their lives were an acceptable cost. Forgotten by the galaxy that had been built on top of their sacrifice.

"We'll wake them," he said. "When we're ready."

Desh didn't answer. He was standing in front of the empty chamber, his glow rod trained on the scoring that radiated outward from the shattered viewport, his jaw tight.

"Someone broke out," he said again. "Recently. And they had weeks."

"I know."

"Do you know what they're going to do?"

Ben looked at the three active chambers—the disciplined woman, the heavy man, the clipped presence in the flickering field—and then at the empty one, its interior gouged and scored, its emergency flags still blinking, its breach still unanswered.

"No," he said. "But I know where to start looking."

Below them, in Level 4, Gavran's body sat at the nexus of the conduit network. His hands were on the control surfaces. His last monitoring log entry trailed off mid-sentence. He held this together until he couldn't.
