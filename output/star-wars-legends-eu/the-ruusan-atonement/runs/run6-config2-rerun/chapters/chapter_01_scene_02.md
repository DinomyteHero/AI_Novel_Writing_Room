Luke's office smelled of cold tea.

Ben registered the scent the moment he crossed the threshold—the stale sweetness of the herbal blend his father had favored for decades. The room was functional, a space that had accumulated rather than been decorated. Data cores stacked one end of the long workstation. A holocomm unit blinked a slow amber standby pulse. Through the single viewport, Coruscant’s permanent haze hung like a shroud. The only concession to personal history was a small, framed star chart on the far wall, hand-annotated in the script Ben had grown up reading over his father's shoulder.

Luke stood with his back to the door, studying a datapad. His Force presence arrived before his words—vast, warm, and steady as gravity. It was the background radiation of Ben’s life. Even at twenty-three, walking into a room where his father stood triggered a knot in Ben’s chest for which he had no name.

He kept his face neutral and stopped two meters inside the door.

"Master Skywalker."

Luke turned. The lines around his eyes had deepened since the Abeloth crisis. He wore them without apology. He extended the datapad across the desk.

"Ben." The warmth in his voice was genuine. It always was. "Look at this."

Ben stepped forward and took the device.

The display showed seventeen survey flags strung along a corridor of Outer Rim space like stress fractures in a viewport. Each carried the same notation: *anomalous Force readings, no matching phenomenon in archive*. The timestamps spanned five weeks, filed by four different survey teams across the Darrpa Sector. The earliest flag had been logged two days before Ben first noticed the friction in the meditation chamber.

He scrolled. The readings didn't cluster like dark side signatures. They didn't radiate from a point source. They ran along a line, as if something structural had shifted beneath the surface of space itself.

"Where did the teams go after filing?" Ben asked.

"Three completed their routes. One turned back." Luke settled into his chair and folded his hands. "The team that retreated reported equipment interference they couldn't explain. Instruments provided contradictory data. Their navigator described the sensation—" he paused, searching his memory, "—as if the stars moved when she wasn't looking."

Ben set the datapad down. "You've been monitoring this."

"Since you first flagged it. Yes."

The wrongness lived here, too. Ben noticed it now that the initial warmth of Luke's presence had settled. Beneath the surface, faint but persistent, he felt a sourness. A flat dissonance threaded through the Temple's harmonics like a misaligned panel vibrating deep in a hull. The office's familiar sounds—the amber pulse of the holocomm, the distant murmur of the city—sat slightly off. The room felt wrong, the way a house feels when a piece of furniture has been moved a few centimeters from its mark.

Luke's hands gripped the desk. He felt it, too—the pressure grinding at the edges of his steady presence. But he did what he always did: he contained it, cataloged it, and fitted it into a known framework.

Ben's shoulders tensed. He wanted to describe the specific texture of that absence he had felt in the meditation chamber. He wanted Luke to stop theorizing and feel it. The urge sat in his throat, heavy and unswallowed.

"You know what it is," Ben said.

Luke's expression turned careful. "I have a theory. There are precedents for Force disturbances along Outer Rim corridors when dark side activity concentrates over time. A cult, perhaps. Or a natural resonance shift in a sector with significant historical trauma." He paused. "Ruusan is along that bearing."

Ben looked at the star chart. He had stood in this office at eleven years old, tracing hyperspace routes with one finger while his father explained the shape of the galaxy. He remembered the weight of that lesson: history lived in the Force. Old wounds scarred rather than closed.

He looked back at the datapad.

Ruusan. The Brotherhood of Darkness. The thought bomb. A thousand years of residual trauma bleeding into the sector was a credible explanation. It was the conclusion the Council would reach. It fit the framework. It allowed the archive to provide the answer.

The wrongness pressed against the back of his skull, flat and patient.

Ben had felt dark side resonance before. He had been in the same room as Jacen when the fractures first appeared—had stood close enough to feel the gravity of that slide. He had felt Abeloth at the edge of the galaxy's sanity, a presence so vast it made the concept of hunger feel small.

This was different. No appetite. No gravity. No cold.

It was an absence. A place where something structural should have been and wasn't. It felt like pressing a hand against a wall and finding the load-bearing beam had gone hollow.

He did not say this.

He picked up the datapad again and studied the coordinates, giving himself a target for his gaze while he calculated. Luke's theory was not wrong, but it was incomplete. Correcting it would require explaining what Ben heard in the silence that Luke didn't. It would require the conversation they had avoided for three months.

The meditation chamber. The liaison's useless response. The hand on his shoulder.

He had already decided this was his to carry.

"The survey route along the Darrpa Sector," Ben said. "You want eyes on it."

"I want your eyes on it." Luke leaned forward. His presence pressed closer—not invasive, but focused. "Your perception of this disturbance has been distinct. The Council's instruments aren't flagging what you're flagging."

There it was. Not an apology, but an acknowledgment offered cleanly: Ben was reading something Luke could not. The words landed like an opening.

Ben's throat tightened. He wanted to close the distance and let Luke help him. The impulse was so strong his hands moved toward the datapad without his permission.

He stopped them.

Luke's presence withdrew, settling back into its familiar vastness. The moment passed.

"I'll file a departure plan by morning," Ben said.

Luke nodded. "Good."

The word hung there, carrying everything they refused to say. Good that Ben was taking the mission. Good that he was going alone. Good that Luke was letting him.

Ben turned toward the door. His father's presence warmed his back—real, yet separated by a gap neither had named. The wrongness hummed its patient, structural note beneath the Temple's harmonics.

He walked out.
