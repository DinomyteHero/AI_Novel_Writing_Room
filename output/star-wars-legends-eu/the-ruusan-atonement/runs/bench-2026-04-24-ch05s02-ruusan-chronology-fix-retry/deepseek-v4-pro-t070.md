Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Ben saw it happen before he understood what he was seeing. Her hand found the activation plate at the lower left edge of the console — a placement he had missed in his own earlier attempts, a recessed catch designed for fingers that already knew where to look. The plate registered her touch and the console hummed to life, holos flickering from standby amber to active blue, and her fingers were already moving across the interface before the displays finished resolving.

A thousand years. Her muscle memory was faster than his modern training.

The hum of the console filled the archive chamber — a low, resonant frequency that Ben felt in his chest before his ears caught up. The air tasted recycled and cold, the same fortress-atmosphere tang that had been pressing on him since they'd entered Level Two, but now it registered differently. Sharper. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case on the table behind him, its faint luminescence a steady pressure at the edge of his awareness, and he found himself cataloguing details he did not need — the precise angle of Sera's shoulders, the way her jaw set as she worked, the fact that Desh had stopped breathing two seconds ago and still had not started again.

"Veranthos protocol index," Sera said. Not a question. The holos shifted, reorganizing into a nested structure Ben had never seen before — pre-Ruusan administrative architecture, the kind of filing logic that had been obsolete for a thousand years. She was reading it like a language she still dreamed in.

Torin stepped forward before Ben could speak. His hand moved across the display with the same thousand-year-old familiarity, pulling the index open into its component sections, and when he spoke his voice carried the easy warmth of someone telling a story he had told before.

"The containment terms were Gavran's work," he said. "Not the public settlement — that came later, and we would not have seen it. But the private protocol. The seal's architecture. This is what we signed ourselves into." He tapped a subsection and the holo expanded, dense blocks of pre-Reformation administrative Basic scrolling past. "He was thorough. I will give him that. No one ever accused Gavran of leaving a loose end."

Ben felt his chest tighten. The public timeline was what he had been taught — the Ruusan Reformations, the end of the Jedi Lords, the demilitarization and centralization and Republic oversight that had reshaped the Order after the New Sith Wars. Real history. True history. But the private Veranthos index predated all of it, and Torin was not explaining the Reformations. He was remembering Veranthos. The distinction was the thing Ben could not stop turning over.

"Gavran," Ben said. "He was the architect of —"

Kael reached past Sera and touched the display.

It was the first voluntary thing he had done since the survivors woke. His finger landed on a designation in the protocol header and dragged it into correction, replacing one term with another, and then he stepped back. The whole motion took less than three seconds.

"Keeper," Kael said. "Not architect. He kept the protocol. He did not write the settlement."

His voice was dry, unhurried, the monk's brevity Ben had already learned to expect. He did not explain the correction. He did not look at Ben to see if it had landed. He simply folded his arms and returned to the silence he had been carrying since the stasis chamber.

Ben registered the significance of the action before he processed the content. Kael had broken his silence for something that mattered to him. The correction was precise and canon-safe — Gavran as custodian of the concealed Veranthos layer, not the public face of the later reforms — but the fact of the correction mattered more than its content. The survivors were not unified in their testimony. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

"Private penance-and-containment," Sera said.

She delivered it in a single composed sentence, no inflection, no hesitation. The words landed in the cold archive air and stayed there. She did not call it the Reformations. She did not elaborate. She did not soften the institutional fact with context or apology. The sentence was clean and complete and it changed everything.

"The Reformations," Ben said. The word came out before he could stop it. "The public settlement after Ruusan — it carried this. It buried this."

Sera did not answer. She did not need to. Her face was composed, the century-of-discipline mask still in place, but Ben had spent enough hours in her presence now to read what lived beneath it. She was waiting for him to catch up.

He felt the connection land before his intellect caught up — a vertigo-like drop in his center of gravity, a sudden clarity that arrived as a physical sensation rather than a thought. One of the histories Luke's Order had inherited was incomplete exactly where Ben now needed it whole. The public Ruusan Reformations were real. The demilitarization, the end of the Jedi Lords, the centralized training, the Republic oversight — all of it had happened. But beneath it, carried forward in the institutional memory of an Order that had chosen not to name it, was a concealed wound. A private penance. A containment protocol built out of thirteen anchors and a secret the later Jedi had buried so thoroughly that even Luke's restored Order had never found it.

If the private protocol was penance, what had it been penance for?

What had the old Order chosen to bury?

The moral weight of the question settled into his shoulders. He did not yet understand the answer, but he understood that the post-Ruusan settlement had been edited at the source, and the three people standing in this room with him were the living evidence of the edit.

Sera the instrument of precision. Torin the keeper of narrative warmth. Kael the quiet correction.

The archive around them was no longer a room full of records. It was a testimony delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it.

Ben looked at Sera's face and read the rest of it there. She did not say what the penance was for.

She did not need to.
