Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Ben saw it happen a beat before he understood what he was seeing. Her hand found the activation plate at the lower left edge of the console — a placement he had missed in his own earlier attempts, a recessed catch made for fingers that already knew the trick of it. The plate registered her touch and the console came awake, holos flickering from standby amber to active blue. Her fingers were already moving across the interface before the displays finished resolving.

A thousand years old. Her muscle memory beat his modern training hands down.

The console’s hum filled the archive chamber — low, resonant, and strong enough that Ben felt it in his ribs before his ears sorted it out. Recycled air carried the same cold fortress tang that had been clinging to him since they entered Level Two, but now it had teeth. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case on the table behind him, its faint luminescence pressing at the edge of his vision, and he caught himself noting details he had no use for: the exact line of Sera’s shoulders, the tight set of her jaw as she worked, the fact that Desh had stopped breathing two seconds ago and still hadn’t remembered to start again.

“Veranthos protocol index,” Sera said.

Not a question. The holos shifted, reorganizing into a nested structure Ben had never seen before — pre-Ruusan administrative architecture, the sort of filing logic that had gone out of use a thousand years ago. She read it like a language she’d been thinking in all her life.

Torin stepped in before Ben could get a word out. His hand moved across the display with the same thousand-year-old ease, pulling the index open into its component sections, and when he spoke his voice had the easy warmth of someone telling a story he’d already told more than once.

“The containment terms were Gavran’s work,” he said. “Not the public settlement — that came later, and we wouldn’t have seen that. But the private protocol. The seal’s architecture. This is what we signed ourselves into.” He tapped a subsection, and the holo bloomed wider, dense blocks of pre-Reformation administrative Basic sliding past. “He was thorough. I’ll grant him that. No one ever accused Gavran of leaving a loose end.”

Ben felt his chest go tight. The public timeline was the one he’d been taught — the Ruusan Reformations, the end of the Jedi Lords, the demilitarization and centralization and Republic oversight that reshaped the Order after the New Sith Wars. Real history. True history. But the private Veranthos index came before all of it, and Torin wasn’t explaining the Reformations. He was remembering Veranthos. That difference was the thing Ben kept turning over in his mind.

“Gavran,” Ben said. “He was the architect of—”

Kael reached past Sera and touched the display.

It was the first voluntary thing he had done since the survivors woke. His finger landed on a designation in the protocol header, dragged it into correction, and replaced one term with another. Then he stepped back. The whole motion took less than three seconds.

“Keeper,” Kael said. “Not architect. He kept the protocol. He did not write the settlement.”

His voice was dry, unhurried, the monk’s brevity Ben had already begun to expect. He didn’t explain the correction. He didn’t look to Ben to see whether it had landed. He just folded his arms and went back to the silence he’d been carrying since the stasis chamber.

Ben understood the significance of the action before he fully processed the words. Kael had broken silence for something that mattered to him. The correction was exact — Gavran as custodian of the concealed Veranthos layer, not the public face of the later reforms — but the fact that Kael had chosen to say anything mattered more. The survivors weren’t one voice. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

“Private penance-and-containment,” Sera said.

She gave it to them in a single composed sentence, no hesitation, no ornament. The words hit the cold air and stayed there. She didn’t call it the Reformations. She didn’t add context. She didn’t soften the institutional fact with apology. The sentence was clean, and complete, and it changed the shape of the room.

“The Reformations,” Ben said. The word was out before he could stop it. “The public settlement after Ruusan — it carried this. It buried this.”

Sera didn’t answer. She didn’t need to. Her face stayed composed, the century-of-discipline mask still in place, but Ben had spent enough time around her now to read the truth she wasn’t saying. She was waiting for him to catch up.

The connection hit him before his mind finished making it — a sudden drop in his center of gravity, a vertigo-lurch that arrived in his body before it arrived in his thoughts. One of the histories Luke’s Order had inherited was incomplete exactly where Ben now needed it whole. The public Ruusan Reformations had been real. The demilitarization, the end of the Jedi Lords, the centralized training, the Republic oversight — all of it had happened. But beneath that, carried forward in the memory of an Order that had chosen not to name it, was a concealed wound. A private penance. A containment protocol built around thirteen anchors and a secret later Jedi had buried so deeply that even Luke’s restored Order had never found the shape of it.

If the private protocol was penance, what had it been penance for?

What had the old Order chosen to bury?

The question settled into his shoulders with a weight that had nothing to do with the room’s gravity. He still didn’t know the answer, but he knew the post-Ruusan settlement had been edited at the source, and the three people standing with him were living proof of the edit.

Sera the instrument of precision. Torin the keeper of narrative warmth. Kael the quiet correction.

The archive around them was no longer just a room full of records. It was testimony, delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it.

Ben looked at Sera’s face and read the rest of it there. She did not say what the penance was for.

She did not need to.
