Luke’s office smelled of old flimsiplast and the dry warmth of a room with overactive climate controls. Ben had grown up with that scent. It meant late nights, mission briefings, and conversations that shifted shape as the hours grew small.

The datapad sat on the desk between them. Its blue glow filled the silence.

Ben picked it up. Twelve survey reports were flagged in red, stacked chronologically. Each described the same phenomenon in varying technical jargon: *Anomalous Force resonance. Non-standard spatial harmonic. Deviation from baseline lattice coherence.* Twelve different surveyors had found twelve different ways to say they were out of their depth. The oldest report was four months old; the most recent had been filed last week.

He kept his face neutral. It was the mask he wore in briefing rooms when the man across the desk was also his father.

"Darpa Sector," Ben said. "All along the same bearing."

"The same vector, yes." Luke leaned back. His presence filled the room—vast, layered with decades of experience, and anchored by a quality Ben had never quite named. Bedrock, perhaps. A warmth without edges. "The survey teams flagged them independently. None of them saw the other reports until I cross-referenced."

Ben scrolled to the third entry. The surveyor had attached a half-apologetic notation: *Recommend follow-up. Anomaly does not match residual dark side profiles from Abeloth incident or Lost Tribe aftermath. Unable to classify.*

"Unable to classify," Ben repeated.

"That’s what caught my eye."

Ben set the datapad down. Luke watched him with a careful, considered calm. It was the expression of a man who had already reached a conclusion and was waiting to see if Ben would find the same path. Ben had been reading that look since he was twelve. 

This one meant: *I’m giving you something.*

"You want me to run the survey route."

"I want someone I trust to run the survey route," Luke said. The distinction was deliberate.

Ben’s jaw set. He forced the tension out of his muscles. The wrongness he’d carried for three months hummed in the Temple’s harmonics even here—faint, but present. It was a frequency that didn't belong to the building’s settled song.

"What’s your read?" he asked.

Luke hesitated, weighing his words. "Residual disturbance. The war stressed the Force throughout this sector. We could be seeing a long-cycle echo, something that won't resolve for years." He paused. "That’s the most likely explanation."

"But," Ben prompted.

Luke’s mouth curved. "But I’ve felt something beneath the Temple’s harmonics for months, and I can’t place it. I wanted to see if it faded on its own."

The flat note hummed louder.

Ben looked at the string of red flags. They ran along the survey route like a wound that refused to knit. An echo loses amplitude. This didn't. He’d felt it for months, grinding in the Force like load-bearing stress rather than the fading ring of a strike. He’d stood in the ruins of Centerpoint at sixteen and learned the sound of structural failure. He’d watched Jacen tune himself to frequencies no one else could hear. He’d survived Abeloth and trained Vestara Khai. Those experiences had carved channels in his senses that standard Jedi training never anticipated.

He pushed the datapad back toward the center of the desk.

"It’s not an echo," Ben said.

Luke’s attention sharpened. "Explain."

"An echo dies out. This is constant." Ben kept his voice level and clinical. "It has a mechanical quality. Regular. Cyclical. It sounds like something under stress, not something at rest."

"You’ve been feeling this for three months."

It wasn't a question. Ben didn't flinch. "Yes."

Luke leaned forward, his posture shifting from settled to engaged. "I feel a disturbance. I don't feel what you’re describing."

The words landed with precision. Ben’s breathing remained steady, but something in his chest constricted and then settled into a cold, familiar shape.

There it was.

He’d known this was where the conversation would land—the gap between their perceptions. It was a rift that had opened with Jacen and widened ever since. Luke’s connection to the Force was extraordinary, but vastness wasn't the same as granularity. Luke felt the ocean. Ben had learned to feel the specific pressure changes that preceded a wave because he’d been underwater when they hit.

He couldn't explain that without sounding arrogant. It wasn't superiority. It was just different. Damage changed the way a person heard things.

"Your read may be right," Ben said. "A long-cycle echo. I’ll run the survey and confirm it."

"And if it’s not?"

"Then I find out what it is."

Luke held his gaze. Something moved in his expression—recognition, or the closest thing to it he allowed himself during a briefing. He slid the datapad back to Ben.

"Fourteen systems. Standard equipment. Check in at each node." He paused. "I’ll want reports."

"You’ll get them."

Luke didn't suggest a partner. Ben didn't offer to take one. The question hung between them, a stone neither was willing to throw. Ben recognized this shape—the space where father and Grand Master overlapped until the lines blurred. 

Luke saw a Jedi Knight with a unique gift. Ben understood what his father wouldn't say: that Ben could feel it because of everything that had broken him. The Order needed Luke on Coruscant. Sending Ben alone was a choice born of necessity.

The logic was clean, terrible, and final.

Ben stood and tucked the datapad under his arm. The wrongness hummed at the base of his skull, pointing toward the vector he now held. Fourteen systems. He had to navigate the quiet knowledge that his father could feel the disturbance but couldn't feel why it was wrong.

He was almost to the door when Luke spoke.

"Ben."

He turned.

Luke remained seated, the blue glow of the displays catching the silver at his temples. He looked like a man who had carried the weight of the Order for thirty years and had mastered the art of hiding the strain.

"Be careful."

Simple words, yet they carried a weight that exceeded their syllables. Ben knew the difference between a warning from Luke Skywalker and one from his father. He knew what lived in the silence beneath this one—the memory of another boy sent alone, trusted to navigate a path no one else could see.

The wrongness hummed. Ben’s hand was steady on the door frame.

"Always am," he said, and left before the lie could catch up to him.
