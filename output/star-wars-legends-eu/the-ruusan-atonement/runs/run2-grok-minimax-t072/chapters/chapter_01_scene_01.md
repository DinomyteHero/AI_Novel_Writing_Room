The stone was cold. Ben had been sitting on it long enough that it should have warmed, but the chill kept finding new ways through his palms.

He pressed them flatter against the floor anyway.

Around him, the meditation chamber held its silence like a cupped hand—the particular quiet of the Coruscant Temple at the third hour, when even the most restless Knights had finally retired. Through the walls, through the stone itself, the Order breathed. He could feel it: hundreds of presences, each one a warm chord in the dark, some dreaming, some in their own meditations, all of them settled.

Ben was not at rest.

He pulled his awareness back toward his own center the way he’d been taught, the way he’d practiced ten thousand times. Let the breath slow. Let the body drop its weight. Let the Force fill the space that tension vacated. The technique was sound. It had worked in the aftermath of Centerpoint, had worked in the Maw, had worked on the floor of a Sith temple with his hands slick with blood and his ears still ringing.

Three months ago, it had stopped working.

Not completely. He could still reach the Force, could still feel the Order’s collective warmth, could still access his own abilities. It was subtler than that. Like trying to hear someone speak in a room where one instrument in the background orchestra was playing persistently flat. Not loud enough to drown anything out. Just wrong enough that he couldn't stop noticing it.

He exhaled through his nose and tried again.

The warmth of the Order’s presences rose around him. His breathing slowed. For four seconds, he almost had it—the edge of genuine stillness, the threshold where the Force stopped being something he reached for and started being something he simply was.

Then the flat note hummed through his back teeth.

It wasn't pain. He’d tried to categorize it as such, but the label didn't fit. It was a physical memory of a sound—a vibration that lived in his molars and the base of his skull and the junction between his shoulder blades. A frequency the rest of the Order apparently couldn't hear, or didn't find worth mentioning, or had collectively decided was acceptable background noise left over from Abeloth. From the war. From everything.

Ben opened his eyes and looked at the far wall.

The other Knights weren’t wrong to move on. That was the rational position. The galaxy had endured three years of reality-warping crisis, and Force disturbances lingering in the aftermath were as predictable as structural damage after an earthquake. The Order had catalogued seventeen distinct residual anomalies in the past year alone. 

This was probably the eighteenth.

He rolled his neck once—the vertebrae popped, loud in the silence—and closed his eyes again. He did something the meditation manuals didn't recommend: instead of releasing the disturbance, he turned toward it. He let his awareness lean into the flat note the way a tongue presses against a sore tooth. Not to hurt himself. Just to understand the shape of it.

It didn't sharpen into pain. It resolved into information.

The flat note clarified as he relaxed his focus. What he’d been experiencing as a single dissonance started to reveal an internal structure. Not a chord gone wrong, but a specific, mechanical deviation. A gear stripped of one tooth that caught on every rotation. Regular. Rhythmic. Ongoing.

Not an echo.

His hands pressed harder against the stone.

An echo faded. An echo was the residue of an event, a wave moving outward from a point of origin, losing amplitude with every passing month. He’d been telling himself for three months that this was an echo. The word had been convenient. It had let him file this under *processing* and move on with his day.

But echoes didn't maintain their amplitude. They didn't have this grinding, cyclical regularity—this sense of a mechanism under stress rather than a wound at rest. He’d felt something like it once before, years ago, standing in the ruins of Centerpoint Station after Jacen had used it. Not the dark side residue, but something underneath. The sound the Force itself made when something load-bearing had been asked to hold more than it was built for.

He’d been a teenager then. He’d filed it away as trauma.

He was twenty-five now, and the flat note had been running for three months, and it was not getting quieter.

His breath caught. He held it there, suspended, the way one holds a breath when recognizing something dangerous in the dark.

The Order didn't know. The Masters didn't know. That meant either they couldn't hear it, or they’d heard it and decided it wasn't worth the weight of their attention. Either answer was a problem. Either answer meant he was on his own.

He sat with that. Let it be true without acting. The Force around him continued its warm, collective breathing. The other Knights slept. Somewhere in the Temple, a night-duty Padawan was likely running forms in an empty training salle, working off nervous energy, at peace with the Force as it currently presented itself.

He was not going to bring this to the Masters’ Council. Not yet. Not with a description that amounted to *something sounds wrong and I can't tell you where it's coming from*, delivered by the Knight who was also the son of the man who had personally faced down both Abeloth and the Lost Tribe of the Sith. The son of the man the Order looked to when reality broke. There was a version of this conversation where it went well, but in that version, he had concrete information to offer.

He’d find the source first. That was the plan. 

The door hissed.

Ben didn't startle—he’d heard the footsteps in the corridor, had registered the familiar weight of that particular presence without consciously processing it. He turned his head.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with the corridor light behind him and a datapad in his hand. Still dressed, which meant he’d been in his study, which meant he hadn't been sleeping either. His expression was the one Ben had been reading since childhood: a careful, considered calm sitting over something more urgent.

Ben had seen that expression before missions. Before hard conversations. Before things changed.

"I thought I'd find you here," Luke said.

Ben's shoulders had already begun to tense. He forced them level. "Predictable. That's me."

"Anomalous Force readings," Luke said without preamble, holding up the datapad. "Darpa Sector. A survey team flagged them three weeks ago."

Three weeks. Ben’s pulse quickened. "And you're just now—"

"I've been waiting to see if they'd resolve on their own." Luke's voice carried that particular weight it took on when he was being careful not to sound defensive. "They haven't."

The flat note hummed through Ben's back teeth, right on schedule. Not relief. Not quite. It was the sensation of a tension that had been pulling in one direction for three months finally finding something to pull against. The sensation of being right about something he’d desperately wanted to be wrong about.

"When do we leave?" he asked.

Luke's expression shifted—something flickered there, a recognition, maybe approval. "You already knew something was wrong."

It wasn't a question. Ben stood, brushing stone dust from his palms. The gesture bought him a second. "I knew the wrongness wasn't finished. I didn't know where it was coming from."

"And now?"

"Now we find out." Ben met his father's eyes. For the first time in three months, the flat note didn't feel like an accusation. It felt like a direction. "Darpa Sector?"

Luke nodded once. "Pack light. We leave at dawn."
