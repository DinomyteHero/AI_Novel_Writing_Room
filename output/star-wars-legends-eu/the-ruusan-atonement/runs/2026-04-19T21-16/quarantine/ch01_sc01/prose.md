The training blade slapped across Ben’s shoulder, and before the sting even landed he knew he’d been late.

Not slow. Late. There was a difference, and he felt it in the half-second gap between where his guard had been and where it should have been—a gap that shouldn’t exist, that hadn’t existed six days ago, and that had now cost him three times in a week on the same basic exchange. His shoulder would bruise. Fair enough.

“Good,” he said, and meant it, because being generous was easier than explaining.

Across the salle, his partner—Rendell, third year as a Knight, decent footwork—had already settled back into neutral stance. Waiting for the reset. Ben looked down at the training saber in his hand, thumb resting near the activation stud, and forced himself to weigh the rematch for exactly as long as would look natural.

“I think I’m done,” he said. “Long week.”

Rendell only nodded. No questions, no second glance. Ben watched him rack his saber and head for the door, and the relief that hit him was immediate, clean, and completely wrong.

He stayed where he was until the door sealed behind Rendell and the bootsteps faded into the corridor. Then he turned and racked his own blade without looking at the wall.

Long week. True enough. It had been a long week in the specific sense that every day had shown up carrying the same low wrongness under it, the way a stringed instrument sounds when the tuning slips—still music, still usable, but off in a way that put your teeth on edge. The presence in the Temple had felt like that since at least Taungsday. He’d been compensating, shaving his timing by instinct the way you adjust your gait on a ship with a slight list, not noticing you’re doing it until the deck levels out and you nearly fall over.

Except the deck never leveled out.

He took a fresh training saber from the rack—not to spar, just to have the weight of it in his hand—and walked back to the center of the floor.

The salle was nearly empty at this hour. A practice droid sat folded against the far wall, sensor array dark. Through the vents came the faint chime of a turbolift somewhere down the corridor, along with muffled late-shift traffic moving through the Temple. Familiar sounds. Ordinary sounds. He’d grown up inside them the way other people grew up with rain against a roof.

He settled into the opening position of Soresu, blade unlit, and began.

The form was reflex. It had been reflex since he was fifteen. His body moved through the opening sequence without needing him to think about it, muscle memory laying out the geometry of defense—weight transfer, guard angle, pivot on the third beat—and he let it run while he listened for the place where the wrongness would show itself. It always did. That was what he’d been trying to pin down for six days: whether his own timing was slipping, or the field around him was, or whether the two had started scraping against each other in some way he couldn’t isolate.

Three sequences in, it hit.

His body turned exactly where it should. The follow-through was clean. But the presence in the room—the background Force he’d learned to read the way you learn the pressure in the air before weather—came in a half-beat behind where his reflex expected it. Not absent. Just late. Like an echo arriving from a chamber built wrong.

Ben stopped.

He stood in the empty salle with the unlit saber hanging from one hand and his arms loose at his sides, and made himself stay still.

He’d been doing this for four days. Running forms in the middle of the night, blade dark, listening for the lag. Telling himself it was fatigue, or residue from the Abeloth crisis, or just the kind of disturbance a galaxy like this produced sooner or later. He’d been treating it like a recurring ache—watching it just enough to keep score, not enough to report. Waiting for it to clear on its own.

It wasn’t clearing.

The lag was tiny. He had to listen for it to catch it, and he’d trained with Knights for years who never would have noticed it at all. But Ben noticed it. He always had. The Temple’s presence had been one of his constants since childhood—the particular feel of it through the meditation chambers, the training salles, the corridors—and this was not that. This was that presence with a wrong note in it. A note played slightly flat in a piece he’d gone through so many times he’d stopped hearing it on purpose, until one day the note went bad and now he couldn’t not hear it.

The question he’d been refusing to ask for six days stood there in the salle with him.

What if it isn’t the field?

What if the problem sits in his own perception instead of in the Force itself? What if the lag is him—some leftover damage from the last three years, from his father and the Sith and the things he’d seen at the end of the Abeloth crisis—and he’s pushing it outward because that’s easier than admitting his own senses might be compromised? He was a Skywalker. There was family precedent for exactly that kind of problem.

He let the thought turn once, then set it down. Not because it was wrong, but because he couldn’t test it from inside the test. If his perception was compromised, then using that same perception to prove anything was a neat little trap. He’d spent six days walking circles around it, and the circles weren’t getting any smaller.

Rendell’s training blade had caught him across the shoulder.

That part wasn’t theoretical. That part was his shoulder.

Ben exhaled and looked down at the unlit saber in his hand, then crossed to the rack and put it away. The folded droid in the corner remained dark and uninterested. Somewhere out in the corridor, the turbolift chimed again.

He was standing there with his hands loose at his sides, not quite ready to decide what came next, when the far door slid open.

His father filled the doorway with a datapad in one hand and the particular expression Ben had learned to read before he could read most others—the one that meant Luke Skywalker had already considered three versions of this conversation and had arrived carrying the one that required Ben.

It meant a mission.
