The archive console was older than any terminal Ben had ever touched. Pre-Reformation manufacture, judging by the casing—brushed durasteel with inset indicator lights that glowed amber rather than blue. The display surface was dark, waiting.

Sera crossed to it without hesitation.

Her fingers found the activation sequence from muscle memory—a press, a chord of three keys, a pause, then two more. The motion was so fast and so certain that Ben registered it as a physical fact before his mind caught up with what it meant.

*She's done this before. She's done this a thousand times.*

The console hummed to life. Amber light bled across the display surface, and text began to scroll in a register Ben recognized but could not read quickly—archaic Basic, pre-Reformation administrative script, dense with legal formalism and technical abbreviations he had never needed to learn.

Sera's shoulders settled as the archive opened under her hands. She did not turn around.

"Where—" Ben started.

"Level Two protocol index," she said. Her voice was clipped, precise, the tone of someone reporting rather than explaining. "Gavran's personal archive. Access-coded to his senior staff."

"Which you were."

Her jaw tightened, but she did not answer.

Behind her, Torin moved to the console's secondary display. His hand found the control surface with the same unthinking familiarity—not the speed of Sera's activation, but the ease of someone who had worked beside these systems for years. He pulled a file tree into the holographic field above the console.

"Veranthos containment protocol," he said. "Indexed by operational phase. The terms are—" He paused, reading. "They are what you would expect from a war that had already gone on too long."

Ben stepped closer, trying to parse the text. The script was dense, the abbreviations opaque. He caught fragments—*containment boundary, anchor allocation, residual management*—but the syntax was alien, the legal register from a thousand years before his Order standardized its record-keeping.

"Can you read it?" Desh asked from the doorway.

"Barely," Ben said. "The language is—"

"Pre-Reformation administrative Basic," Sera said. "Gavran standardized the protocol documentation in this register. It was not the common tongue even then."

She pulled a section of the index into the main display. The text resolved into clearer script—still archaic, but structured as a formal protocol heading.

*Veranthos Containment Directive: Operational Parameters*

Below it, a subheading:

*Penance-and-Containment Protocol: Gavran's Commission*

Ben's chest tightened.

"Penance-and-containment," he said. The words felt wrong in his mouth. "That is what he called it."

"That is what it was," Sera said. She did not look at him. Her eyes were fixed on the display, and her voice carried a flatness that Ben recognized as the surface of something deeper. "Private penance. Private containment. No public record. No institutional acknowledgment."

"Because it was a war crime," Ben said.

The word hung in the air.

Sera did not confirm it. She did not deny it. She simply let it sit, and that silence was more damning than any answer she could have given.

Ben's mind was already moving, the way it did under pressure—not toward the emotion yet, but toward the architecture. The timeline. The connection that had been forming since the survivors woke.

"Veranthos predates the public settlement," he said. "The Ruusan Reformations—the real ones, the ones in the history records—they came after the Seventh Battle. After the New Sith Wars ended. But this protocol—" He gestured at the display. "This was written before that."

"Yes," Torin said quietly.

"Then the Reformations did not create this. They inherited it."

No one corrected him.

Ben looked at the display again. The text was scrolling, revealing more sections—*anchor protocol, containment maintenance, residual management*—but he was no longer reading it. He was seeing the shape of what it meant.

"The public record says the Reformations demilitarized the Jedi," he said. "Ended the Lords, centralized training, bound the Order to Republic oversight. That is all true."

"It is," Desh said. He had moved closer, his datapad out, cross-referencing something. "I am pulling the public text now. It matches what you were taught."

"Then the Reformations are real," Ben said. "But they are not the whole story."

He turned back to the display. The protocol index was still scrolling, and his eye caught a designation he had not noticed before: *Gavran, J.—Central Keystone: Keeper of the Veranthos Protocol.*

"Keeper," he said. "Not architect."

A pause.

Kael moved.

He had been standing at the edge of the group, arms folded, watching the display with the same detached attention he gave everything. But when Ben said *keeper*, Kael stepped forward. His hand reached past Sera's and tapped a control on the secondary display. The designation shifted, the text scrolling to reveal a longer entry.

*Gavran, J.—Central Keystone: Keeper/Custodian of the Veranthos Protocol (Containment Engineering)*

Kael did not explain. He did not look at anyone. He simply made the correction, held the display for a moment, and stepped back to his position at the edge of the group.

The silence that followed was heavier than any explanation.

Ben understood. Kael had not corrected the designation because he cared about archival precision. He had corrected it because *architect* and *keeper* were not the same thing, and the difference mattered to someone who had been conscripted into a system that had named him something he was not.

Sera was the first to break the silence.

"The protocol had two functions," she said. Her voice was still flat, still precise, but there was something underneath it now—a tension, a holding. "Containment of the wound Gavran's technique created. And penance for the technique itself. The private penance was the price of the public reform."

"The public Reformations were built to carry it," Ben said. The words came out before he had fully thought them, and he felt the shape of them as he spoke—the realization landing in his body before his mind had finished processing it. "The public settlement after Ruusan was real. But it carried a concealed layer underneath. The Veranthos protocol was the wound they buried so the public reforms could stand."

Sera met his eyes for the first time. Her expression was unreadable—not cold, not warm, but held at a precise distance.

"Yes," she said.

The word was simple. Final. It landed like a stone dropping into still water.

Ben stood in the center of the archive, the amber light of the display washing over him, and felt the ground shift under his feet. The history he had inherited was not false. The Reformations had happened. The demilitarization, the centralization, the oversight—all of it was real.

But it was incomplete. The public settlement had been built on top of a private penance, and that penance had been concealed so thoroughly that a thousand years of Jedi had not known to look for it.

*What else did they bury?*

The question surfaced and sank before he could hold it.

He looked at the three survivors. Sera stood at the console, her hand still resting on the control surface, her back straight, her face unreadable. Torin had stepped back from the display, his arms crossed, his expression carrying the weight of memory rather than revelation. Kael had not moved from his position at the edge of the group, but his eyes were on the display, and Ben saw something in his posture that had not been there before—a stillness that was not detachment but attention.

Three separate people. Three separate relationships to the truth.

And none of them had asked to be here.

Ben let out a breath he had not realized he was holding.

"We are going to need the rest of the archive," he said. "The protocol language. The anchor allocations. Everything Gavran wrote about what he did and why."

Sera nodded once.

"Then we find it," she said.

She turned back to the console, and her fingers found the next search sequence from muscle memory—a thousand years old and still faster than Ben's modern training.

The archive hummed around them, full of records no living Jedi had ever read, and Ben stood in the middle of it, feeling the weight of what he had just learned settle into his bones.

The penance remained unnamed.

But the room was no longer a room full of records.

It was a testimony delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it.
