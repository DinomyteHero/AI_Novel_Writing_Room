The training blade caught Ben across the shoulder, a solid, stinging impact that drove the air from his lungs.

He stepped back, disengaging. The hum of the training sabers filled the silence between his ragged breaths.

“Point,” the other Knight said, lowering her weapon. She didn’t gloat. She was older, a veteran of the last war, and her expression held professional assessment, not triumph. “Your guard dropped. Just for a half-second.”

Ben thumbed the deactivation switch. The blade vanished, leaving the smell of ionized air and his own sweat. He rubbed his shoulder, the muscle already tightening into a bruise. “I know.”

He’d felt it. Not fatigue, not distraction. Something else.

It had happened in the middle of a standard Shii-Cho exchange, their blades a blur of controlled motion. The Force was the metronome for a fight like this, the pulse that synchronized parry and thrust. For that half-second, the pulse had… stuttered. A note played slightly flat. His body had followed the old rhythm, but the music had changed, and his guard had dropped into empty air.

That was the third time this week.

“Again?” the Knight asked, resetting her stance.

“No.” The word came out sharper than he intended. Ben softened it with a shake of his head, a manufactured weariness in his shoulders. “I’m done. You’ve worn me out.”

She studied him for a beat, then deactivated her own blade. “Fair enough. Good session.” She clipped her hilt to her belt, gave him a nod that was neither warm nor cold, and left the salle.

The door hissed shut behind her. The silence that followed was immediate and heavy, broken only by the low, permanent hum of the Temple itself—the whisper of a thousand lives, a thousand presences in the Force, like the distant murmur of a river.

Alone, Ben let the facade drop. He wasn’t tired. His breath evened out in ten seconds flat. The ache in his shoulder was superficial, a badge of failure, not exhaustion.

He paced the length of the empty salle, boots scuffing on the polished floor. The evening light from the high windows cut long, amber bars through the dust motes. He’d chosen this time deliberately—late enough that most Knights were at dinner or in their quarters, early enough that the cleaning droids hadn’t yet started their silent rounds. He’d wanted the space, the quiet. He’d wanted to test a theory without an audience.

Now he had the space, and the theory was proving itself.

He stopped in the center of the room, closed his eyes, and sank into the first stance of Form III. No blade. Just the motion. He flowed through the defensive sequences, the shifts of weight, the precise angles of deflection. Muscle memory, drilled into him since childhood. His body knew these shapes.

Halfway through a complex riposte maneuver, his left foot planted a centimeter too wide. The balance was off. Not enough to make him stumble, but enough to break the form’s perfect geometry.

He opened his eyes. He hadn’t been using the Force. Not consciously. This was pure kinematics.

He started again, from the top. This time, he opened his awareness as he moved, letting the Force flow into his limbs, using it to guide his speed, to anticipate the imaginary strikes. The difference was subtle, a layer of surety beneath the physical act. For several minutes, it worked. He was a conduit, motion and energy in harmony.

Then it happened. Not a spike of wrongness, not a dramatic tear. A skip. A momentary absence, as if the river of the Force had hit a dry patch in its bed. His next pivot was a fraction too slow, his block a hair too low. The rhythm fractured.

Ben stopped moving. He stood in the silent salle, sweat cooling on his skin, and listened.

The Temple hummed around him. Life. Peace. Purpose. But beneath it, woven through it, was the dissonance. It had been there for months, a background irritant the Council had attributed to galactic recovery—the psychic aftershocks of the Abeloth crisis, the settling of old wounds. Everyone felt it to some degree. A lingering headache. A sense of static. Most had learned to filter it out.

Ben couldn’t filter it out. For him, it wasn’t static. It was a structural flaw.

He knew what a broken Force felt like. He’d grown up in the shadow of his cousin Jacen’s fall, had felt the galaxy warp under the weight of that choice. He’d stood on planets where Abeloth’s touch had left the Force scabbed and feverish. This was different. It wasn’t a wound that bled darkness. It was a… silence. A place where the music simply stopped. Where the lattice of light and dark, the framework that gave the Force its moral texture, went momentarily blank.

And it was getting worse. It was moving from a background hum to a active disruptor. Today, it had cost him a spar. In a real fight, it would cost him his life.

The frustration was a cold, hard knot in his gut. He was a Skywalker. He was supposed to have a preternatural connection to the Force, not a preternatural sensitivity to its malfunctions. Talking about it felt like admitting a weakness. *My magic’s broken. Can you fix it?* He could imagine the concerned looks, the gentle suggestions for deeper meditation, the unspoken suspicion that maybe, after everything he’d seen, Ben Skywalker’s perceptions weren’t entirely reliable.

So he didn’t talk. He observed. He cataloged. He got hit in training salles and blamed his reflexes.

The isolation of it was the worst part. Standing there, the only one in the room who could hear the fundamental note going flat.

He forced himself through two more forms, pushing the pace. The skips came more frequently—a missed step, a slight tremor in his hands. His body was fine. His mind was sharp. The variable was the Force itself. The realization settled in his chest, heavy and cold. This wasn’t trauma. This was truth. Something was happening, and he was alone in feeling the severity of it.

He was on his fifth repetition of a basic parry sequence when the ambient hum of the Temple shifted. A presence, familiar and vast as a solar wind, brushed against the edge of his awareness. Footsteps, deliberate and unhurried, sounded in the corridor outside.

Ben let his arms fall to his sides. He turned toward the door just as it slid open.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway. He held a datapad in one hand. His expression was the one he wore when the galaxy needed something—calm, focused, and utterly mission-ready.
