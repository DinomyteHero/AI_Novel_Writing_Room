The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Third time this week.

He stood with his guard still half-raised, processing it. The hit hadn't been fast. Hadn't been clever. His partner had telegraphed the angle from two moves out, and some part of Ben's nervous system had known it was coming — had already begun the parry — and then the Force had done that thing again, that half-step skip between intention and execution, and his arm had arrived a half-second late to a party that had already ended without him.

The other Knight lowered her training blade. She was good — quick, controlled, a clean technical style — and she had the decency not to say anything, which Ben appreciated.

"Good session," he said. "I'm done."

She tilted her head a fraction. Not skepticism, exactly. More the polite non-expression of someone choosing not to ask a question they'd already guessed the answer to.

"Fatigue," Ben added, as if she'd pushed. She hadn't. He deactivated his training saber, clipped it to his belt, and rolled his shoulder where the blade had connected. It didn't hurt. Training sabers weren't built to hurt. That was almost the problem — if it had hurt, he'd have had something concrete to catalog.

"Tomorrow," she said, and left.

He listened to her footsteps cross the salle and fade into the corridor. The door slid shut. The room settled back into its own sounds: the low circulation hum of the Temple's ventilation, the faint resonance in the walls that came from being surrounded by a few hundred Force-sensitives going about their evenings, the occasional distant clang from the equipment racks at the far end.

Ben stood in the middle of the floor and didn't move.

Fatigue. Sure. He was twenty-four years old and had been running the same combat conditioning regimen since he was seventeen. He'd sparred through concussions, through grief, through the particular bone-deep exhaustion of consecutive field deployments that left him running on stim packs and stubbornness. Fatigue didn't make him miss a parry he'd already read.

He unclipped the training saber and held it, unlit, at his side.

The wrongness was still there. It was always there now, had been for — he counted back — eleven weeks, give or take. Not painful. Not loud. Just present, like a note played slightly flat in a piece of music he'd known since childhood. Most of the time he could tune it out. During meditation he could almost convince himself he was imagining it. During a spar, when he needed the Force to close the gap between thought and motion, it cost him that half-second.

Three times this week. Twice the week before.

He started moving.

Not a drill — no partner, no target, no sequence. He walked through the opening form of Shii-Cho slowly, paying attention. Elbow angle, weight distribution, pivot point. His body knew this. His body had always known this; he'd been running forms since before he could reliably tie his own boots. The footwork was automatic, the blade positions precise even with the saber dark and the motions purely physical. Nothing wrong there. His muscle memory was intact.

He sped up. Moved into Makashi, then the Ataru sequences he'd spent two years drilling after the Corellian deployment, then the hybrid forms his mother had taught him that didn't have official names and probably never would. His body tracked all of it cleanly. Sweat worked down his back. His breathing stayed even.

He reached for the Force.

There it was. Present, yes — always present, he was a Skywalker, the Force had never in his life felt absent — but carrying that flat, wrong undertone that he couldn't quite locate. Like pressing a key on an instrument that had been tuned correctly in every register except one, and not being able to tell which string was off until you played the chord that needed it.

He stopped moving.

His body was fine. He'd just confirmed it, form by form, the way you'd check a ship's systems after a hard landing — methodical, exhaustive, slightly humiliating. His body was completely fine. The variable was the Force itself.

Ben stood in the center of the empty salle and let that sit.

The Order's consensus, such as it was, ran along predictable lines: post-Abeloth disruption, residual sensitivity in certain Force-perceptive individuals, probably clears up on its own, the Masters were monitoring it, nothing to indicate active danger. He'd heard variations of that assessment from three different senior Knights in the past month. They weren't dismissing him — they were measuring the same readings he was and reaching a different conclusion about severity.

The problem was that Ben had reference points they didn't.

He'd spent formative years adjacent to Jacen. Not the Jacen his father remembered, the one people built retrospective mythology around — the real one, the one Ben had watched from close enough to see the mechanics. And then Abeloth, which had been something else entirely, something that made Jacen look like a training exercise. He knew what it felt like when something was wrong with the Force at a level deeper than surface turbulence. He knew the difference between weather and a crack in the foundation.

This felt like the second one.

He didn't want it to. That was the honest version, the one he kept below the self-deprecating internal commentary he used to keep the honest version at a comfortable distance. He didn't want to be right about this. Being right meant the Order was wrong, which meant he was alone in it, which meant —

Boots in the corridor. He tracked them without thinking, the way you tracked any sound that broke a pattern.

The door opened.

His father stood in the doorway. Luke Skywalker, Grand Master, holder of more titles than any reasonable person needed, wearing a plain training tunic and holding a datapad with the particular quality of attention that meant he'd already read everything on it twice. He looked at Ben the way he sometimes did — not assessing, exactly, more like listening to something Ben wasn't broadcasting consciously.

The datapad meant a mission.

Ben clipped his training saber back to his belt and crossed the salle toward the door, the flat note still humming somewhere underneath everything, the crack in the foundation still there. His father's expression didn't change. It didn't need to.

A problem with a location. A problem with edges. Something Ben could actually do something about.

He'd take it.
