# Chapter 1

## Scene 01.01

The training salle smelled of sweat and ozone, the faint tang of recycled air doing what recycled air always did at this hour — carrying the ghosts of a dozen earlier sessions through the vents. Ben had been working with Rendell for twenty minutes, long enough for the rhythm to settle into something his body knew by heart. Strike, parry, pivot. Strike, parry, pivot. The training sabers hummed their low, familiar note.

Rendell feinted high and came in low, a sequence Ben had seen him favor since they were both padawans running the same drills under the same Masters. Ben's guard should have dropped into the block before Rendell's wrist finished its turn. It should have been automatic.

Instead his arms moved a half-second late, and Rendell's blade caught him across the shoulder with a sharp *crack* of contained energy meeting cloth and skin.

The sting was clean and specific — the kind of hit that told you exactly how badly you'd misjudged the timing. Ben stepped back, rolling his shoulder against the ache, and for a moment the wrongness pulled at his attention like a note played slightly flat in a piece he'd heard a hundred times. The ambient Force in the salle arrived behind the beat his body expected. It had been doing that all week.

Rendell deactivated his training saber. "You all right?"

"Fine." Ben killed his own blade and offered a half-smile that cost him nothing. "That's three this week. I think you're owed a rematch."

"Whenever you want it." Rendell was still watching him, the way you watched a sparring partner who'd just taken a hit he shouldn't have taken. "You've been off all session."

"Late nights. You know how it is." Ben kept his tone light, the words landing somewhere between truth and deflection. He was tired. He had been tired since the week started. Fatigue was a reason Rendell would accept, and it had the minor virtue of being almost true.

Rendell hesitated — one beat, two — and then nodded, the way Knights nodded when they'd decided not to press. "Get some sleep, Skywalker."

Ben waited until Rendell's footsteps faded into the corridor, until the turbolift chimed its arrival and departure, until the salle was empty except for the hum of the climate control and a cleaning droid working its way along the far wall. The droid paused at a scuff mark, considered it with the gravity of a Jedi examining an ancient text, and moved on.

He paced the salle floor once, twice, letting his boots find the familiar worn spots in the decking. The wrongness sat at the edge of his perception like a half-beat lag he couldn't shake. He had been telling himself it was fatigue. He had been telling himself it was the week, the accumulated weight of late shifts and poor sleep and the kind of restlessness that came from spending too long inside Temple walls.

He had been telling himself a lot of things.

Ben stopped in the center of the salle and raised his deactivated training saber. The form was an old one — one of the basic sequences they taught initiates in their first year, the kind of thing his body knew well enough to run in his sleep. He moved through it slowly, deliberately, not igniting the blade. Strike. Pivot. Guard. Step. The motions were correct. His feet found their marks. His shoulders turned at the right angles.

But the Force arrived a half-second behind the rhythm his body had known by reflex for years.

He felt it in his chest first — a tightening that had nothing to do with exertion. The lag was there, in the ambient field, the way the salle's Force-presence should have risen to meet his movement and instead arrived late, like a musician coming in a beat after the rest of the ensemble. It wasn't him. It wasn't fatigue. It wasn't the week.

Something was wrong with the Force itself.

Ben didn't articulate the thought. He didn't have the words for it yet, and he wasn't sure he wanted them. The realization landed as a physical sensation — a sharpening of focus, a stillness in his breathing, the specific quiet that came when you stopped pretending a problem was temporary. He lowered the training saber and stood in the empty salle, listening to the wrongness the way he'd been avoiding listening to it all week.

It wasn't getting better. It had been getting worse.

He had been handling it alone because no one else seemed to feel it. Rendell didn't feel it. The other Knights he'd sparred with this week didn't feel it. Ben had mentioned it once, obliquely, to Master Cilghal after a meditation session, and she'd asked if he'd been sleeping. He'd said yes and dropped the subject. It was easier than explaining something he couldn't name.

The cleaning droid finished its circuit and retreated into its charging alcove with a soft click. Somewhere down the corridor, the late-shift foot traffic continued — the occasional tread of boots, the distant chime of a turbolift, the muted voice of someone passing on their way to somewhere else. The Temple at night was never quite silent. It was lived in, the way Coruscant was lived in, the way any place became when enough people moved through it long enough.

Ben stood in the center of the salle and let the wrongness sit at the edge of his perception without fighting it. He didn't have a name for what he was feeling. He didn't have an explanation. What he had was a half-beat lag in the ambient field and a growing certainty that it wasn't going to resolve itself.

The door hissed open behind him.

Ben turned. Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and an expression Ben had learned to read years ago — the particular stillness that meant a mission was coming, and that whatever it was, it wasn't going to be simple.

Ben felt the shift in his chest before he could name it: relief, reluctant and sharp, that he might not be alone with this after all. And underneath it, the cost of what came next. He was going to have to say it aloud.

Luke met his eyes and waited.

## Scene 01.02

The datapad lay face-up on Luke's desk, its screen glowing blue against the polished wood. Seventeen flagged reports, clustered along a bearing that ran straight into the Outer Rim like a blaster bolt aimed at nothing in particular.

Ben hadn't sat down yet. He'd come through the door expecting a routine briefing — a follow-up to yesterday's sparring session, maybe, or another of the administrative check-ins his father scheduled when he wanted to talk without saying so. The datapad changed the shape of the room.

"Sit," Luke said. Not an order. An invitation with weight behind it.

Ben sat. He reached for the datapad before his mind caught up to what his hand was doing, and the part of him that noticed the impulse filed it away. His father's eyes tracked the motion without comment.

The first report was a standard survey scan — mineral composition, gravimetric readings, atmospheric density. The anomaly flag at the bottom was what stopped him. *Force readings inconsistent with known phenomena. Recommend follow-up.* The second report said the same thing in different words. The third. By the seventh, the phrasing had shifted from *inconsistent* to *anomalous* to something that looked like a junior analyst trying very hard not to sound alarmed.

Ben zoomed on the three readings clustered at the far end of the bearing. The sourness in the data matched what he'd been carrying in the back of his skull for weeks — a wrongness that didn't have a name yet, that he'd been treating like a private problem because naming it meant admitting how long he'd been feeling it.

He looked up. Luke was watching him with the particular stillness that meant he already knew what Ben was going to say.

"How long have you had these?"

"Three weeks." Luke's voice was even. Grand Master register, but worn lightly — the way he spoke to the Council, not the way he spoke to the galaxy. "The first four came through standard channels. The next six were flagged by the sector coordinator. The last seven I pulled myself."

Ben scrolled back to the top of the list. The dates confirmed what he already suspected. The earliest report had been filed two days before the wrongness had started keeping him awake.

"You've been sitting on this."

"I've been reading." Luke leaned back in his chair. The motion was casual; the look behind it wasn't. "So have you."

The words landed with the weight of something Ben hadn't said out loud. He kept his face still and his voice level. "I didn't have seventeen reports. I had a feeling."

"A feeling you didn't bring to me."

"I didn't have anything to bring." Ben set the datapad down. The blue glow threw his father's face into half-shadow, the lines around his eyes deeper than they'd been a year ago. "I still don't. Neither do these. They're flags, not answers."

"They're confirmation." Luke reached across the desk and tapped the screen, scrolling to the bearing overlay. "The pattern is too consistent to be sensor error. Too specific to be background noise. Someone else would have noticed by now if I hadn't been sitting on it."

The implication settled between them. Ben felt it in his chest — a brief, unwelcome warmth, the thing he'd wanted without knowing he wanted it. His father had been reading the same signals. Had been protecting the information, keeping it close, waiting for the right moment to hand it to the one person in the Order who'd feel it the same way.

The warmth curdled into something sharper. Luke was describing an anomaly. Ben was recognizing a frequency he'd lived inside since he was a boy.

"You've felt it," Ben said. Not a question.

Luke's hand stayed on the datapad. The pause before he answered was the only tell. "Something. A disturbance. Not close enough to name."

"Not close enough." Ben heard the edge in his own voice and didn't pull it back. "You're reading a blip on a scan. I'm reading—"

He stopped. The sentence he'd been about to finish wasn't one he could hand his father without handing him everything else along with it.

Luke waited. The silence stretched long enough that the ambient hum of Coruscant traffic bled through the window — a continuous low wash, speeders and haulers and the million small sounds of a planet that never stopped moving.

"I'm reading something I can't calibrate against," Ben said finally. "It doesn't match anything in the archives. It doesn't match anything I've felt before."

"But you know what kind of trouble it is."

Ben looked at him. His father's face was calm in the way that meant he was choosing calmness — the expression Ben had learned to read as a child, when he'd realized that Luke Skywalker's serenity was a discipline, not a default.

"Yes," Ben said. "I know what kind of trouble it is."

Luke set the datapad down. The motion was precise — the pad aligned with the edge of the desk, the screen dimming as it lost contact with his hand. When he spoke again, the Grand Master register had dropped away.

"I've felt something too." His voice was quieter now. Not the voice he used with the Council. The voice he used with Ben, when there was no one else in the room. "Not the way you're feeling it. Not as clearly. But I've felt it."

Ben's chest tightened. The warmth was back, undercut by something that felt a lot like standing on a ledge and realizing how far the drop was. His father had just handed him the thing he'd been carrying alone — had named it, had made it real — and the relief of that recognition was tangled up with the knowledge that it didn't change anything. Luke could feel the shape of the problem. He couldn't feel what Ben felt. The gap between them was still there, visible now in a way it hadn't been five minutes ago.

"Then you know why I can't take a team," Ben said.

Luke didn't answer immediately. His hands rested on the desk, still and patient, the way they'd rested on the controls of an X-wing through a hundred battles Ben had only read about. When he spoke, his voice carried the particular weight of a man who had already made the decision and was only now saying it out loud.

"I'm not offering you one."

The words landed cleanly. No apology. No explanation. Luke was trusting him with the mission and trusting him to carry it alone, and the trust was a gift and a cost wrapped around each other so tightly Ben couldn't separate them.

He stood up. The chair scraped against the floor, a small sound that felt louder than it was. He moved to the window and let his shoulders settle into the posture of someone who'd already accepted the assignment — back straight, weight balanced, hands loose at his sides. The Coruscant skyline stretched out below him, towers and traffic and the endless motion of a galaxy that had no idea anything was wrong.

"How bad do you think it is?" he asked, without turning around.

Luke's silence was the answer. Ben heard him stand, heard the soft click of the datapad being picked up, heard the three steps it took for his father to come around the desk and stop a pace behind him.

"I think," Luke said, "that you're the only person I trust to find out."

Ben didn't turn. He kept his eyes on the traffic, on the clean lines of the Temple's outer wall, on the faint sourness that coiled at the edge of his perception even here, even in the heart of the Order's strongest place. His father's presence was warm at his back — steady and familiar and not quite close enough to touch.

"I'll leave in the morning," Ben said.

He didn't ask for a partner. Luke didn't offer one. The silence between them held the shape of a choice neither of them was willing to name, and the weight of it settled into Ben's shoulders like a pack he'd been carrying long before he walked into this room.

## Scene 01.03

The preflight checklist had been complete for three minutes. Ben sat in the cockpit of the *Shadow*, his thumb hovering over the datapad, the Jedi partner roster glowing on the screen. Three names. Three Knights he had run missions with before, competent people who would answer a comm without hesitation.

His thumb slid past them and opened his personal contacts.

The motion was smooth, practiced. He did not pause to weigh the options. He did not tell himself Desh was the right call — quiet, deniable, experienced — though all of that was true. He simply did it, the way a pilot corrected for wind shear before the conscious mind registered the buffet.

The comm chimed twice before the pickup clicked.

"Skywalker." Desh's voice crackled over the long-range channel, tinny with static but warm underneath. "It's been six months. I was starting to think you'd found someone else to drag into your messes."

"There isn't anyone else," Ben said. "You're the only one stupid enough to say yes."

A dry laugh. "Flattery. You must need something expensive."

"I need a second body for a survey run. Outer Rim. Minimal contact, probably boring, possibly not." Ben kept his voice light, the same register Desh was using. "Standard charter rate plus hazard differential if it stops being boring."

"Where?"

"Veranthos system. Old ruins, nothing confirmed. I'll brief you en route."

The pause was brief — long enough for Desh to run the calculus Ben had not asked him to run. No questions about why a Jedi Knight needed a non-Jedi partner. No mention of the roster Ben had bypassed. No request for the rationale Ben could not give.

"When do we leave?"

"Now."

"Give me forty minutes. I'll meet you at the Drift Landing pad." Another pause, shorter this time. "Ben. It's good to hear your voice."

The channel closed before Ben could answer.

He sat in the cockpit, the datapad gone dark, and registered the relief settling into his shoulders. It was immediate and disquieting — the ease of someone who did not require explanation, who understood without being told what Ben was avoiding. Desh's tone had carried the weight of years neither of them discussed: the Galactic Alliance Guard, the dissolution, the thing they had both survived as teenagers in uniforms the Order had later disavowed.

Two beats. That was all the history required. Ben had served in the GAG at sixteen, too young to understand what he was being used for, old enough to recognize the smell of a cover-up by the time it ended. Desh had been his handler — older, sharper, the one who taught him that institutional loyalty and institutional trust were different things. When the Guard was dissolved and the investigations began, Desh had walked away without waiting to be asked. Ben had stayed. They had never talked about why.

Ben's hand moved to the ignition. The *Shadow* hummed to life, instruments cycling through their startup sequence. Hangar echoes filtered through the hull — service techs calling to each other, the distant whine of a repulsorlift being tested, the particular recycled-air tang of the Temple's vehicle bay. He had grown up in this hangar. He could navigate it blindfolded.

The ship lifted, rotated toward the exit vector, and angled for the sky.

---

Coruscant fell away beneath him. The Temple receded into the sprawl of the Senate District, then the curve of the planet swallowed it entirely. Ben guided the *Shadow* through the approach corridor, his hands moving across the controls with the familiarity of ten thousand flight hours. The ship responded the way it always did — responsive, predictable, the one thing in his life that never asked him to explain himself.

The wrongness did not diminish.

It should have. Leaving the Temple should have put distance between him and whatever was pressing against the edges of his perception. Instead the pressure sharpened, a sensation he could not name and did not try to — something like a missing handrail in the dark, the moment your hand reaches for support and finds only air.

His jaw tightened. Something was wrong with the wrongness itself.

---

Drift Landing was a civilian pad on the lower levels, far enough from the Temple that no one would think to look for a Jedi there. Desh was waiting when the *Shadow* touched down — a compact man in a practical jacket, a duffel slung over one shoulder, his expression unreadable in the landing lights.

He climbed the ramp without ceremony, stowed his bag in the cargo locker, and dropped into the copilot's seat. "You look terrible."

"Thanks."

"I'm serious. When was the last time you slept?"

Ben did not answer. Desh did not push. The silence settled between them, comfortable in the way of two people who had spent too many hours in cockpits together to need to fill every gap with words.

"Veranthos," Desh said, pulling up the nav charts on his console. "That's old Republic territory. Pre-Ruusan. What's out there?"

"I don't know yet."

Desh looked at him. One eyebrow rose slightly — the only sign that he had registered the evasion. "You don't know, or you don't want to tell me?"

"Both."

"Fair enough." Desh turned back to the nav computer and began plotting the jump. "I'll get us a vector. You focus on not flying into anything."

Ben guided the *Shadow* out of the landing pad and toward the orbital departure lane. The ship climbed through the atmosphere, the hull heating briefly before the cold of vacuum pressed in. Stars sharpened. The nav computer chimed as Desh locked in the first jump.

"Ready when you are," Desh said.

Ben reached for the hyperspace controls. His fingers touched the lever.

The wrongness did not fade. It held steady — a pressure that should have been ambient but was not, a disturbance that should have been location-bound but followed. His hand tightened on the lever.

"Ben?"

He pulled back.

The stars stretched into lines. The *Shadow* punched into hyperspace, the familiar thrum of the hyperdrive filling the cockpit. Ben's instruments flickered — once, twice — and then stabilized.

The wrongness persisted.

It should not have. Hyperspace was a different medium, a different relationship between mass and space. Disturbances in the Force dissipated across the jump. They did not follow. They did not sharpen.

But this one did. It sharpened, acquiring weight and direction — not diminishing but orienting, like a compass needle settling toward a bearing it had been seeking all along. The pressure stopped being a problem and became a destination.

Ben's breath caught. His hands stayed on the controls, steady through reflex rather than intention.

"What?" Desh asked.

"I don't know." Ben stared at the hyperspace tunnel streaming past the viewport. "But I think we're about to find out."

# Chapter 2

## Scene 02.01

The *Steady Returns* dropped out of hyperspace like a crate kicked off a loading ramp.

Ben's caf sloshed across the copilot console before his shoulders had finished registering the reversion shudder — hot liquid arcing over the nav display, splashing the comm panel, pooling in the gap between the primary sensor readout and the housing Desh had jury-rigged three months ago with a coupling that didn't quite seat flush.

"Kriff," Ben said, and it was more reflex than language.

He was already moving, sleeve pulled over his palm, mopping before the caf could find its way into anything that mattered. The cockpit smelled the way it always smelled — recycled air with a faint mechanical undertone, the lived-in funk of a ship that had carried people for longer than either of them had owned it — but now with caf layered over it, acrid and warm.

"Sorry," Desh said from the pilot's seat. He didn't sound sorry. He sounded like a man who had just wrestled his ship through a hyperspace reversion that shouldn't have been that rough and was still deciding how annoyed to be about it.

"You're not sorry."

"No," Desh agreed. "I'm curious."

Ben kept wiping. The caf had found the seam between two panels, and he worked his sleeve into the gap with the kind of focused attention that kept him from having to look at the nav display. The reversion shudder was still in his shoulders — not the physical jolt, that had passed, but the thing underneath it. The thing that had been there since Coruscant and was, he was beginning to understand, not going to resolve itself by being ignored.

"Curious about what?" Ben asked.

"About why the nav computer just aborted a perfectly good hyperspace route and dropped us into realspace like it was trying to shake something off the hull."

Desh's hands moved across the pilot's console with the ease of someone who had been flying ships longer than Ben had been alive. He tapped the sensor panel with two fingers, frowned at what he saw, tapped it again. The gesture was patient. Dry. A man who trusted his instruments enough to be annoyed by them when they misbehaved.

Ben finished with the caf and sat back in the copilot's seat. The nav display was still blinking amber — recalibration in progress, the computer chewing through star positions and trying to figure out where it was. Through the viewport, hyperspace blue had given way to the star-speckled black of realspace, and somewhere out there was the first survey world, and somewhere between here and there was the thing Ben had been trying not to think about since they'd left Coruscant.

"The reversion," Ben said. "Was it rough on your side too, or just mine?"

"Rough enough." Desh didn't look up from the sensors. "Nav computer kicked us out on an error it's still not explaining. Hyperspace route was clean when we jumped — I checked it twice — and now it's showing a drift correction that doesn't match any of the charts."

"Drift correction."

"Mm." Desh tapped the panel again, and this time the gesture had a little more weight behind it. "The kind of drift you get when your nav computer thinks it's somewhere it isn't. Except it's not reading as a positional error. It's reading as a bearing."

Ben's hands were still. He'd been reaching for the caf cup — the spare one, the one Desh had wedged against the console with a scrap of duratape because the holder on Ben's side had broken six months ago and neither of them had bothered to fix it — and now his hand had stopped halfway there.

"A bearing," he said.

"Directional. Consistent. Gets stronger the farther we go along the route." Desh finally looked at him. "You know what that sounds like?"

Ben knew exactly what it sounded like. It sounded like the thing his body had been tracking since Coruscant — the wrongness that sat at the base of his skull and tugged, faint and steady, in a direction he couldn't have pointed to on a chart but could have drawn with his eyes closed. The thing he'd been calling *unease* because he didn't have a better word for it and didn't want one.

"Sounds like a sensor problem," Ben said.

"It's not a sensor problem."

"You sound sure."

"I've been flying this ship for eight years." Desh's voice was flat, the operational deadpan he used when he was working through something and didn't want to be interrupted by someone else's feelings about it. "The sensors are fine. The nav computer is fine. The route was fine when we jumped. Something changed."

Ben picked up the caf cup. It was empty — he'd spilled most of it — but holding it gave his hands something to do that wasn't gripping the armrest.

"Something," he said.

"Something." Desh reset the sensor panel and watched the readings rebuild themselves. The drift came back immediately, same bearing, same intensity. Growing, if Ben was reading the numbers right. "That's the third time I've reset it. Same result every time. Whatever this is, it's not noise."

Ben didn't answer. He was looking at the sensor display, at the bearing indicator that was pointing in a direction his body already knew. The wrongness wasn't abstract anymore. It was on the instruments. It was directional. It was getting stronger.

And Desh could see it.

That was the thing that landed — not the confirmation, not the external witness to what Ben had been carrying alone, but the fact that Desh, who couldn't feel the Force, who registered in Ben's awareness as a warm absence rather than a presence, had found the problem with his hands and his instruments and his eight years of knowing his own ship. The thing Ben had been trying not to name was now sitting on a sensor display between them, and Desh was waiting for him to say something about it.

"Ben," Desh said.

"Yeah."

"You're quiet."

Ben looked at the caf cup in his hands. "That's the bad kind of quiet?"

"That's the kind of quiet where you already know what my sensors are telling me and you're trying to figure out how to say it without saying it."

The recycled air hummed through the cockpit vents. Through the viewport, the stars held steady.

"It's the bearing," Ben said. "The one I've been feeling. Since Coruscant."

Desh didn't ask what he meant. He'd been there — not on Coruscant, but in the years after, in the thing they didn't talk about. The thing that sat between them like a third crew member, comfortable and careful and never named. The thing that had taught Ben what it felt like to be around someone who didn't register in the Force, and why that was a relief.

"Same bearing," Desh said. It wasn't a question.

"Same bearing."

Desh reset the sensors one more time. The drift came back. Stronger.

"The first survey world's on scanners," Desh said. "Bearing's pointing right at it."

Ben's hands tightened on the armrest. The caf cup was still in his lap, empty, forgotten. Through the viewport, the stars were just stars — but the wrongness was a vector now, a destination, a thing that could not be outrun.

The cockpit settled into the quiet of two people who had learned, a long time ago, how to sit with hard things without filling the silence.

## Scene 02.02

The freighter's contrail had faded to a white scar against the atmosphere by the time Ben and Desh cleared the ridge. Below them, the ruins pushed up through wind-scoured rock like bones working free of a shallow grave.

Desh stopped at the crest, hands on his hips, and surveyed the valley with the flat assessment of a man who had spent too many years in intelligence to be impressed by anything on first sight. "Sixty years of surveys and nobody wrote this down."

"Nobody saw it," Ben said. He was already moving, boots finding the descent path without conscious direction. The terrain here was wrong in ways that had nothing to do with the Force — erosion patterns that didn't match the prevailing wind, tool-marks on exposed stone that caught the light at angles his training couldn't name. "The last team was geological. They weren't looking for structures."

"They weren't looking for anything buried under three meters of silt." Desh followed, loosening a scatter of gravel with each step. "Which raises the question of what else they missed."

Ben didn't answer. The question was the right one, but it was also the one he'd been turning over since the briefing — the gap between what the chart said and what his instincts kept flagging. The survey catalog for this sector was thin. Thin enough to be wrong, or thin enough to be convenient.

A discarded supply crate lay half-crushed against an outcrop near the valley floor, its stenciled markings bleached past legibility. Civilian-grade. Not recent, but not ancient either. Someone had been through here in the last decade — a prospector, maybe, or a smuggler using the system as a waypoint. The sector was flown through. It just wasn't *known*.

The ruins themselves were larger than the ridge had suggested. What Ben had taken for a single collapsed structure resolved into a complex — three distinct buildings, maybe four, their foundations cut directly into the bedrock. The exposed stone was dark, fine-grained, worked with a precision that had survived wind and time and whatever had buried the site in the first place.

Desh circled left, giving Ben space to work, his boots crunching on gravel at a careful distance. He didn't ask what Ben was looking for. He knew the rhythm by now.

Ben crouched beside the nearest wall. The surface was smooth where the wind had polished it, rough where the original tooling remained. He ran his fingers above the stone without touching it, mapping the cuts in the air before committing to contact.

The markings were precise. That was the first thing that registered — not crude, not weathered past recognition, but deliberate. Straight lines intersecting at angles that suggested a system. Not decoration. Not language, or not any language he knew. Something closer to notation.

He reached out.

His fingers brushed the cut.

The wrongness arrived before the touch was complete — a low vibration that traveled up through his hand and into the bones of his forearm, a sensation that had no business being there because the stone was *still*. It wasn't heat. It wasn't a hum in the audible range. It was a fractional error, the way a step you expect to be there isn't, the way a handrail gives a quarter-inch when you're already leaning on it.

His body recoiled before his mind caught up. He was standing. He didn't remember standing.

"Ben."

Desh's voice came from fifteen meters out, flat and careful. Not a question. An assessment.

"Give me a minute." Ben's own voice sounded steady. That was something. He flexed his hand, feeling the absence of the vibration more than he'd felt the vibration itself. The wrongness was gone now. The stone was just stone.

He didn't reach for the Force. Not yet. The Force was the problem.

He crouched again, slower this time, and pulled the datapad from his belt. The scanner activated with a soft chime, its display casting pale light across the carved surface. Ben began his catalog: angle of the primary cut, depth, tool-width, evidence of secondary working. He photographed the markings from three angles. He noted the erosion pattern and ran the age-estimate algorithm, watching the numbers resolve on the screen.

The datapad gave him a range. The lower bound put the ruins older than the Republic.

He recorded it. He did not record what his hand had felt.

Desh had moved closer while Ben worked, close enough to read the tension in his shoulders without crowding him. "What did you find?"

"Markings. Pre-Republic construction style, or something that looks like it. The tool-work doesn't match any tradition I can place." Ben stood, tucking the datapad against his hip. "I need to send this to my father."

"That's not what I asked."

Ben met his eyes. Desh's expression was neutral in the way that meant he was reading everything and filing it for later. They'd known each other long enough that the silence between them had its own vocabulary.

"I know," Ben said.

He walked to a flat shelf of rock a few meters from the ruins, where the transmission signal would clear the ridge. The datapad's interface was familiar — standard survey template, fields for geological context, structural description, age estimates, photographic attachments. He filled each one with the precision his training demanded.

The Force impression did not appear in any field.

He read the report twice before sending it. The first time to confirm the data was accurate. The second time to confirm the omission was complete.

The transmission went through. The acknowledgment pulse returned in under three seconds — Luke's personal receiver, routing through the relay network Ben had set up before they left Coruscant. His father would read the report within the hour. He would see the markings, the age estimate, the geological context. He would ask the questions Ben was not prepared to answer truthfully.

Ben stood at the edge of the ridge, datapad dark in his hand, and felt the quiet alarm settle into his chest like a stone dropped into still water. The decision had been made before he'd consciously framed it — somewhere between the recoil and the catalog, between the wrongness in his arm and the blank field on the template. He hadn't theorized. He'd simply done it.

The freighter's contrail was gone now. The sky was empty except for the pale disc of the system's primary, and the wind had picked up, carrying mineral grit that stung his cheeks.

Desh appeared at his shoulder, close enough to speak without raising his voice. "You're going to have to tell him eventually."

"I know."

"But not today."

Ben turned the datapad over in his hands. "Not today."

Desh nodded once, the way he nodded at mission parameters he didn't like but couldn't argue with. "Then we should move. We've got three more sites on the grid and daylight's not getting any longer."

Ben felt it in the Force before he saw it — a recognition, not a surrender. The wrongness here acknowledged him. The sensation wasn't comforting. It was unsettling in its specificity, like a door that had been waiting for someone who looked enough like him to pass.

His stance widened without him deciding to. His shoulders braced.

"Green's good," Desh said. "Green means open, right?"

"I think so."

The fortress door ground open.

The sound was low and sustained, stone grinding against stone after a thousand years of silence. Air spilled out through the widening gap — ancient air, sealed since the Jedi Lords had walked these halls, carrying a weight that Ben's lungs registered immediately. Staleness. Dust. The particular cold of rock that had never known sunlight.

Ben dropped his hand from the mechanism. The green light held.

Desh pulled a glow rod from his belt and thumbed it on. The beam cut through the darkness inside, picking out a corridor of the same weathered stone, walls that angled inward as they rose, a ceiling lost in shadow. "No welcoming committee."

"Not yet."

Ben reached into the Force ahead of them, feeling for movement, for presence, for the shape of the wrongness that had drawn them here. It wasn't a void. It wasn't a wound. It was structured — concentrated, directional, moving through the mountain like a held breath that had been waiting to exhale.

Something was alive in here.

He didn't say it. Desh would know soon enough, or he wouldn't, and saying it wouldn't change what they were about to walk into. Ben stepped across the threshold, and the ancient air closed around him like a hand that had been waiting a very long time.

Behind him, Desh swept the corridor with the glow rod and followed.

## Scene 04.02

The Operations level smelled of dust and dead circuits.

Ben swept his glowrod across the first bank of consoles, the beam catching a millennium of undisturbed silence — control chairs pushed back at precise angles, data terminals dark but intact, a command board whose buttons had been pressed by hands that turned to bone before the Republic was born. The air was dry. Preserved. The fortress had sealed this level when the power failed, and nothing had breathed here since.

Desh moved along the far wall, his own light picking out the seams of blast doors and the darker rectangles where equipment had been removed. "Communications array's fried. Power conduits are cold." He tapped a junction box with his knuckle. "Dead cold."

"They built for residual." Ben stopped at the central monitoring station — a horseshoe of screens arranged around a single chair bolted to the deck. The chair's occupant was long gone, but the position read command. Whoever sat here watched something. "The emergency systems are still drawing from somewhere."

He reached for the Force.

It came sluggishly, the way it had on the landing pad — the deadened quality he'd felt since they entered the valley, a wrongness he still couldn't name. But beneath the deadening, something else. A current. Faint, buried deep in the stone, running through conduits that hadn't been touched since the Ruusan Reformations were new law.

Ben pressed his palm flat against the console's surface and pushed.

The Force answered — not the clean flow of a training exercise, but a ragged pulse, like priming a pump that hadn't been used in a thousand years. The console hummed. A light flickered amber on the auxiliary board. Another followed, then three more, and the entire horseshoe lit in sequence, screens glowing pale blue in the dark.

"There," Ben said.

Desh turned. His face, caught in the console glow, showed nothing — which was how Desh looked when he was filing something under *problems to solve later*. "That's residual?"

"That's what residual looks like when the people who built it were Jedi."

The screens resolved. Not data — a map. A network of nodes spread across a star chart Ben didn't recognize at first, the sector boundaries wrong, the trade routes labeled in names that hadn't been used since the Old Republic fell. He oriented himself by the Core — there, the Deep Core systems, and spiraling outward the colonies, the Rim, the wild space beyond.

Most of the nodes were red.

Ben's eyes tracked the pattern before his mind caught up. Red meant dead. Dozens of points scattered from the Mid Rim to the Outer Rim, each one a location where something had been built and was now dark. A few glowed amber — degraded, still holding, still drawing power. The amber nodes clustered closer to the fortress's position, as if whatever they were monitoring had failed outward from a center.

One node flashed white.

BREACH.

The word pulsed on the display in characters that predated Basic standardization, but the meaning was unmistakable. Ben felt his breath catch. The Force around the console was wrong in a new way — not deadened now but *strained*, a hum at the edge of hearing that wasn't sound, a pressure behind his eyes that wasn't pain. The system was screaming, and the scream had been going on for centuries.

"Ben." Desh's voice was flat. "What am I looking at?"

"I don't know yet."

He did know, or was starting to. The fortress wasn't a tomb. It was a control center. The nodes weren't colonies or listening posts — they were part of the same system, the same purpose, and the one flashing BREACH meant something had already failed.

Desh was already moving. He pulled his datapad, framed the console display, captured three stills from different angles. Then he turned and photographed the auxiliary boards, the power routing diagrams stenciled on the wall in flaking aurebesh, the layout of the blast doors and the corridor they'd entered through. His movements were precise. Professional. His jaw was tight.

"Exits," he said, not looking up. "Two blast doors on this level. The main stairwell we passed, and a service ladder at the far end of the archive. I'm marking them now."

Ben heard what Desh wasn't saying. *I want to know how to leave.*

"Good."

"Ben." Desh lowered the datapad. In the blue console light his face looked older, the lines around his eyes deeper. "That word. The flashing one. You know what it means."

"I know what it means."

"Then you know we should be calling this in. Not walking deeper."

Ben looked at the display again. The amber nodes. The red. The one white pulse that wouldn't stop. He thought about calling Luke — what he'd say, what he'd ask for, what Luke would tell him to do. The answer was obvious. It was also wrong.

"If I call this in now," Ben said, "I'm calling it in blind. I don't know what's breached, what's holding, or what's sealed below. I don't know if this is a containment failure or a power failure or something else entirely. I need to see Level 3."

"You need to see it, or you want to?"

Ben didn't answer. The Force was pulling at him — not the strained hum of the console, but something below. Something alive. He'd felt it since they entered the fortress, a presence at the edge of his perception that he'd dismissed as the building's residual charge, the accumulated Force-echo of the Jedi who had worked and died here.

It wasn't an echo.

"Two exits," Ben said. "You've got them marked. If this goes wrong, you pull back to the ship and call Luke yourself."

"That's not the point."

"I know it's not."

Desh held his gaze for three seconds. Then he nodded once, the way he'd nodded before operations that had gone bad and operations that hadn't, the nod of a man who'd learned that arguing with a Skywalker was like arguing with a gravity well.

"After you," he said.

The stairwell to Level 3 was a spiral cut into the fortress's core, the steps worn smooth in the center where centuries of boots had passed before the centuries stopped. Emergency lighting ran along the wall in strips — not the pale blue of the consoles but a deeper blue, almost violet, casting the stone in colors that belonged underwater. The air changed as they descended. Warmer. Heavier. Charged with something that made Ben's skin prickle the way it did before a storm.

He stopped three steps down.

The Force was different here.

Not wrong. Not deadened. *Alive* — pulsing up through the stone like a heartbeat transmitted through bedrock, too deep to hear but impossible to ignore. Ben closed his eyes and let it wash over him, not reaching, just receiving.

Three rhythms.

One was disciplined — a steady cadence, controlled even in sleep, the Force-signature of someone who had spent a lifetime in practice and a millennium in stasis and had not forgotten either.

One was heavy — dense with something held back, a pressure behind a door that had not yet opened.

One was clipped — quick, sharp, the rhythm of a mind that moved faster than the bodies around it and had learned to wait.

People. Living people, sealed below.

Ben opened his eyes. The blue light flickered once, steadied, and pulled him downward.

His hand found the railing. His feet found the next step.

## Scene 04.03

Level 3 opened into a ring.

Ben stopped at the threshold, one hand braced against the doorframe. The chamber beyond was circular, maybe forty meters across, with a central shaft that dropped into darkness. Around the circumference, spaced evenly like hours on a clock face, stood twelve alcoves. Most of them were dark.

Emergency lighting ran in strips along the floor, casting blue shadows up the walls. The air tasted recycled and old — the same dead-station tang they'd been breathing since the landing bay, but heavier here, layered with something Ben's lungs registered before his mind caught up. Ozone. The faint electrical sharpness of active containment fields.

Desh moved up beside him. "That's a lot of chambers."

Ben didn't answer. He was counting.

Three alcoves still glowed — faint blue stasis fields flickering across their openings like heat shimmer over a landing pad. The light was weak, irregular, the kind of unsteady pulse that meant failing power regulation. Behind each field, a human shape hung suspended in the blue.

Nine alcoves were dark.

Ben walked. The ring's floor was durasteel grating, and his boots rang on it with each step, the sound bouncing up into the shaft and coming back thin. He passed the first dark chamber at a distance, close enough to see the figure inside — preserved, motionless, the stasis field that had held it long since collapsed. The body was intact. The face was calm. Whatever had killed this one, it hadn't been violence.

He kept walking. Second dark chamber. Third. Fourth.

The pattern was not a pattern yet. It was just a sequence of dead people in a ring, and Ben's mind was refusing to assemble the implication. His chest had gone tight somewhere around the fifth alcove, the instinct to move warring with the instinct to stop looking, and he kept walking because stopping meant standing still with what he was seeing.

Fifth dark chamber. Sixth.

Desh had gone the other direction around the ring. Ben heard his footsteps on the grating, slower than his own, pausing at each alcove. Neither of them spoke.

Seventh dark chamber. Eighth.

The three active chambers were clustered together on the far side of the ring — ten o'clock, eleven o'clock, twelve. Ben could feel them before he reached them. Not through the Force, not exactly. The stasis fields muffled perception, flattened it into something distant and underwater. But the signatures were there, distinct even through the distortion: one disciplined, one heavy, one clipped. Three people. Three minds that had been sealed inside these fields for a thousand years and were still, against every reasonable expectation, alive.

Ninth dark chamber.

Ben stopped walking. He had completed the circuit. Nine dark, three lit, and —

The twelfth alcove was not dark. It was broken.

The stasis field was gone. The alcove's interior was visible in the emergency lighting — durasteel walls scored with deep parallel gouges, radiating outward from the center like something had clawed its way free. The monitoring panel beside the opening still blinked, amber and red, still logging a breach that the timestamp said was weeks old.

Desh was already there. He had his hand inside the empty chamber, fingers tracing one of the gouges.

"These are systematic," he said. "Not structural failure. Something worked at this from the inside."

"Someone," Ben said.

Desh nodded. He didn't look up from the scoring. "Someone broke out. Recently."

The word *recently* landed wrong. The timestamp on the panel was precise — nineteen standard days. Nineteen days since one of the twelve had woken up inside a failing stasis field and decided not to wait for rescue. Nineteen days to move, to act, to get clear of the fortress and whatever it was the fortress had been built to contain.

Ben turned back to the three active chambers. The blue fields flickered. The figures inside didn't move, but their signatures pulsed faintly in his perception — not awareness, exactly, but presence. Something that registered his attention and responded at a level below consciousness.

Three people. Still sealed. Still dependent on systems that had already killed nine of their companions and let a tenth escape into a galaxy that wouldn't recognize them.

"Keep looking," Ben said. "I need to find the control level."

The shaft at the ring's center had a ladder. Ben descended.

Level 4 was smaller than the ring above — a single circular chamber built around the base of the central shaft, where the conduit network from all twelve alcoves converged into a nexus of cabling and power relays. The emergency lighting was dimmer here, concentrated around a single control station at the chamber's center.

Gavran sat at the station.

His body was preserved — the same stasis-adjacent effect that had kept the anchors intact, or something close to it. He was Human, or near-Human, his skin drawn tight over prominent cheekbones, his hair gone white and thin. His eyes were closed. His hands rested on the control surfaces in front of him, fingers still curved to the shape of the interface, as if he had been mid-adjustment when he stopped.

Ben had seen dead Jedi before. He had seen his mother's body, and Mara's death had been a wound he carried in a place the Force couldn't reach. This was different. This was not a death that had happened to someone. This was a death someone had chosen.

The posture told the story. Gavran had not slumped. He had not fallen. He had settled — the way a man settles into a chair at the end of a long watch, when the watch is over and there is nothing left to do but stop. His hands were still on the controls. His last act had been to hold the system together.

Ben stood at the edge of the control station and didn't touch anything. The monitoring log was still running on the primary display, lines of text scrolling past in a language the fortress's systems had already translated. Most of it was automated status reports — power levels, field integrity, anchor vitals. The last manual entry was dated three months ago.

*—containment holding at sixty percent. Anchor Seven failed this morning. I have rerouted what I can, but the cascade is accelerating. The three remaining are stable for now, but I do not know how much longer I can—*

The entry stopped. Not with a period. Not with a conclusion. Just a sentence that had been interrupted by something more urgent than writing, and then never resumed.

Ben looked at Gavran's hands. At the controls they were still resting on. At the quiet, settled quality of the body, which was not peace but something closer to completion.

He had held this together until he couldn't.

Ben didn't say anything. There was nothing to say. The grief was not the kind that needed words — it was the kind that settled into the shoulders, a weight that wasn't going anywhere. Gavran had stayed. Had kept the seal intact while the anchors failed one by one, had rerouted power and adjusted fields and written status reports that trailed off mid-sentence, and then he had died at his station because there was no one else to take the watch.

Until now.

Ben turned away from the body. He climbed back up to Level 3, where Desh was waiting beside the three active chambers.

"The control station's below," Ben said. "Gavran's dead. He's been dead for months."

Desh absorbed that without comment. His face did what it always did with bad news — went still and practical, filing the information for later. "The three?"

"Still alive." Ben moved to the nearest active chamber — ten o'clock, the disciplined signature. The stasis field hummed at his approach, a low vibration he could feel in his teeth. "The controls are responsive. I checked one on the way up."

He reached out and touched the chamber's interface panel. The field flickered, then steadied, the hum deepening. Behind the blue shimmer, the figure didn't move, but the signature sharpened — a brief pulse of awareness, like someone stirring in sleep and then settling again.

One disciplined, one heavy, one clipped. Three distinct personalities, three distinct wills, each one a person who had been sealed in this ring when the Republic was young and the Jedi Lords still ruled and the galaxy still believed you could build a prison out of living people and call it necessity.

The hum carried a question. The three signatures waited.

Ben's hand stayed on the panel. He didn't wake them. That decision belonged to the next chapter, and he could feel its weight already — the obligation settling into place beside the grief, the recognition that he was now the only person standing between these three and the thing that had already escaped.

Desh moved up beside him. "What's the call?"

"Not yet," Ben said. "We need to know more before we wake them."

"And the one that got out?"

Ben looked at the empty chamber — the gouged walls, the blinking emergency flags, the nineteen-day timestamp still scrolling on the panel.

"That," he said, "is the problem we're going to have to solve first."

# Chapter 5

## Scene 05.01

Ben's hand rested on the stasis release lever. The metal was cold, the kind of cold that had been sitting in the dark for a thousand years. Desh stood three paces back, close enough to move, far enough to give Ben room to be the Jedi.

"Point of no return," Desh said.

Ben pulled the lever.

The first chamber's field dissolved with a sound like held breath released — a long, low hiss that became a pressure drop Ben felt in his chest. Emergency lighting flickered once and steadied, casting the stasis ring in amber and shadow.

The woman inside the chamber opened her eyes.

She came out of stasis the way a soldier comes out of a drop pod — body moving before consciousness caught up. One moment she was prone on the stasis slab; the next she was in a combat crouch three meters from the chamber mouth, weight balanced forward, hands empty but positioned to receive a weapon that wasn't there.

Her eyes tracked the room. Found Ben. Found Desh. Catalogued exits, sightlines, threat vectors. All of it before she spoke.

And underneath the physical movement, something else arrived — a Force presence that cut through the ambient wrongness of the fortress like a blade through fog. Sharp. Disciplined. Shaped by training Ben recognized as Jedi but couldn't place, the way a musician recognizes a scale played in a tuning system a thousand years old.

She was wearing the same simple tunic the other sleepers wore. Her dark hair was cut short, practical. A thin scar ran from her temple to her jaw on the left side — old enough to have healed white, precise enough to have come from something sharp.

She straightened. Her breathing was controlled, but Ben could see the effort it cost her.

"You are Jedi," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice was formal, each word placed with care. No contractions. "Identify your Order and your commander."

Ben kept his hands visible. "Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight. There's no commander here — it's just the two of us, and we're the ones who woke you."

Her eyes narrowed. The scrutiny felt physical, a pressure against his presence in the Force that he hadn't expected from someone who'd been unconscious for a millennium. She was reading him, and she was good at it.

"Skywalker," she repeated. The name meant nothing to her. "I am Sera Varik, Jedi Knight of the Army of Light. How many others have been revived?"

"None yet. You're the first."

He moved to the second chamber's release before she could ask another question. The lever resisted for half a second — old machinery, stiff from disuse — then gave. The field collapsed with the same exhale of pressure.

The man inside staggered out.

He was taller than Ben had expected, broad-shouldered, with a face that had been handsome once and was now weathered into something more interesting. His hands caught the doorframe and held it, knuckles white, the way a man holds something solid after a long time falling.

His Force presence arrived differently than Sera's. Where hers was an edge, his was a weight — warm, heavy, settled deep in the room like something that had been there a long time and intended to stay. Ben felt it press against his awareness and had no name for what it was. Not dark. Not light in any way he'd been taught to recognize. Just *present*, the way a large animal in a small room is present.

The man looked up. His eyes found Ben's, and after a moment he smiled — a tired, genuine smile that rearranged the exhaustion on his face into something almost gentle.

"Well," he said. His voice was warm, the vowels slightly broader than Sera's, the rhythm more fluid. "You're not Gavran."

"Ben Skywalker. Jedi Knight."

"Torin Hal." He released the doorframe, tested his balance, and stayed upright. "Also a Jedi Knight, though I suspect that means something different where you're standing than it does where I'm lying down." He glanced at Sera. "Varik. You're still alive."

"So are you," Sera said. The words were flat, but something passed between them — a recognition that held more than the words carried.

"More or less." Torin's gaze moved past them, tracking the dark chambers, the emergency lights, the empty one with its field already dissolved. His expression didn't change, but Ben felt the weight of his attention land on that empty chamber and stay there.

Sera was still watching Ben. "What year is it?"

Ben had known the question was coming. It still landed like a physical blow.

"Forty-seven ABY," he said.

The silence that followed was the kind of silence that has a shape. Sera's face didn't change, but something behind her eyes went very still — the look of a person doing math they didn't want to finish. Torin drew a breath and held it, and the warmth in his presence flickered once, the way a candle gutters in a draft.

A thousand years. Ben watched them each arrive at the number, and he watched what the number did to them.

Sera's jaw tightened. She said nothing. The discipline of it was almost worse than a reaction would have been.

Torin exhaled slowly. "A thousand years," he said, and the words were quiet, almost conversational, the way a man might note a change in the weather. "I wondered if it would be that long."

Ben didn't know what to say to that. There wasn't anything to say.

The third chamber's release was waiting.

He pulled the lever. The field hissed away, and the man inside stepped out without sound, without stumble, without any of the disorientation the other two had shown. He moved to the nearest wall and put his back against it in a single fluid motion, and then he was still.

Kael Drenn was leaner than Torin, darker, with the kind of watchfulness that didn't come from training alone. His eyes moved across the room — Ben, Desh, Sera, Torin, exits, sightlines, angles — and his expression gave away nothing at all.

His Force presence arrived clipped and guarded, folded tight against him like a cloak drawn close. Trained. Controlled. But the register was wrong — not Jedi, not Sith, not anything Ben had encountered before. It felt like a language he didn't speak.

Kael said nothing.

Sera turned to look at him. "Kael."

He met her eyes. Acknowledged her with the barest inclination of his head. Still said nothing.

Torin broke the silence. "He's always been like that. You get used to it." The warmth was back in his voice, but it was thinner now — a conscious effort rather than a natural state. "Kael Drenn. He was a late addition to the anchoring. Not Army of Light. We never did get the full story."

Kael's gaze shifted to Torin, held for a beat, and returned to scanning the room. If he had an opinion about being discussed in the third person, he didn't offer it.

Ben became aware of Desh moving — a quiet shift of weight, a hand resting near but not on his sidearm. The operational part of Ben's brain noted it and filed it. Desh was reading the room the same way Kael was, and he'd arrived at the same conclusion: three unknown Force-sensitives, one of them already combat-ready, and no backup within call.

"Three," Sera said. She was looking at the stasis ring now — the twelve chambers, the nine that were dark, the three that had held them. "There should be more."

"There were," Ben said. "Most of the chambers failed. We don't know when."

Sera absorbed that. Her expression didn't change, but her presence in the Force sharpened further, the edge of it pressing against Ben's awareness like a blade held just above the skin.

Torin was the last to orient. He'd been standing still, one hand braced against the doorframe, his attention moving slowly across the room as if he were memorizing it. When he spoke, his voice was quiet.

"Who got out?"

He was looking at the empty chamber. The field had been dissolved from the inside.

Ben followed his gaze. The chamber was dark, the stasis slab empty, the control panel dark. Someone had woken before the others. Someone had left.

"One of the anchors," Ben said. "We found the chamber empty when we arrived."

Torin didn't look away from the dark chamber. "Which one?"

"We don't know yet."

The silence that followed was different from the silence that had followed the year. That silence had been grief. This silence was the kind that came before a fight — the moment when everyone in the room realizes the situation is worse than they thought, and no one wants to be the first to say it.

Kael still hadn't spoken. His back was against the wall, his eyes on the empty chamber, and his silence was the loudest thing in the room.

## Scene 05.02

Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Ben watched her fingers find the activation sequence—not guessing, not decoding, just touching the interface the way he might key in his own quarters on Coruscant. The console hummed to life. A bank of holos flickered amber, then blue, then steadied into a lattice of access nodes that predated the Temple architecture by centuries.

She had been faster than his modern training.

The wrongness that had been pressing at Ben's senses since they entered the fortress sharpened into something concrete. His chest tightened. The hum of the console registered as too loud, the precise angle of Sera's shoulders as too composed, the sealed case holding the Codex fragment as too heavy for the table it rested on. His instinct pushed toward action—toward asking a question, demanding a translation, doing something—but his body held him still. The archive was activating for her. It would not have activated for him.

Desh stood near the door, arms crossed, watching the room with the flat assessment of a man who had learned patience in places where impatience got people killed. He said nothing. Ben was grateful for the silence.

"Records index," Sera said. Not to Ben. To the console.

The display shifted. Names and dates scrolled in a script Ben recognized as Old Galactic Standard—the formal register of the Jedi Lords era, preserved in Temple archives but rarely accessed outside historical scholarship. He could read maybe one word in three. Sera read it the way he read mission briefings.

Torin stepped forward before Ben could frame a question. His hand moved across the display, pulling a timeline into the holographic field, and the gesture carried the same thousand-year-old familiarity Sera's had—a man reaching for a shelf in a kitchen he had not seen since childhood and finding it still there.

"This is the public record," Torin said. His voice held a storyteller's inflection, warm and wry at the edges. "The version the galaxy received. Ruusan. The Reformation. The end of the Army of Light. All very noble. All very necessary." He tapped a date. The display expanded, showing a cascade of legislative actions, military demobilizations, the formal dissolution of the Jedi Lords' temporal authority. "Every Jedi learns this version. I would wager you did."

Ben nodded. His voice, when he found it, came out steadier than he felt. "The Reformations ended the Jedi Lords era. Centralized the Order under the Council. Separated the Jedi from galactic governance. Established the modern Temple system."

"The public version," Torin said again. He was not mocking. He was drawing a distinction Ben had never thought to look for. "Gavran wrote it. He was very good at writing things that were true enough to satisfy inquiry without being true enough to invite it."

Ben's focus tightened. The timeline was not what he had been taught. The names were the same—Gavran, the Council of First Knowledge, the post-Ruusan settlement—but the causal chain was wrong. Or rather: the causal chain he knew was missing a link. Something had happened between the end of the war and the beginning of the Reformations. Something the public record did not name.

"What did he leave out?" Ben asked.

Sera did not answer immediately. Her composure was absolute—a century of discipline worn like armor—but Ben caught the micro-expression at the corner of her mouth. Not satisfaction. Recognition. She had waited a thousand years for a Jedi to ask that question.

Kael moved.

Ben had almost forgotten he was there. The monk had been standing at the edge of the console's glow, still and silent, the way he had been still and silent since they woke him. Now he reached past Sera—not pushing, not crowding, just extending one hand with the economy of someone who did not waste motion—and touched a single entry on the display.

"Not left out," Kael said. "Edited."

The word he had touched was a date. The display corrected itself, the Old Galactic Standard characters shifting, and Ben saw that the original entry had been altered. A single digit changed. The correction was small. It was also the first voluntary thing Kael had said since the survivors woke.

He did not explain it. He simply made it and stepped back.

Ben registered the significance before he processed the content. Kael had broken his silence for something that mattered to him—a detail, a precision, a truth he was not willing to let stand wrong. The monk's brevity landed heavier than explanation would have. Sera was the instrument. Torin was the keeper. Kael was the correction.

They were not unified in their testimony. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

"The Reformations," Sera said, "were public reform and private penance."

She did not elaborate. She did not soften the institutional fact with context or apology. The sentence landed clean and complete, delivered with the composure of someone who had waited a thousand years to say it out loud to a Jedi.

Ben felt it land before his intellect caught up.

His body registered the impact—a vertigo-like drop in his center of gravity, a sudden clarity that came with the understanding that everything he had been taught about the Order's origins was incomplete. The hum of the console receded. The blue glow of the holographic timeline seemed to sharpen, each entry a testimony he could not read but whose weight he could feel. The Codex fragment in its sealed case sat at the edge of his vision, unopened, waiting.

Penance.

The Order he had been raised inside—the institutional identity he had never questioned, the history he had learned as a youngling and recited as a Padawan and carried as a Knight—had been built on what Gavran chose not to say. The lie was not accidental. It was structural. The Reformations were not merely a reorganization. They were an apology. A cover. A containment.

For what?

Sera did not say. She did not need to. Ben could read the rest of it off her face—the discipline that was holding something back, the precision that was choosing its moment. She had given him the sentence. The explanation would come when he was ready to hear it.

The moral weight of the question settled into his shoulders. Not despair. Not yet. A new clarity about what he was responsible for, and what he did not yet know.

Torin was watching him with something that might have been sympathy. Kael had retreated to the edge of the light, his single correction hanging in the display behind him. Desh had not moved from the door, but his expression had shifted—the operational flatness giving way to something more careful, a man who understood that the room had just rewritten itself and was waiting to see what Ben would do with the new text.

The archive was no longer a room full of records.

It was a testimony delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it. Sera the instrument. Torin the keeper. Kael the correction.

Ben braced for what came next.

## Scene 05.03

The monitoring logs for the empty chamber ran for centuries.

Ben stood at the flickering display on Level 1, the fortress's recycled air cold against his face, and watched the data scroll backward. The earlier entries were sparse—automated pings, atmospheric checks, the kind of baseline telemetry a containment system generated when nothing changed. Then the pattern shifted. Small anomalies began appearing at regular intervals. Temperature fluctuations. Pressure differentials. Power draws that didn't match any scheduled maintenance cycle. The entries grew more frequent as the centuries rolled forward, the intervals between them shortening, until the last two hundred years read like a patient, methodical conversation between the chamber and whatever was inside it.

The readable portion ended abruptly. Two months ago, the logs dissolved into corruption—not the random scatter of degraded storage, but a clean, systematic wipe that left nothing but static.

Desh leaned over his shoulder. "That's not hardware failure."

"No," Ben said. "It's not."

"Someone didn't want us reading past that point." Desh straightened, his face unreadable in the display's blue-white glow. "Which means there's something past that point worth reading."

Ben pulled back from the display. The weight of the timeline settled across his shoulders—not as a thought, not yet, but as a physical tightening that made him want to roll his neck and didn't let him. "Show me what you found in the chamber."

Desh had catalogued it with the same methodical patience the escapee had used to erase the logs. He walked Ben through it on a datapad: terminal access records from stations the fortress's automated systems shouldn't have allowed. Equipment lockers opened and resealed, their contents inventoried against the manifest and found short. Supply caches depleted in increments too small to trigger alerts—a ration pack here, a medkit there, a portable power cell lifted from a stack of twenty. The pattern was so careful, so unhurried, that Ben had to remind himself he was looking at evidence of escape, not routine maintenance.

"Whoever this is," Desh said, "they didn't break out. They walked out. Slowly."

"How long?"

"Hard to say without the logs. But the erosion pattern inside the chamber—" He paused, scrolling to an image capture. "That's not weeks of work. That's not even years."

Ben looked at the image. The chamber's interior surface had been worn away in concentric rings, layer after layer of material removed with a precision that spoke of time and patience and a clear understanding of what the containment field could and couldn't detect. The edges were smooth. No scoring, no gouging, no evidence of panic or desperation. Just work, repeated across centuries, until the field had nothing left to hold.

Sera was inside the chamber when they returned to Level 3. She stood with one hand pressed flat against the eroded surface, her head bowed, her breathing controlled in a way that Ben recognized—the rhythm of someone steadying herself against something she hadn't expected to feel.

She didn't turn when they entered. "The technique is familiar."

Ben waited.

"I have seen this pattern before." Her voice was precise, each word placed with the care of someone building a sentence that would hold weight. "Acolytes of the Brotherhood trained in sustained erosion. It was considered—" She stopped. Her hand withdrew from the wall. "It was considered a discipline of patience. A test of will."

She said nothing else. The silence stretched long enough that Ben felt the question form in his throat—*what aren't you telling me*—and then he swallowed it. Sera's shoulders were tight in a way he hadn't seen before. Her composure had cracked for half a second when she touched the wall, and she had rebuilt it with the speed of long practice, but the crack had been there.

If Sera was shaken, the threat wasn't routine.

Ben filed that alongside the weight in his shoulders and said nothing.

Torin stood in the doorway.

He hadn't been there a moment ago—Ben was sure of it, would have sworn to it—but there he was, one shoulder against the frame, arms crossed, his face caught in the half-light from the corridor. He was looking past Ben, past Sera, into the chamber's eroded heart.

"She knew what she was doing," Torin said.

The observation was mild. Warm, even—the kind of thing Torin said when he was thinking aloud, inviting the room to think with him. But something in the inflection landed wrong. Ben heard it the way he might hear a note played slightly flat in a piece of music he knew well: not wrong enough to stop the performance, but wrong enough that his attention snagged on it.

*She knew what she was doing.*

The warmth was there. The ease was there. But underneath it, or beside it, or threaded through it in a way Ben couldn't isolate, was something that didn't sit. Not a warning. Not a tell. Just—a degree off.

Ben didn't interpret it. He didn't have a place to put it. He filed it the way he'd filed Sera's silence, and the weight in his shoulders, and the two-month gap in the logs, and he turned back to the chamber because the chamber was the problem he could solve.

"Two months," he said. "The logs go dark two months ago. The physical evidence says she was active after that—terminals, equipment, supplies. She didn't just escape. She prepared."

Desh nodded. "She's been moving through the fortress for weeks. Maybe longer."

"Then she's not in the fortress anymore."

Ben was already pulling up the fortress schematic on his datapad. The map rendered in wireframe blue, Level 3 at the center, the access corridors branching outward. He overlaid the escapee's activity timestamps—the terminal accesses, the supply withdrawals, the door logs Desh had managed to reconstruct from the corrupted data.

The pattern was methodical. Each stop served a purpose. Each movement brought her closer to the outer perimeter.

The direction was clear.

"The Corridor," Ben said.

The word landed in the cold recycled air and stayed there. No one argued. No one asked for clarification. The chamber had told them everything it was going to tell them, and what it told them was that they were already behind.

Ben looked at Desh. "Pull every monitoring packet from the last three months. Corrupted or not. Start reconstructing what you can. I want to know what she took, where she stopped, and how fast she was moving."

Desh nodded once. "First light?"

"First light."

The schematic flickered on Ben's datapad. The fortress sat at the edge of the Corridor like a lock on a door that was already open. The escapee was out there somewhere, weeks ahead, moving with purpose and patience and centuries of preparation.

Ben didn't know her name. He didn't know what she intended. He knew she'd had time to plan, and time to move, and that the gap between her departure and his arrival was wide enough to hide almost anything.

His shoulders registered it before his mind did. The weight settled, and this time he let it.

## Scene 05.04

Ben spread the data across the command table in the fortress's upper chamber. Corridor maps unspooled beside seal readings, the chamber file with its name-shaped blank sitting at the edge like a question no one had asked yet. The wrongness he'd felt spreading through the wider sky was still there, still diffused, but it had a shape now. Absence.

His hands kept moving because stopping meant sitting with the incomplete picture.

Sera and Torin worked through the archive materials beside him, their hands finding patterns in the data Ben had pulled from the fortress systems. Torin's arrangement carried an ease that looked like warmth—materials grouped by operational relevance, the kind of sorting someone did when they'd trained others to follow their lead. Sera's carried precision without the warmth, every file placed exactly where it belonged and nowhere else.

Ben watched Torin's hands move and saw someone who had done this a thousand times before. Literally. The thought landed and he let it pass without comment.

"The monitoring packets are corrupted," Desh said from the secondary console. "Whatever broke containment scrambled the automated logs. But the response-quadrant processing on the fortress network is still intact." He looked up. "I can have the escapee's name by morning."

"Morning," Ben said.

"Overnight reconstruction. The packets need to run through the quadrant's archival processor, and that takes time." Desh's voice was matter-of-fact, the same operational flat he'd used when they'd worked together in the GAG. "But it'll resolve. The data's there. It just needs to be reassembled."

The timeline crystallized. Ben felt it settle into place like a lock cycling closed. The escapee had two months of head start. The seal was failing regardless. The survivors depended on him—three people a thousand years out of time with no resources, no allies, and no understanding of the modern galaxy. And Desh was already assuming they would leave. Already assuming they would pursue.

Ben did not name it as a choice. The pattern was closing too fast for naming.

"How long until Luke's reply window opens?" he asked.

Desh checked the comm logs. "Standard relay lag for this sector. If you sent now, you'd have a response by mid-morning tomorrow. If you wait—"

"If I wait, I lose the initiative."

"Yes."

Ben pulled his datapad from his belt. His hands moved with the precision of someone executing a plan he had not consciously made. The report he composed was partial—enough to explain the delay, the discovery of the fortress, the seal degradation that required immediate investigation along the Corridor. Not enough to reveal the scope. Not the survivors. Not the escapee. Not the word *help*.

He did not say *help*.

Sera watched him from across the table. Her expression gave nothing away, but her stillness was the kind that came from discipline rather than calm. Torin's warmth had thinned slightly, the easy storyteller's register replaced by something more watchful. Kael stood near the chamber's entrance, arms crossed, saying nothing. He'd been saying nothing for most of the hour, and Ben had stopped expecting him to fill the silence.

The transmission went through before Ben's conscious mind could catch up to what his hands had just done.

"There," he said. "Luke knows we'll be out of contact for a few days. He doesn't need to know more than that."

Desh looked at him for a long moment. The operational flat didn't shift, but something behind it registered—a calculation, a reassessment, a man who'd spent years reading between the lines of official reports and recognizing what wasn't being said.

"Alright," Desh said. "I'll have the name by morning. The packets will finish overnight."

"Good." Ben pocketed the datapad. "We lift now."

The Steady Returns strained under the weight of five passengers. The ship had been built for two, and the engines protested as she climbed off the mountain plateau, the fortress shrinking below them until it was just another shape in the uncharted world's grey terrain.

Ben kept his eyes on the nav display. The Corridor stretched ahead of them, a line of systems that had been contested ground a thousand years ago and were now just names on a chart. Somewhere along that line, an unnamed anchor was moving. Someone who had escaped weeks before the cascade, whose breakout had accelerated the system failure that had woken the three survivors now sitting in Ben's ship.

Sera sat in the copilot's seat, her hands hovering above the modern cockpit interfaces without quite touching them. Close but restrained. Torin stood behind her, observing the readouts with the focused attention of someone cataloguing what had changed in a millennium. Kael had found a corner of the cramped cabin and settled into it, deliberately still, the way someone did when they'd learned to make themselves small in confined spaces.

The wrongness was still there, diffused through the wider sky, present but spread thin now that they were climbing away from the fortress. Ben felt it as a faint wrong note in the ship's recycled air, a deadened tang that made the familiar cockpit feel slightly unfamiliar. Not enough to impair. Enough to notice.

He checked the nav display again. The course was locked. The Steady Returns was climbing.

The monitoring packets would resolve the escapee's name overnight. By then they would already be in pursuit, committed to the Corridor with incomplete information and a ship that wasn't built for what they were asking of it. The seal was failing. The clock was tightening on all three fronts—degradation, head start, ignorance—and Ben had just chosen to face all three without institutional backup.

He did not feel heroic. He did not feel triumphant. He felt braced.

The plateau was a grey smudge now, barely visible through the cockpit viewport. Ben watched it shrink and let the quiet cost settle into his awareness. The pattern had closed before he'd named it. He was already committed.

The Steady Returns kept climbing.
