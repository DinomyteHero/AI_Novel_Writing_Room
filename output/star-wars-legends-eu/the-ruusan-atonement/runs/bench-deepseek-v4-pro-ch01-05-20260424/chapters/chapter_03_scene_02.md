Desh pried his multitool under the corroded edge of the access panel and leaned into it. The metal gave with a shriek that echoed down the corridor longer than it should have.

"Old," he said.

Ben held the glow rod steady. The beam caught dust swirling in air cold enough to taste — recycled and dry, the way shipboard life support tasted after too many cycles without a scrub. His breath came visible. "Can you bridge it?"

"Give me a minute."

The coupler was a modern piece of kit, Alliance-standard interface, designed to talk to storage media manufactured in the last thirty years. The port Desh was jamming it into predated the Republic. Ben watched him work — the careful pressure, the angle adjustment, the muttered curse when the connection sparked and died.

"Again," Ben said.

"I know how to do my job."

The second spark held. The coupler's indicator flickered amber, then green. Desh sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on his trousers. "There. Whatever's on there, we can read it now."

The console woke in stages. First the power cells cycling up — a subsonic thrum Ben felt through the soles of his boots before he heard it. Then the display surface, flickering blue-white, shedding dead pixels like scab tissue. The holographic field stabilised and threw a star map into the air between them.

Ben stopped breathing for a reason that had nothing to do with the cold.

The map was wrong. Not in the way corrupted data was wrong — the stars were real, the coordinate grid was legible, the navigational notation was archaic but parseable. It was wrong because it did not match any modern chart Ben had ever seen, and he had been flying since he was old enough to reach a cockpit's controls.

"This is pre-Ruusan," he said.

"Stars don't move that fast." Desh stood, brushing dust from his knees. "The notation's the tell. They were using a different zero-point for the galactic coordinate system back then. Shift's about twelve degrees off modern standard." He studied the display. "That's military infrastructure."

Ben looked at him.

"Command and control." Desh pointed at the perimeter pattern resolving in the holographic field — installations arranged in a ring, equidistant, each one a node in a geometry that was not accidental. "See the spacing? That's not survey outposts. That's a containment perimeter. Someone built these facilities to watch whatever's in the middle."

The middle was a fortress. The map rendered it as a solid marker — larger than the perimeter nodes, positioned at the centre of the ring like a weight holding down a sheet.

Ben's chest tightened. The Force-pressure in the facility shifted — still ambient, still the recording-quality he had felt since they entered, but now directional. The walls remembered, and what they remembered was pointed at the centre of that ring.

"Gavran," Desh said.

The name landed without context. Ben turned.

Desh was reading a line of text scrolling along the display's edge — a monitoring log, timestamped in the same archaic notation as the star chart. "Authorization signature on the last entry before the system went dormant. Jedi Lord Gavran. That's who built this."

Ben filed the name. He did not reach for the Force to examine it. The Force was the problem — the pressure sitting beneath his attention, the wrongness he had been feeling since they dropped out of hyperspace, the thing he could not name and was not going to try to name while standing in a dead facility with a data core spilling secrets a thousand years old.

"Can you pull the route data?"

"Already copying." Desh tapped the coupler. "Navigation's intact. Whoever shut this place down wanted someone to find it eventually."

The display shifted. The perimeter ring resolved into a route — a corridor of navigable space connecting the installations, threading through star systems Ben did not recognise, terminating at the fortress marker. The coordinate string scrolled across the bottom of the holographic field, and Ben read it twice because the numbers did not make sense the first time.

"That's uncharted," he said.

"Deep uncharted. No hyperspace lane, no Republic survey, no trade route within fifteen parsecs." Desh's voice had gone flat in the way it did when he was processing something professionally alarming. "Someone put a fortress where nobody would look for it and then built a ring of watchtowers around it."

The weight of it settled into Ben's shoulders. Not one facility. A network. Not a survey anomaly. A containment system. The mission parameters Luke had given him — investigate the distress signal, assess the facility, report back — had just become obsolete.

"We need to copy everything," Ben said. "Coordinates, route data, the log entries. All of it."

"Already running." Desh was watching him now, not the display. "You're thinking we don't wait for backup."

"I'm thinking we don't know what we're waiting for backup to respond to."

"That's the kind of thinking that got you into GAG."

Ben did not answer. The coupler's indicator pulsed steady green, copying thousand-year-old data onto modern storage. The cold pressed against his face. The Force hummed in the walls — a recording, a memory, a pressure that had been building for a millennium and was only now finding someone to press against.

"Gavran's authorization was the last entry," Desh said. "He sealed this place personally. Whatever he was containing, he didn't delegate."

"Would you?"

Desh considered it. "No."

The display flickered. The route data finished copying, and the nav computer on the *Steady Returns* — slaved to the coupler through Ben's datapad — chirped confirmation. Coordinates locked. Destination plotted. Deep uncharted space, no lane, no survey, nothing.

Something had been deliberately hidden there.

Ben looked at the holographic star map one more time — the perimeter ring, the central fortress, the corridor connecting them — and did not construct a theory about who had built it or why. The data was fragmentary. The interpretation could wait. He had the coordinates. He had the route. He had a choice that his original mission parameters did not cover.

"We should report this," Desh said. "Standard protocol. Uncharted military infrastructure, possible containment breach, unknown hostile — "

"I know the protocol."

"I know you do. I'm saying it out loud so we both remember it exists."

Ben pocketed the datapad. The weight of it was negligible. The weight of what was on it was not.

"Copy complete," Desh said.

"Pull the coupler. We're done here."

The holographic field died. The console went dark. The power cells cycled down, and the subsonic thrum faded until the only sound in the data core was Ben's breathing and the distant hum of the facility's ancient life support.

Ben turned toward the corridor. The Force-pressure followed him — directional now, pointed at the centre of a ring of dead watchtowers, a thousand-year-old memory pressing against the present.

He carried the coordinates. He carried the choice.

The cold followed him out.
