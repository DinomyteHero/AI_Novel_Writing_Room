The second world had the same markings.

Different climate, different geology, same hand. Ben crouched in the ruin's shadow and ran his fingers over the carved stone — a coarser granite than the first site's limestone, tougher on the tools, but the cut was identical. Same depth. Same angle. Same hesitation at the curve where the carver had paused, reconsidered, and continued.

The wrongness pressed against his senses harder here. Not pain. A parallax — the Force registering the world a handspan left of where his eyes placed it. When he straightened, the distance to the rubble pile at the ruin's edge felt wrong by half a meter. He blinked and it corrected, then drifted again.

"Same hand," he said.

Desh stood ten meters downslope, scanning the valley with a portable sensor pack. "You're sure."

"I'm sure."

"Right." Desh clipped the sensor to his belt and turned back toward the landing site. "Then we're done here."

Ben didn't move. The vibration in the stone under his palm had no physical source — no machinery buried beneath the foundation, no seismic activity the instruments would register. Just a hum that wasn't a hum, pressing into his fingertips like a tuning fork held against bone.

"Ben."

He pulled his hand back. The sensation stopped the moment contact broke. "Coming."

---

The *Steady Returns* smelled of recycled air and warm circuitry, the same as every ship Ben had ever called home. Desh had the nav chart pulled up on the main console before the hatch finished cycling shut, the first world's survey data already loaded in a side panel, the second world's coordinates populating in real time as the ship's computer chewed through the landing zone telemetry.

Ben dropped into the co-pilot's chair and watched Desh work.

The man had been an analyst before he was an operative, and it showed in the way he handled data — no wasted motion, no second-guessing the overlay. The two anomalous sites appeared on the chart as amber markers, bright against the standard survey grid. Desh tapped a command and the computer drew a line between them.

Then he extended it.

The line cut through the chart's populated sectors — past the survey boundary, past the established hyperlane corridors, past the last recorded deep-space navigation beacon — and kept going, straight into a sector of the chart that was simply blank.

Not uncharted. *Removed*.

Ben had seen blank sectors before. The Outer Rim was full of them — regions no one had mapped because no one had needed to, or because the hyperspace routes didn't reach, or because the local stellar geography made navigation suicidal. But this wasn't that kind of blank. The surrounding sectors were fully mapped. The hyperlane infrastructure ran right up to the edge and stopped, as if someone had drawn a perimeter and decided that everything beyond it was someone else's problem.

Desh sat back. "That's not supposed to be there."

"No," Ben said. "It's not."

"Chart says empty. Chart also says the hyperlane from Ord Mantell to Bilbringi runs straight through this sector, which it doesn't, because I've flown that lane and I'd remember a two-hundred-light-year detour into nothing." Desh's voice stayed flat, the operational deadpan he defaulted to when things stopped making sense. "Someone scrubbed this."

Ben didn't answer. The line on the chart ran exactly along the bearing of the wrongness he'd felt at both sites — the direction the vibration in the stone seemed to point, if stone could point. He hadn't articulated that yet, even to himself. Articulating it would mean admitting that the survey route Luke had designed ran precisely along the same axis.

Coincidence or design.

He didn't want to know which.

"Run the bearing," he said.

Desh glanced at him — a quick look, reading something in Ben's voice that Ben hadn't meant to put there — and then his fingers were on the console, pulling the line's heading and translating it into navigational coordinates. Numbers filled a side panel. Declination, ascension, distance projection. The computer chewed for three seconds and then populated the target sector with a single pulsing marker: *bearing terminus, uncharted space*.

Ben leaned forward and punched the coordinates into the nav computer manually. The system hesitated — querying a database that had nothing to return — and then the main chart refreshed, centering on a cluster of star systems that did not appear on any map Ben had ever seen.

The display showed gravity wells. Stellar masses. Planetary bodies in orbital configurations that suggested terrestrial worlds, maybe habitable ones. The raw data was there — the nav computer could read mass shadows and orbital mechanics from half a galaxy away — but the labels were absent. No names. No registry numbers. No Republic survey codes, no Imperial astrogation marks, no old Republic era designations from before the Empire. Nothing.

Someone had gone to a great deal of trouble to make sure no one looked at this place.

Ben stared at the empty sector and felt the dread from the ruin site settle into something colder. More focused. The wrongness had a direction now. A destination.

"Ben." Desh's voice was quiet. "What are we looking at?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's the third time you've said that since we left the first world."

"It's been true all three times."

Desh didn't press. He let the silence hold for a moment, then reached over and killed the chart projection, leaving only the amber bearing line glowing on the console's surface. "Survey route runs right up to the edge. Another two jumps and we'd be in the blank sector whether we meant to go there or not."

Ben heard what he wasn't saying. *Your father designed this route.*

"Yeah," Ben said. "I noticed."

He could feel Desh reading his tension — the set of his shoulders, the way his hands had gone still on the console. They'd worked together long enough that Desh didn't need the Force to know when Ben was carrying something he wasn't ready to put down. The man had spent years in Galactic Alliance Guard learning to read people the hard way, through training and instinct and the kind of attention that came from knowing the person next to you might be compromised.

Ben wasn't compromised. He was just not ready.

"Your call," Desh said. "We can fold this into the survey report, flag it for follow-up, and move on to the next site on the schedule. Or we can see what's at the end of that line."

The friendship held. The scope had outgrown the job, and Desh knew it as well as Ben did, but he was still offering the out — the professional exit, the clean handoff to someone else's problem. He was offering it because he was a good friend, and because he knew Ben well enough to guess that Ben wouldn't take it.

Ben looked at the bearing line. At the empty sector beyond it. At the cluster of unlabeled gravity wells that shouldn't exist.

"Plot a course," he said. "Quietly."

Desh nodded once and turned back to the console. "Quietly is my favorite way."
