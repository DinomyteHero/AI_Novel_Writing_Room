The Operations level smelled of dust and dead circuits.

Ben swept his glowrod across the first bank of consoles, the beam catching a millennium of undisturbed silence — control chairs pushed back at precise angles, data terminals dark but intact, a command board whose buttons had been pressed by hands that turned to bone before the Republic was born. The air was dry. Preserved. The fortress had sealed this level when the power failed, and nothing had breathed here since.

Desh moved along the far wall, his own light picking out the seams of blast doors and the darker rectangles where equipment had been removed. "Communications array's fried. Power conduits are cold." He tapped a junction box with his knuckle. "Dead cold."

"They built for residual." Ben stopped at the central monitoring station — a horseshoe of screens arranged around a single chair bolted to the deck. The chair's occupant was long gone, but the position read command. Whoever sat here watched something. "The emergency systems are still drawing from somewhere."

He reached for the Force.

It came sluggishly, the way it had on the landing pad — the deadened quality he'd felt since they entered the valley, a wrongness he still couldn't name. But beneath the deadening, something else. A current. Faint, buried deep in the stone, running through conduits that hadn't been touched since the Ruusan Reformations were new law.

Ben pressed his palm flat against the console's surface and pushed.

The Force answered — not the clean flow of a training exercise, but a ragged pulse, like priming a pump that hadn't been used in a thousand years. The console hummed. A light flickered amber on the auxiliary board. Another followed, then three more, and the entire horseshoe lit in sequence, screens glowing pale blue in the dark.

"There," Ben said.

Desh turned. His face, caught in the console glow, showed nothing — which was how Desh looked when he was filing something under *problems to solve later*. "That's residual?"

"That's what residual looks like when the people who built it were Jedi."

The screens resolved. Not data — a map. A network of nodes spread across a star chart Ben didn't recognize at first, the sector boundaries wrong, the trade routes labeled in names that hadn't been used since the Old Republic fell. He oriented himself by the Core — there, the Deep Core systems, and spiraling outward the colonies, the Rim, the wild space beyond.

Most of the nodes were red.

Ben's eyes tracked the pattern before his mind caught up. Red meant dead. Dozens of points scattered from the Mid Rim to the Outer Rim, each one a location where something had been built and was now dark. A few glowed amber — degraded, still holding, still drawing power. The amber nodes clustered closer to the fortress's position, as if whatever they were monitoring had failed outward from a center.

One node flashed white.

BREACH.

The word pulsed on the display in characters that predated Basic standardization, but the meaning was unmistakable. Ben felt his breath catch. The Force around the console was wrong in a new way — not deadened now but *strained*, a hum at the edge of hearing that wasn't sound, a pressure behind his eyes that wasn't pain. The system was screaming, and the scream had been going on for centuries.

"Ben." Desh's voice was flat. "What am I looking at?"

"I don't know yet."

He did know, or was starting to. The fortress wasn't a tomb. It was a control center. The nodes weren't colonies or listening posts — they were part of the same system, the same purpose, and the one flashing BREACH meant something had already failed.

Desh was already moving. He pulled his datapad, framed the console display, captured three stills from different angles. Then he turned and photographed the auxiliary boards, the power routing diagrams stenciled on the wall in flaking aurebesh, the layout of the blast doors and the corridor they'd entered through. His movements were precise. Professional. His jaw was tight.

"Exits," he said, not looking up. "Two blast doors on this level. The main stairwell we passed, and a service ladder at the far end of the archive. I'm marking them now."

Ben heard what Desh wasn't saying. *I want to know how to leave.*

"Good."

"Ben." Desh lowered the datapad. In the blue console light his face looked older, the lines around his eyes deeper. "That word. The flashing one. You know what it means."

"I know what it means."

"Then you know we should be calling this in. Not walking deeper."

Ben looked at the display again. The amber nodes. The red. The one white pulse that wouldn't stop. He thought about calling Luke — what he'd say, what he'd ask for, what Luke would tell him to do. The answer was obvious. It was also wrong.

"If I call this in now," Ben said, "I'm calling it in blind. I don't know what's breached, what's holding, or what's sealed below. I don't know if this is a containment failure or a power failure or something else entirely. I need to see Level 3."

"You need to see it, or you want to?"

Ben didn't answer. The Force was pulling at him — not the strained hum of the console, but something below. Something alive. He'd felt it since they entered the fortress, a presence at the edge of his perception that he'd dismissed as the building's residual charge, the accumulated Force-echo of the Jedi who had worked and died here.

It wasn't an echo.

"Two exits," Ben said. "You've got them marked. If this goes wrong, you pull back to the ship and call Luke yourself."

"That's not the point."

"I know it's not."

Desh held his gaze for three seconds. Then he nodded once, the way he'd nodded before operations that had gone bad and operations that hadn't, the nod of a man who'd learned that arguing with a Skywalker was like arguing with a gravity well.

"After you," he said.

The stairwell to Level 3 was a spiral cut into the fortress's core, the steps worn smooth in the center where centuries of boots had passed before the centuries stopped. Emergency lighting ran along the wall in strips — not the pale blue of the consoles but a deeper blue, almost violet, casting the stone in colors that belonged underwater. The air changed as they descended. Warmer. Heavier. Charged with something that made Ben's skin prickle the way it did before a storm.

He stopped three steps down.

The Force was different here.

Not wrong. Not deadened. *Alive* — pulsing up through the stone like a heartbeat transmitted through bedrock, too deep to hear but impossible to ignore. Ben closed his eyes and let it wash over him, not reaching, just receiving.

Three rhythms.

One was disciplined — a steady cadence, controlled even in sleep, the Force-signature of someone who had spent a lifetime in practice and a millennium in stasis and had not forgotten either.

One was heavy — dense with something held back, a pressure behind a door that had not yet opened.

One was clipped — quick, sharp, the rhythm of a mind that moved faster than the bodies around it and had learned to wait.

People. Living people, sealed below.

Ben opened his eyes. The blue light flickered once, steadied, and pulled him downward.

His hand found the railing. His feet found the next step.
