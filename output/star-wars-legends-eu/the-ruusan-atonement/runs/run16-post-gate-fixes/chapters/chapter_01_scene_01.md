The training blade slashed across Ben’s shoulder. He staggered back, boot squeaking on the salle floor.

Third time this week.

He reset his guard anyway, left hand rising, weight settling into his back heel. Across the practice circle, Knight Arev held his training saber at half-mast, watching Ben with the careful scrutiny of someone weighing words.

Ben didn’t give him the opening. He advanced.

The exchange lasted four seconds. Arev was good—quick feet, economical cuts—and Ben usually read the rhythm before it formed. Tonight, the Force flattened between breaths. Not wrong. Not painful. Just gone, like a stair step vanishing in the dark when your foot swings through empty air. His read of Arev’s next move never came. His guard arrived late. The blade bit his shoulder again.

Ben lowered his saber. Arev lowered his.

“I’ve got caf on a timer,” Ben said. “Miss it, and I’ll be insufferable tomorrow.”

Terrible excuse. They both knew it. Arev let it stand, which meant either generosity or the wisdom not to push. Ben hadn’t decided which.

“Same time next week?”

“Sure.”

He watched the doors close behind Arev, then stood in the empty salle, listening to the training blade’s hum fade to silence in his grip. The room settled around him—the scuff marks on the floor, the rack of practice weapons along the far wall, the faint vibration threading through the Temple’s bones at all hours, layers of presence stacked like sediment.

Normally that pressure was comfortable. Background noise. The Force equivalent of a city’s ambient hum.

Tonight it had a sour note.

Ben deactivated his saber and set it on the bench.

He moved to the center of the floor and dropped into Shii-Cho’s opening stance, empty-handed. No blade. No Force precognition. Just muscle memory and geometry. He ran the sequence at half speed, then full, then faster than he’d dare in a real fight. His body knew every transition—the pivot at the end of the third sequence, the weight shift that set up the counter, the exact angle of the guard. Every motion landed where it should.

He stopped, breathing steady, not even winded.

His shoulder stung where the blade had cut. Everything else was fine.

Ben stood in the center of the salle and let the implications sink in.

He’d done this calculation before. After Jacen, he’d spent months dissecting his own perceptions, hunting for signs of corruption, searching for the moment he’d started sliding toward something unnamed. After Vestara, he’d done it again—different focus, not corruption but blind spots born of trust. He knew the texture of grief warping his precognition, how anger sharpened some reads and blurred others.

This wasn’t that.

The wrongness wasn’t in him. It was in the foundation. Like pressing a palm to a wall and feeling the vibration shift because something beneath had changed—not the surface, not his hand, but the thing supporting both. He’d felt something like it during the Abeloth crisis, that hollow distortion where the Force withdrew from itself, and again in the space Jacen had occupied in his final months. Those had been wounds. Corruption. Damage.

This felt like removal.

He ran Makashi next, precise dueling form, and let his body move while his mind worked. The Order’s stance on the anomaly was technically defensible. Post-Abeloth disturbances were real; they were still cataloguing residual effects three years later. His first report had earned a thorough, polite response concluding *consistent with documented recovery patterns*. The second got a shorter reply. The third received an acknowledgment timestamp and silence.

The Order was stretched thin. He understood.

He also understood none of his colleagues had looked up from their work long enough to notice the note was flat.

Ben finished the Makashi sequence and held the final position—right arm extended, left hand open at his side. In the empty salle, with only the Temple’s deep vibration and the distant thrum of his pulse, he felt it clearly now. Directional. New—or newly undeniable. Before, it had been pressure, omnidirectional, easy to dismiss. Now it had a vector. Southwest, past the Outer Rim, somewhere beyond reliable charts.

A Skywalker should recognize a Force threat.

He lowered his arm. His father would have named it by now, filed a full report, assembled a team, probably already had a plan on a datapad. His father also had forty years of experience and a connection to the Force Ben had spent most of his life not measuring himself against, because that comparison never ended well.

The wrongness pulsed. Not painfully. Just there, like a loose tooth—always present, impossible to ignore.

He crossed to the bench, picked up his saber, turned it in his hands. The casing still held the warmth of their earlier session. He traced his thumb along the activation plate without pressing it.

The Order thought it was background noise. Maybe they were right. Maybe he was miscalibrated, reading structural failure into what was just the Force settling after recent trauma. He’d been wrong before. Wrong about Jacen until it was almost too late. Wrong about Vestara until it was too late entirely. Both times, he’d trusted his read over the evidence in front of him.

But his reads hadn’t been wrong. His trust had been.

There was a difference.

He clipped the saber to his belt and reached for his jacket.

The salle doors hissed open.

Ben turned.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway, datapad in hand, wearing the stillness that came when a problem had moved past uncertainty. Not worried. Decided.

Ben knew that look.

He’d been waiting for an outlet for this restlessness for three weeks. He hadn’t expected it to walk through the door.
