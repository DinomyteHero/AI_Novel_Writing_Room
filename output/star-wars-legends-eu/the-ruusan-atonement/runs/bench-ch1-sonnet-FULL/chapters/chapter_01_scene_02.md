Luke's office smelled like old flimsiplast and the particular kind of quiet that accumulated in rooms where serious decisions got made. Ben had grown up in spaces like this — had spent half his childhood waiting outside doors exactly like this one, learning to read the weight of his father's silences through durasteel walls.

The datapad hit the desk with a soft click and slid toward him.

"Seventeen reports," Luke said. "Filed by four different survey teams across a six-month window. No coordination between them — different sectors, different crew manifests, different equipment specs."

Ben picked it up. The blue glow caught the underside of his fingers as he scrolled.

The readings were formatted in standard survey notation, dry columns of numbers that shouldn't have made his stomach tighten. But three entries in, he stopped scrolling. Zoomed on a cluster of anomaly markers from a team working the Outer Rim corridor near Ruusan. The notation was clinical: *Force-index deviation, magnitude 0.7, duration 14 standard hours, no proximate cause identified.*

The sour note under the Temple's harmonics sharpened.

He'd felt that exact deviation. Not the number — he didn't have instruments for numbers — but the quality of it, the way it sat wrong against everything else the Force was doing. He zoomed on two more. Different coordinates, same signature. The wrongness he'd been mapping in the salle for three months, laid out in survey notation by people who'd never compared notes.

"The route," he said.

"Runs directly along the bearing." Luke leaned back in his chair. His presence in the Force was the same as it had always been — vast, warm, the particular steadiness that Ben had oriented toward since before he could name what he was doing. "I want someone on the ground. Firsthand readings, not remote telemetry."

Ben set the datapad down and crossed to the window.

Coruscant spread below him, the city-planet's lights smearing upward into the lower atmosphere, traffic lanes threading between towers in patterns that looked chaotic from here and were anything but. He'd stood at windows like this one his whole life and never stopped finding the scale slightly absurd.

"Standard survey protocols?" he asked.

"Modified. The teams flagging these readings were running basic Force-index sweeps — they weren't equipped for anomaly characterization. You'd be going in with a full sensor suite and authorization to extend the route if the readings warrant it."

Ben watched a transport navigate the lane below. Solo mission, then. The parameters made that obvious without requiring it to be said.

*Dad,* something in him noted, and then the professional distance closed back over it like water over a dropped stone.

"Timeline?"

"Departure within the week. I'd rather have you out there before the next survey window closes."

He turned from the window. Luke was watching him with that particular stillness — not guarded, exactly, but careful. The expression of a man who had already mapped the conversation and was waiting to see which path Ben would take through it.

"You've been monitoring these independently," Ben said. It wasn't a question.

A beat. "Yes."

"How long?"

"Eight months." Luke's hands rested flat on the desk. "The early readings were ambiguous enough that I wasn't certain I was perceiving anything real. By the time the pattern clarified, I had four survey reports and a set of my own observations I couldn't fully account for."

Ben looked at him. "What do you make of it?"

"Fluctuation." Luke said it without hesitation, but something in his posture shifted — a slight weight in the word, like he'd chosen it carefully. "Post-Abeloth instability. The Force settling after significant trauma. It's happened before, after Malachor, after the Yuuzhan Vong crisis. The pattern is unusual but not unprecedented."

Ben's pattern recognition filed that interpretation next to the clinical reports, next to Master Sebatyne's careful patience, next to every response he'd gotten when he'd tried to describe what he was actually perceiving.

He'd been seventeen when he'd first felt something wrong around Jacen. He'd been twenty when he'd stood close enough to Abeloth to feel the specific quality of wrongness that came from the Force behaving as though its rules had been suspended. He'd spent time with Vestara, had felt the way Sith training warped Force perception into something architecturally different from anything the Order produced.

His father had more raw power than anyone alive. He had more experience than anyone Ben had ever met.

He also hadn't been the one in the room with those specific variables.

"I think it's targeted," Ben said. He kept his voice even. "Not diffuse. The deviation doesn't read like settling — it reads like something applying pressure to a specific point. Repeatedly."

Luke was quiet for a moment. "That's a significant interpretive difference."

"I know."

Neither of them said the obvious thing, which was that Ben's interpretive track record on exactly this category of wrongness was better than the Order's consensus had been, twice running. Luke knew it. Ben knew it. The gap between knowing it and saying it was the size of the office.

"The survey will clarify," Luke said.

Ben picked up the datapad. He looked at the seventeen flagged reports, the identical anomaly signatures, the six-month window of teams filing the same notation without knowing they were describing the same thing. The route ran along the bearing. First-hand readings. Full sensor suite.

One person.

The impulse surfaced cleanly: *I could take Sera. She knows the Outer Rim sectors better than I do, and she's been running her own anomaly observations since—*

He throttled it.

Not because the idea was wrong. Because accepting the mission meant accepting the terms of the mission, and the terms were what they were. His father was offering him the assignment and the autonomy in the same package, and unpacking one from the other wasn't something either of them was going to do tonight.

He set the datapad back on the desk.

"I'll need the full sensor suite calibrated for Force-index deviation above 0.5," he said. "And authorization to extend the route past the initial survey markers if the readings warrant it."

"Already noted in the brief."

"Then I'll be ready to depart by end of week."

Luke nodded. The warmth in his presence hadn't changed — it was the same vast steadiness it had always been, the thing Ben had navigated by since he was old enough to navigate. But the office held its careful quiet, and neither of them moved to close the distance across the desk.

"Ben." His father's voice was even. "Be thorough."

It meant half a dozen things. It meant *be careful* and *trust your read* and possibly *I'm sorry I can't go with you* and definitely *I love you* and none of those things were what it said.

"Always am," Ben said.

He took the datapad and left.
