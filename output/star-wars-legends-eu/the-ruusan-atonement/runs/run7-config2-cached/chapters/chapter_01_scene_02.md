Luke’s office smelled of stale flimsi and recycled air—nothing new. The datapad slid across the desk with a quiet click.

"Seventeen flagged reports," Luke said. "Three months of survey data along the Outer Rim. All inconclusive."

Ben picked it up.

Blue light from the screen painted his fingers as he scrolled. Survey coordinates. Telemetry logs. Force-sensitivity readings from four Jedi Knights in the Darrpa Sector, each annotated the same way:

*Anomalous reading, cross-reference pending.*
*Anomalous reading, cause undetermined.*
*Anomalous reading—*

He paused on the third entry and zoomed in.

The telemetry signature was familiar in the way a sound from a nightmare is familiar—not from waking life, but from somewhere the body remembers before the mind catches up. He’d felt it twice before. Years ago, standing in the same room as his cousin, the wrongness of Jacen not as darkness but as a frequency glitch, something off in the resonance. Later, near Abeloth’s dissolution, when the Force felt like walking on a surface that looked solid but gave slightly beneath his feet.

He zoomed on two more readings. Same signature. Different coordinates. Weeks apart.

Not residual. Not an echo of a single event. Ongoing.

"These three," he said, setting the datapad down. "The rest are noise. These are the same thing."

Luke’s chair groaned as he leaned back. "The Council’s initial assessment is residual disturbance from the Abeloth crisis. Sensitivity readings consistent with prolonged exposure to—"

"The Abeloth crisis felt like something trying to consume the Force." Ben tapped the three flagged entries. His finger lingered on each. "This doesn’t. It’s the opposite. It’s like—" He stopped, searching for the right word, finding none that fit. "Something structural is missing. Not contaminated. Missing."

Luke didn’t answer right away. His Force presence filled the room—warm, vast, the kind of deep luminosity that had once made Ben, as a child, feel like standing near a sun. Comforting. Blinding. He’d spent years learning to stand in that light without squinting.

"I feel it too," Luke said.

The words landed differently than Ben expected. He looked up.

"Not the way you’re describing it." Luke’s voice carried the measured precision of the Grand Master register, but beneath it, something shifted—a hesitation, as if he were choosing words against instinct. "More diffuse. A disturbance in the current—distant, like weather on the horizon you can see but can’t yet feel. I flagged it three weeks ago."

Three weeks. Ben had felt it for three months.

He didn’t correct the timeline. Instead: "The bearing."

Luke reached over to scroll to the last page. A stellar cartography overlay. The seventeen flagged coordinates plotted across the Outer Rim, and even at a glance, the clustering was obvious—not random, not diffuse, but linear. A path. A direction.

"The survey route follows that bearing," Luke said. "Eight coordinates. Standard reconnaissance protocol. You’d look for confirmation of the pattern—and, if possible, a source point."

"How long?"

"Six weeks. Maybe eight, depending on what you find at the early sites."

Ben took the datapad back, studying the cartography. The bearing ran toward a gap in the charts—a region where survey notation simply ended, replaced by a block of text he’d seen on old maps before:

*Records incomplete. Equipment malfunction reported by multiple survey teams.*

"You want me to go alone."

Not a question. Luke answered anyway. "I want to send someone I trust with a perception I can’t replicate." A pause, heavy. "Your read on Force anomalies is different from most Knights. Has been since—" He didn’t finish. Neither needed him to. "The survey requires someone who can distinguish between a true structural anomaly and residual dark side contamination. The Council’s preliminary assessment may be correct. If it is, the mission is routine. If it isn’t—"

"Then you need someone who’ll recognize the difference."

"Yes."

The words hung between them. Luke wasn’t asking. He was assigning. But the framing—*someone I trust with a perception I can’t replicate*—made it sound like a choice. Like Ben had something Luke didn’t. That distinction mattered more than it should have.

Ben studied the overlay a moment longer. The bearing. The gap in the charts. The three telemetry signatures that matched something his body had logged years ago in the worst rooms of his life.

He set the datapad down.

The window behind Luke’s chair framed the Coruscant skyline—the standard Temple view, all vertical light and perpetual motion. Ben moved to it. Not for the view. Just to move. To put the desk between himself and what came next.

"The Council’s assessment," he said, back half-turned. "Abeloth residue, prolonged exposure. That’s the working theory."

"It’s the most parsimonious explanation."

"It’s wrong." His voice stayed level, but his palm pressed flat against the glass. "Abeloth had weight. Intention. Even her echoes had a kind of hunger. This is—" He felt the faint vibration of the city’s pulse through the pane. "This is more like the floor plan changed. Same building. Same walls. But the load-bearing elements are somewhere they shouldn’t be—or they’re not there at all."

Silence behind him. Three seconds. He counted.

"Ben." Luke’s voice had shifted. Not Grand Master register. Older. Quieter. The voice from the Ossus kitchen when Ben had been twelve and woken from a nightmare he wouldn’t admit to. "I hear you."

Two words that weren’t about the mission.

Ben lifted his fingers from the glass. His shoulder ached where Teva’s training blade had caught it—a reminder that bodies keep score of things minds try to ignore. Four meters. Five. The width of a room that should have felt smaller. He could feel Luke’s presence at his back—warm, patient, waiting.

He turned back to the desk.

"You feel it too," Ben said. Not a question. "But you’re not sure."

Luke’s expression tightened slightly. "I’m sure of what I feel. Less sure of what it means."

"And you’re sending me to find out."

"I’m sending you because you’re the only one who can." A beat. "Your instinct on this is sharper than mine. Has been since Abeloth. You see patterns I miss."

"Or I’m seeing patterns that aren’t there."

"Are you?"

Ben said nothing. They both knew the answer. He’d been right about Jacen. Right about Abeloth’s dissolution. His body’s early-warning system had been calibrated in the worst way—and it was screaming now.

"Six weeks," he said instead. "I can work with that."

Back to the professional register. He watched Luke’s expression settle in response—a subtle accommodation, the way two people who know each other well adjust their posture when a conversation shifts shape.

"The Steady Returns is available," Luke said. "Desh Rolan is listed as your current crew."

"He’ll do."

"I’ll have the full mission package transferred to your nav-system by 0600."

Ben crossed back to the desk, picked up the datapad, held it a moment. Put it down again. It was Luke’s. He didn’t need to carry it out to know what was on it. The weight of it was already in his hands.

"I’ll depart day after tomorrow," he said. "Give me time for a supply check."

Luke nodded. No *take someone else.* No *ask for backup.* No *are you sure?*

The holocomm on the desk flickered—a low-priority message, queued automatically. Neither looked.

"Stay in contact," Luke said.

"Standard check-ins."

"Yes."

Ben set the datapad down one last time, straightened, and moved to the door. The wrongness still thrummed beneath the Temple’s harmonics—patient, structural, quietly doing its work. He felt it even here, in this room of warmth and stale flimsi and recycled air.

He didn’t mention it.

The door opened. He walked through.

Behind him, Luke didn’t call him back.
