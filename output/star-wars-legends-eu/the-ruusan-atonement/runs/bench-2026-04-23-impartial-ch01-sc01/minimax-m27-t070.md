The training blade caught him across the shoulder before his guard finished dropping.

Ben felt the hit as a bright, clean sting — not pain exactly, but the specific failure of a reflex that had never failed him before. Rendell stepped back, training saber raised, and for a half-second the only sound in the salle was the low hum of two blades and Ben's own breathing.

"Solid," Rendell said. He wasn't gloating. That was the worst part. He was just being honest — it had been a good strike, and they'd been at this long enough that Rendell knew the difference between luck and skill.

"Yeah." Ben straightened. The shoulder wasn't even tender. That was the part that sat wrong in his chest, like a splinter he couldn't reach. "Good hit."

The wrongness was still there. It had been pulling at him all week — a half-beat lag in the ambient field that made every exchange feel like trying to walk on ground that wasn't quite solid. He'd been attributing it to fatigue, to the tail end of a rough mission rotation, to anything that didn't require him to look at it directly. But his body knew what his mind kept sidestepping: the Force was arriving a fraction of a second late, and every time it happened, he was a half-step behind.

His guard dropped a half-second late. Third time this week.

"Rematch?" Rendell asked.

Ben deactivated his training saber. The hum died. "I'm done for tonight."

"You sure? You've got more in the tank."

"I'm sure."

The words came out light enough. Ben heard himself saying it and almost believed it — almost believed this was just a Knight calling it a session after a long week, nothing more. He kept his posture easy, his expression open, the whole performance of a man who had nothing to hide.

Rendell studied him for a moment. Ben held the gaze without flinching. He was good at that — at being seen without being read. His mother had taught him that, or maybe he'd just inherited it, the way he inherited everything useful and everything broken.

"Alright," Rendell said. He deactivated his own blade. "Get some rest. We've got drills with the padawans tomorrow."

"I'll be there."

Rendell nodded and headed for the locker alcove, boots loud on the salle floor. Ben watched him go, then turned and walked the long way around — past the meditation alcoves, past the cooling racks where the training sabers charged in their racks, toward the far end of the salle where the evening cleaning cycle had already started. A sanitation droid hummed in the corner, working its way methodically across the floor with soft bristles and a faint chemical smell.

Ben waited until the locker room door hissed shut behind Rendell before he let himself stop pretending.

He walked to the center of the salle floor and set his training saber on the rail. Then he raised his hands and began a form — the third Shii-Cho sequence, the one he'd known since he was nine years old, the one his body ran without needing his mind to participate. He moved through the first pass at full speed, muscle memory carrying him the way it always had, and felt nothing wrong.

He stopped. Started again. Slower this time.

The first pass was clean. The second, he began to feel for it — that half-beat lag he'd been ignoring all week, the place where the ambient field stuttered like a musician hitting a note slightly flat. He moved through the third position, the fourth, the transition into the fifth, and there it was: the Force arriving a half-second behind the rhythm his body had already committed to.

He stopped mid-position. His hands stayed raised. His breath stayed even.

The wrongness was not in him.

He'd been treating it like fatigue, like the slow degradation of his own focus — a problem he could solve by sleeping more, by eating better, by pushing harder until his body caught up to the standard he'd always met. But his body wasn't the problem. His body remembered the forms the way it remembered how to breathe. The problem was out there, in the field itself, and the realization hit him like a change in air pressure — a tightening in his chest, a sharpening of everything around him, a shift from *I am failing* to *something is wrong*.

He didn't have a name for it yet. He wasn't sure he wanted one. Naming it would make it real, and making it real would mean admitting it aloud, and admitting it aloud would mean —

What? Walking into the healers' wing and telling them the Force felt wrong? Walking into the Council chambers and saying his reflexes had stopped working because the ambient field was stuttering? He'd sound like someone who'd spent too long near Abeloth and come out the other side with cracks in his perception he couldn't see.

Or he'd sound like someone telling the truth nobody else could feel.

Ben lowered his hands. The salle was quiet except for the sanitation droid and the distant murmur of the late shift moving through the corridors outside — boots on durasteel, the soft chime of a turbolift announcing a floor change, the low hum of the Temple's environmental systems keeping forty thousand living beings breathing in a city that had never evolved to hold them.

He picked up his training saber and clipped it to his belt. The weight was familiar. The motion was automatic. Everything his body did was still reliable; it was only the Force that was slipping, and only in the ways he couldn't prove to anyone who hadn't felt it.

Footsteps. Not the soft bristle-scrape of the droid, not the distant corridor traffic — these were deliberate, unhurried, and coming from the main entrance.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway, datapad in one hand, the expression on his face the one Ben knew as well as his own reflection. Not anger. Not concern. The specific stillness of a man who had already decided something and was about to tell you what it was.

Ben felt the reluctant hope rise in his chest before he could push it down — the sharp, uncomfortable hope that his private problem was about to become someone else's to carry. He kept his face neutral. He was good at that.

"Ben," Luke said. Not a greeting. An introduction to something that was already happening.

He was holding the datapad like it mattered. He was standing in the doorway like he was waiting for Ben to come to him. And Ben knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a son who had grown up watching his father make decisions that changed everything, that whatever was on that datapad was about to change the shape of his week, his month, maybe his year.

The wrongness sat in his chest like a stone. The hope sat beside it, heavier than it had any right to.

Luke said nothing yet. He was waiting, the way he always waited, for Ben to close the distance himself.

Ben walked toward the doorway.
