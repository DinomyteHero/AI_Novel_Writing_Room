Rendell’s training blade slapped across Ben’s shoulder hard enough to sting through the padded tunic.

Ben was already moving for the block that should have been there. Half a beat late. Again.

He cut away on instinct, boots squeaking against the salle floor, and reset his stance before the embarrassment could fully land. The blue practice blade in his hand hummed steady. The room did not. The Force around him came in just behind the motion his body had already committed to, like a note struck a fraction late by someone who should have known the piece.

Rendell lowered his weapon at once. “You all right?”

“Sure,” Ben said. “If we’re grading on artistic interpretation.”

Rendell gave him the polite half-smile of a fellow Knight deciding whether this counted as a joke or a warning sign. He was younger than Ben by a few years, competent, serious, and at the moment probably trying not to look surprised that he’d just scored a clean shoulder hit on Luke Skywalker’s son.

Fair enough. Ben was trying not to look surprised too.

He rolled the struck shoulder once. The sting was clean, physical, almost welcome. Better that than the other thing. Sweat had slicked his palms inside the practice gloves, and the hilt sat a little less securely than it should have. Across the training salle, a cleaning droid whispered along the far wall, collecting dust no one could see on polished stone. Somewhere out in the corridor, a turbolift chimed and voices passed by, low and tired with the late shift.

Rendell raised the blade again, tentative. “Another?”

Ben wanted it. Immediate rematch, harder pace, one more pass to prove his reflexes still belonged to him. Instead he thumbed the activator off. The training saber died with a clipped snap-hiss.

“No,” he said. Then, lighter, “If I lose twice in one night, I’ll have to start offering excuses with actual creativity.”

Rendell let his own blade retract. “Long week?”

“That’s the current theory.”

It wasn’t even a lie, which was the irritating part. It had been a long week. Too many Council briefings, too little sleep, too many aftershocks still moving through the Temple after everything the Order had already weathered. Plenty of reasons for an off evening. None that explained the first two missed beats. Or this one.

Rendell hooked his saber to his belt. “You want me to tell the healers you pulled something?”

Ben snorted. “Please don’t. If the healers hear I’ve been defeated by basic footwork, they’ll make me do balance drills with six-year-olds.”

That earned a real grin. Good. Humor was useful. It gave people something easy to hold.

Rendell hesitated anyway. “You sure you’re all right?”

There it was. Not alarm, exactly. Just enough concern to make Ben want the conversation over.

“I’m tired,” Ben said. “You took advantage of my tragic dependence on decent timing. It’s a credit to your training and a serious character flaw in me.”

“You always say that when you don’t want to explain something.”

Ben tipped his head. “See? You are learning.”

Rendell huffed a laugh, but he let it go. “Get some sleep, then.”

“Bold medical thinking. I’ll pass it on to the healers.”

Rendell shook his head and headed for the door. Ben waited through the sound of retreating boots, the slide of the salle door, and the returning hush. Not silence; the Temple was never silent. Air systems whispered through vents. The cleaning droid turned somewhere behind him. Foot traffic moved and thinned in the corridor beyond, lives crossing at odd hours because this was still a home as much as a fortress, still a place where someone was always coming off duty, heading to meditation, carrying tea, arguing softly over a datapad.

Ben stayed where he was until the door had been shut long enough to make following him out look normal instead of evasive.

Then he looked down at his shoulder and muttered, “Artistic interpretation. Nice save.”

The room did not laugh.

He set the deactivated hilt on the edge of the mat, flexed his hand once, and paced the length of the salle. Ten steps one way. Turn. Ten steps back. His body still wanted the rematch. His pride wanted witnesses erased. Neither was going to help.

Because it had happened three times now.

Not dramatic failures. Nothing clean enough to point at. A missed catch in a corridor when a youngling tossed him a practice remote battery. A meditation session that had felt off-key from the first breath. Now this. Tiny slips, except they weren’t tiny if they were his.

He stopped in the center of the mat.

Maybe fatigue. Maybe not enough sleep. Maybe the sort of leftover damage nobody liked naming after Abeloth, because saying it aloud gave it shape. That was a cheerful line of thought. Very restful. His mother would have called it a waste of time and told him to gather evidence before panicking. His father would have asked two quiet questions and waited him into honesty.

Ben missed being old enough for either of those responses to solve things for him.

He drew a slow breath and settled into the opening stance of a standard form, empty-handed for a heartbeat before habit made him pick up the hilt again. He didn’t ignite it. He just held the weight and began.

Step. Turn. Cut. Recover.

The motions lived in muscle memory so deeply he normally didn’t need to think about them at all. His body knew the rhythm. The Force met it. That was how it had always worked: action and answer, foot and floor, breath and center.

Tonight the answer came late.

Not by much. A fraction. Nothing a non-Jedi would ever notice, maybe nothing most Jedi would notice if they weren’t listening for it. But Ben had lived inside this rhythm since childhood. He knew where the next beat should land.

He moved through the form more slowly.

Step. Turn. Cut.

There. The same dragging hitch. Not in his shoulder. Not in his grip. Not in the line of the motion. The lag brushed past him a half-second after it should have, a note played slightly flat and just late enough to spoil the whole measure.

Ben stopped with the hilt angled across his body.

The back of his neck had gone cold under the sweat. He stood very still and listened without quite meaning to, not with his ears but with the part of him that had spent a lifetime sorting presence from absence, balance from strain, living current from dead stone. The salle’s familiar shape was there. The sleeping quiet of polished floor, old walls, distant minds moving through the Temple, all of it known.

And beneath it, that delay.

His chest tightened.

Not me, some part of him said, quick and fierce and not nearly as calm as he would have preferred.

He didn’t answer it. He started the form again.

Faster this time. Testing. If it was him, it would stay with him no matter the pace. If it was the room—

The next transition caught. His weight landed a touch ahead of the support that should have been under it. Not enough to fall. More than enough to know.

He broke off hard, lowering the hilt before he could throw it across the salle like an overtired initiate.

The urge to laugh hit him first. That was almost insulting. Weeks of carrying on like a competent adult Jedi Knight, and apparently his reward was discovering that either the Force had started missing cues or he had. Excellent. Very stable. Exactly the sort of thing one mentioned casually over breakfast.

He scrubbed his free hand over his face.

No. Not fatigue. He could stop pretending that one, at least.

The relief that came with it was thin and ugly and much too close to fear to look at directly. If the problem wasn’t in his head, that should have been good news. Instead it only shifted the ground under him. He had something outside himself to blame, and somehow that was worse.

Because if this was real, if this half-beat drag was real, then it had been there all week while everyone around him went on as normal. Training, patrol schedules, Council traffic, the ordinary pulse of Temple life. No one else had said a word. No one else had looked up mid-conversation and gone still because the room had missed a step.

Maybe they weren’t feeling it.

Or maybe they were, and he was the only one being precious enough to call it wrong.

He hated both options.

The cleaning droid clicked against the base of the wall, reversed, and went on with the patient determination of a machine that had never once questioned its purpose. Out in the corridor a pair of voices drifted past, one Human, one not, followed by another turbolift chime and the quick patter of someone jogging to catch it. The Temple lived around him, busy and ordinary and entirely uninterested in the possibility that the floor under his instincts had shifted.

Ben reset his grip on the hilt and lifted it again, not into guard this time but into readiness. If the next thing through that door was duty, he could do duty. If it was a healer, he could probably fake patience for at least a minute. If it was another Knight asking whether he wanted that rematch after all, he might actually commit a small and regrettable act of violence.

The door slid open.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
