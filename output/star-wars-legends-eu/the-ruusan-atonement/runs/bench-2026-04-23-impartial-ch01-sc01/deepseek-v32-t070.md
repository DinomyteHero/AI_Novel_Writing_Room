Rendell’s training blade caught Ben across the shoulder.

The impact wasn’t pain. It was failure—clean, sharp, a mistake his reflexes should not have allowed. Ben’s own blade was still moving, a half-second behind where it should have been. He felt the lag in the Force like a note played slightly flat, a vibration in the air that arrived after his body had already committed to the move.

He deactivated his saber. The hum died.

“Again?” Rendell asked, his own blade still lit, the blue glow casting a sharp line across the salle floor.

“No.” Ben kept his tone light. “I’m done.”

He didn’t look at Rendell. He looked at the floor, at his boots, at the sweat on his palms. The wrongness pulled at his attention, a tug in the ambient field that had been there all week. He’d called it fatigue, distraction, the lingering residue of a long mission cycle. Now it had cost him a sparring exchange he shouldn’t have lost. The third time this week.

Rendell powered down his blade. “You’re off your timing.”

“I know.”

Ben didn’t explain. He didn’t say the Force was arriving late. He didn’t say he felt a half-beat lag in the rhythm his body had known by reflex for years. He offered a reason that wasn’t quite a lie. “Just tired.”

Rendell nodded, accepting the out. He collected his gear, gave Ben a nod that was more acknowledgment than concern, and left.

The salle was quiet. The training sabers’ hum had faded into the background noise of the Temple—the distant foot traffic of the late shift, the occasional turbolift chime from the corridor beyond, the low thrum of Coruscant itself bleeding through the walls. Ben paced the empty space, his boots echoing on the polished floor.

He ran through a form without igniting the blade.

His body moved through the sequence with precision. Muscle memory carried him—the pivot, the extension, the follow-through. But his Force-sense registered the lag. The ambient field arrived a half-second behind the rhythm, like a musician hearing a note played slightly flat. It wasn’t fatigue. It wasn’t distraction. It was something in the Force itself, a structural wrongness he couldn’t name.

He stopped.

The realization arrived not as a thought, but as a physical sensation—a tightening in his chest, a sharpening of focus. He’d been failing all week, and he’d blamed himself. Now the failure shifted. It wasn’t in him. It was in the field. Something was wrong.

He didn’t articulate it. He felt it.

The frustration deepened into something closer to dread, but he didn’t name that either. He was a Knight. Knights handled problems privately. They didn’t announce them in the salle. They didn’t tell another Knight the Force was arriving late. They trained through it, or they meditated, or they filed a report with the Council if it got bad enough.

Ben hadn’t filed a report.

He ran the form again, slower. The lag was there, consistent, a half-beat stolen from every move. He could compensate—force his body to wait, adjust his timing, push harder—but compensation was a choice. Choosing to wait meant acknowledging the lag. Acknowledging the lag meant admitting the wrongness was real.

He deactivated the training saber in his hand, though it wasn’t lit. He set it on the rack.

The salle doors opened.

Luke appeared in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
