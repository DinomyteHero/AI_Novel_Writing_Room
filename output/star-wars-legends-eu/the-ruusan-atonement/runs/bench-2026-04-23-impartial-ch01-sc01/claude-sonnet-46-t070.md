Rendell's training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back before he'd consciously decided to.

Third time this week.

He knew the count because he'd been trying not to count it. The hit itself was nothing — a clean tap, no weight behind it, the kind of touch that scored a point and left no mark. What it left instead was the specific sting of a mistake his body had no excuse for making. His guard had been there. He'd felt it, the angle right, the timing — and then the Force had arrived a half-beat behind, like a chord played with one finger late, and his arm had moved on reflex instead of sense and reflex wasn't quite enough.

Rendell lowered his blade. "Again?"

Ben deactivated his training saber. The hum died.

"I'm done." He kept his voice easy. "Long week."

Rendell accepted that without pressing — he was a good sparring partner partly because he didn't editorialize — and gave a short nod before heading for the equipment rack. Ben watched him go and did not watch the door. Behind him, the salle's overhead panels held steady at their late-hour dimness, and through the corridor wall came the distant chime of a turbolift changing floors. The Temple at this hour was a different kind of busy: maintenance droids, night-shift Knights rotating through the ready rooms, the particular rhythm of a building that never fully slept.

Rendell gathered his gear, said something about the early session tomorrow, and left.

Ben stood in the middle of the salle floor and breathed.

The recycled air tasted of sweat and the faint metallic edge of the training blades' power cells. He turned his unlit saber over once in his palm, then clipped it to his belt. His shoulder didn't hurt. That was almost the problem — if it had hurt, he'd have had somewhere to put the frustration.

*Long week* was true as far as it went. It just didn't go far enough.

He started moving.

Not a drill — no targets, no count, no point to score. He paced the length of the salle and back, and then without deciding to, he settled into the opening stance of Shii-Cho and let his body run through the form. Blade unlit, just the grip and the motion, the weight of the hilt familiar in a way that predated conscious memory. He'd been doing this since he was nine years old. His muscles knew it the way his lungs knew how to breathe.

The form moved through him, and he moved through the form, and he paid attention.

Not to his footwork. Not to his angles. He paid attention to the field.

It was there, the way it was always there — the ambient presence of the Force, the thing that lived underneath his perception like a floor he'd never had to think about. But this week it had been arriving late. Not absent. Not wrong in any way he could point to or name. Just — behind. Half a beat behind. He'd step into a turn and the Force would catch up to him a half-second after his body expected it, and in that gap his timing fractured.

He ran the form through to the third sequence and felt it happen again.

His arm swept the deflection, and the Force arrived a fraction late, and his arm compensated on reflex, and the compensation was almost invisible, and it was also completely wrong.

He stopped.

Stood still in the middle of the salle with his unlit blade at his side and the turbolift chiming again somewhere down the corridor.

Not fatigue. He'd trained exhausted before — after the GAG, after Abeloth, after the kind of weeks that left marks. He knew what his body felt like when it was running on nothing. This wasn't that. His reflexes were fine. His footwork was fine. The form was fine. The problem wasn't in him.

He didn't finish the thought. He didn't need to.

The half-beat lag was there, and it wasn't fatigue, and he'd been pretending for five days that those two facts were reconcilable and they weren't.

He exhaled through his nose and stood in the quiet salle and let the not-knowing sit there without trying to resolve it. Outside, a cleaning droid hummed past in the corridor — the low, patient sound of it moving along the baseboards, doing its rounds. Someone's boots clicked past in the other direction. The Temple breathed around him, indifferent and steady.

He ran the form again.

Slower this time. Deliberate. He moved through the first sequence with the careful attention of someone checking for a loose stone in a wall they'd walked past a thousand times. His body was where it should be. His blade was where it should be. The Force —

Late. Again. Half a beat, no more, but there.

He stopped at the top of the fourth sequence, arm extended, and held the position. The hilt sat in his palm. His shoulder didn't hurt.

Something in the ambient field was off.

Not him. The field.

He didn't know what that meant. He didn't try to know what it meant. He just stood there with the knowledge sitting in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed without noticing, heavy and specific and not going anywhere.

A week of blaming the week, and the week wasn't the answer.

He lowered his arm.

The turbolift chimed again, closer this time — one of the Temple's upper shafts, by the sound of it. Ben turned back toward the equipment rack and was halfway through unclipping his training saber when he heard the door.

Luke was standing in the doorway.

He had a datapad in one hand, held loosely at his side. He wasn't wearing his formal robes — just the plain working layers he wore when he'd been in his office late. But his posture was the posture Ben had grown up reading: the specific quality of his father's stillness when stillness meant something was already decided. Not tense. Just ready.

Ben looked at the datapad. Looked at his father's face.

The hope came before he could stop it — unwelcome and sharp, the kind that arrived with a cost attached. Whatever Luke had on that datapad, it wasn't a social call. And if it wasn't a social call, then the thing Ben had been handling alone for five days was about to stop being his to handle alone.

He wasn't sure yet if that was a relief or a problem.

His father's eyes moved from the unlit training saber in Ben's hand to the empty salle behind him, and something in his expression settled into a question he hadn't asked yet.
