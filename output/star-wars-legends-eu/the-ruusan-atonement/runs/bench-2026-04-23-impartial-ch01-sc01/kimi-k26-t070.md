Rendell's training blade caught Ben across the left shoulder with a sharp crack. Not pain—failure. The clean, specific sting of a mistake his reflexes should never have allowed.

The exchange had started clean. Ben had pressed the attack, his blade humming in tight arcs, and Rendell had given ground, blocking high, blocking low. The rhythm had held for the first ten passes. Then the lag had crept in, subtle at first, a hesitation where certainty should have lived. Ben's wrist had turned a fraction late. His weight had shifted a heartbeat after the Force prompted him. By the fourteenth pass, the gap had widened enough for Rendell's blade to find flesh.

Ben disengaged, putting two meters of polished floor between them. His training saber hummed, low and steady, in a grip gone slick with sweat. Across the salle, Rendell straightened, chest rising and falling with controlled breath. He did not gloat. He did not need to. The hit spoke for itself. It was the third time this week Ben had lost a beat he could not spare, and each time the wrongness had arrived the same way: a half-beat lag in the ambient field, like a note played slightly flat.

"Again?" Rendell asked.

Ben deactivated his blade. The hum died, leaving the salle quiet except for the whisper of the ventilation system and a distant turbolift chime. "Not tonight. I'm running on yesterday's rations and four hours of sleep."

"You're a Skywalker. Four hours is standard issue."

"Then I'm setting a bad example." Ben kept his tone light, easy, the same deflection he'd used since the week started. He wiped his palm against his tunic and tried not to think about how true the fatigue excuse almost was. "Go find someone who'll actually challenge you."

Rendell hesitated, blade still active, the pale light catching the edge of his jaw. Then he shrugged. "Your funeral. Next time I won't pull the swing."

"Appreciated."

Rendell deactivated his weapon and left. The salle door hissed shut behind him, sealing Ben in with the recycled air and the faint echo of boots in the corridor beyond. Ben stood alone, listening to the Temple's late shift move through its routines. Somewhere, a cleaning droid whirred past the intersection. The durasteel walls held the faint vibration of a city that never stopped humming.

He watched the door for a moment after it sealed, as if the metal might offer an opinion. It did not. The salle was a simple space, unadorned, functional. A place for Knights to test themselves without the Temple's usual grandeur intruding. Tonight it felt too large, the overhead lights too bright, the silence too complete.

He settled into the opening stance of Shii-Cho in the center of the salle. Twenty meters of polished floor stretched around him, catching the overhead lights. No blade. Just the form, slow and deliberate, as a musician practices fingerings on a silent instrument. Feet finding the proper angle. Weight balanced over the balls of his feet. Knees bent. Arms moving through blocks and strikes against an invisible opponent. A high parry. A low sweep. The turn. Muscle memory carried him. The body knew the shape.

But the Force was supposed to arrive ahead of the body. That was the point. The field should have pressed against his awareness, a constant presence guiding timing, anticipating weight. He should have felt the next position before his muscles engaged, a pre-echo of movement that turned combat into something closer to dance. He shifted into the second sequence, pivoting on his heel, and felt the absence: the ambient field arriving a half-second behind his movement, the rhythm broken, the note flat.

He tried to adjust. Led with his body instead of his sense, anticipating the lag. It made the third sequence clumsy, mechanical. His arm completed the block before the Force confirmed it was necessary. His foot touched down before the field told him the floor was there. He was fighting ahead of his own shadow, and the shadow kept shrinking.

Ben stopped. The salle was quiet now, the only sound his own breathing and the distant groan of a turbolift somewhere in the Temple's eastern spire. Coruscant never truly slept, and neither, apparently, did whatever was wrong with the Force.

He started again. Slower. First sequence. Second. Third. The lag held steady, a half-beat stubbornly out of time, throwing off the internal rhythm he had trusted since he was a child. It was not fatigue. He had told himself that lie for six days, blamed the week, blamed the sleeplessness, blamed the Abeloth aftermath that everyone else seemed to have metabolized except him. But fatigue dulled the senses. It did not rearrange their timing. It did not make the Force arrive late, dragging behind the beat.

The wrongness was not in him. It was in the field itself.

The realization did not come as a thought. It landed in his chest, a physical tightening, a sharpening of focus that made his hands curl into fists at his sides. He was not failing. Something was failing around him. The frustration was clean and sharp, a Knight's frustration with his own body—but no, not his body. The field.

He stood in the empty salle, breathing hard, and let the isolation settle over him. Rendell did not feel it. The other Knights did not feel it. He had sat in the meditation chambers yesterday, surrounded by Masters, and no one had flinched. The dissonance was his alone, a private wrong note nobody else could hear. If Ben spoke of it now, he would sound like a man making excuses, or worse, a man broken by trauma he should have already processed. He did not want to be either. He had been handling this privately because privacy was the only thing that kept the wrongness from becoming someone else's diagnosis to offer.

He raised his arms, started the form a third time, and stopped almost immediately. The lag was there, patient and unmistakable, a note held slightly flat against the scale he had known all his life. He could not name what was broken. He only knew his body had not betrayed him; the rhythm had.

The salle door hissed open.

Luke stood in the doorway, datapad in hand, his expression settling into the particular quiet focus that meant a mission.
