Rendell's training blade caught Ben across the shoulder guard a half-second after Ben's own blade should have intercepted it. The impact wasn't hard—training sabers hummed rather than bit—but Ben's body registered it as a sharp, clean failure. Not pain. The specific sting of a mistake his reflexes had no business allowing.

"Third time this week," Rendell said, stepping back. The older Knight deactivated his saber, the blade collapsing into the hilt with a familiar snap-hiss. "You want to talk about it?"

Ben deactivated his own blade. The salle's overhead lighting glinted off the durasteel floor, clean and empty between them. "I'm fine. Long week."

"That's what you said yesterday."

"Yesterday was also a long week."

Rendell's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but close enough. Ben had sparred with him enough to know the expression. Rendell was competent, thorough, and possessed of exactly the kind of patient observation that made him useful on the practice floor and exhausting everywhere else. Ben could see him weighing whether to press.

The wrongness pulled at Ben's attention again, a half-beat lag in the ambient field. Like a note played slightly flat. He'd been attributing it to fatigue, to the residue of the Abeloth crisis, to the particular exhaustion of being twenty-five and trying to prove he deserved the rank he'd earned at nineteen. But his body knew the rhythm of the Force the way his hands knew the weight of a lightsaber, and the rhythm had been arriving late all week.

"Another rematch?" Rendell asked.

Ben shook his head. "I'm done. You'd just win again."

"Modesty doesn't suit you, Skywalker."

"It isn't modesty if it's true."

Rendell studied him for a moment longer—evaluating the claim, the posture, the sweat that hadn't quite dried on Ben's palms. Then he nodded, a short, economical gesture, and clipped his saber to his belt. "Tomorrow, then. Early session."

"Early," Ben agreed.

Rendell left without looking back. The salle door slid shut behind him, sealing Ben in the quiet hum of the Temple's ventilation systems. Late-shift foot traffic murmured in the corridor outside—boots on stone, the distant chime of a turbolift, the mechanical whir of a cleaning droid passing on its scheduled route. Coruscant continued its perpetual motion beyond the Temple walls, indifferent to whether one Knight's timing had gone soft.

Ben stood alone in the center of the salle. His shoulder ached where Rendell's blade had landed. Not from the impact—from the failure.

He didn't reach for the Force. Not yet. The Force was the problem.

Instead, Ben began to move through the first form, Shii-Cho, without igniting his blade. The movements were muscle memory carved into him over fifteen years of training—stance, pivot, guard position, strike. His body knew the sequence. His body had always known the sequence.

But the Force was supposed to arrive with the motion. The ambient field should have pressed against his awareness like a hand against his back, guiding, confirming, completing the circuit between intention and action. He moved through the opening stance and waited for the familiar weight of connection.

It came late. A half-second behind his body's rhythm. The lag hit him like a missed step on a staircase, a moment where the ground failed to meet his foot at the expected moment. Ben continued the form, slower now, feeling for the gap between his physical motion and the Force's response.

He extended his empty hand into a blocking position. The Force should have firmed around his guard, a tactile certainty. Instead, it arrived after his muscles had already committed, a echo following the shout.

Ben stopped. His hand hung in the air, fingers slightly curled around the absent hilt.

The realization didn't arrive as words. It landed as sensation—a tightening in his chest, a shift in his breathing, the specific dread of a pilot who discovers his instruments have been lying to him for days. He wasn't failing. Something was wrong. The wrongness wasn't in his reflexes or his concentration. It was in the field itself, the background presence he had trusted since childhood. The Force was arriving late, and he had been compensating without knowing it, adjusting his timing to match a rhythm that had gone subtly, dangerously out of tune.

He lowered his hand. His palm was damp. The recycled air of the Temple tasted metallic on his tongue.

For a week, he had told himself fatigue. For a week, he had adjusted and compensated and pretended the misalignment was his own distraction. But standing alone in the salle, moving through forms without the blade's distraction, he could no longer pretend. The half-beat lag was real. The Force itself was running slow, or his perception of it was, and the uncertainty opened a hollow space beneath his ribs that he refused to name as fear.

If the wrongness was external, he was not failing.

If it was internal—trauma, bleed, some fracture he couldn't locate—then he was unreliable, and every mission he accepted put others at risk.

Ben turned toward the salle's entrance. The door remained closed, but he could feel the approach through the Force before the chime sounded. Not the lag this time, but the specific, steady presence that had anchored his life since birth.

The door slid open. Luke stood in the corridor, a datapad in one hand and the expression Ben had learned to read before he'd learned to read Basic—shoulders set, eyes focused past the immediate moment into the shape of work to come.

"Ben," Luke said.

Ben felt the shift in his own posture, the automatic adjustment from private trouble to professional readiness. The hope arrived reluctantly, mixed with the cost of what he would have to admit. But for the first time in a week, he wasn't alone with the wrongness.

"Master," Ben said. "Mission?"

Luke's nod was small, precise, and final. He stepped into the salle, the datapad already extended, and Ben braced himself for the handoff.
