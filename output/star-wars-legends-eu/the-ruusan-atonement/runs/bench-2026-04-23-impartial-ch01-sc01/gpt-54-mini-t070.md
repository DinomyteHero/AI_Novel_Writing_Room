Rendell’s training blade kissed Ben’s shoulder hard enough to jolt his teeth.

Ben stumbled back one step before he could stop himself. The contact was clean, controlled, and entirely his fault. His shoulder throbbed under the sleeve of his tunic, right where the practice blade had scored across the fabric and skin beneath.

“Again,” Rendell said, already resetting his guard.

Ben rolled his shoulder once and hated the tiny hitch it gave him. The salle floor was smooth duracrete under his boots, warm from hours of use. Two other pairs were farther down the room, their sabers hissing in steady rhythm. Beyond the open arch, the corridor traffic of the Temple still drifted past in soft bursts — boots, murmured conversation, a turbolift chime, the hum of a cleaning droid nosing along the wall.

Ben brought his saber up again. “You are enjoying this too much.”

Rendell’s mouth twitched. “You were late.”

“Was I?”

Rendell lunged.

Ben met the strike, turned it, and moved into the counter with the kind of ease he knew made instructors think he was showing off. Usually it worked. Usually his body stayed half a step ahead of anyone trying to crowd him. Tonight there was a drag in the rhythm, a tiny catch in the space before his blade and Rendell’s crossed.

Not much. Just enough.

Rendell pressed. Ben parried high, pivoted, and almost had the opening. Almost. The wrong beat slipped again, thin as a miscounted step, and Rendell’s blade clipped his side before Ben could recover.

The hit landed with a sharp snap of failure.

Ben heard himself swear under his breath. His right hand tightened on the hilt. He had trained too long, too hard, to miss something that small. Not twice. Not three times this week.

The air in the salle seemed to settle wrong around him. Not silent. Just off. Like the field itself had leaned a fraction late and expected him to catch up.

Rendell fell back into guard, waiting.

Ben forced his breathing even and lifted his chin. “That was generous of you.”

“It was not.”

“Then I will treasure the memory.”

Rendell snorted once, the sound brief enough to count as mercy. He lowered his saber a little. “You want another pass?”

Ben’s shoulder stung every time he shifted. He could have asked for another round. He should have. A Knight did not quit after one bad exchange, and he had not lost because Rendell had outclassed him. He had lost because something had tugged at the edge of his attention at exactly the wrong moment, for exactly long enough to cost him a beat he should never have given away.

That explanation would sound worse than fatigue.

“Not tonight,” Ben said.

Rendell frowned. “You are quitting on a scratch?”

Ben powered his saber down and clipped it to his belt. “I am being wise enough to preserve my dignity. It is a rare skill.”

Rendell gave him a look that suggested he did not buy that for a second, but he did not push. Good. Ben did not need the lecture, the concern, or the inevitable question he could not answer without sounding like he had taken one too many blows to the head. He had no interest in explaining a problem he could barely pin down to someone who would hear it as either excuse or confession.

“I have seen your dignity,” Rendell said. “It is not in immediate danger.”

“Harsh.”

“True.”

Ben rubbed a hand over the back of his neck and kept his face deliberately light. “Go on. Before I make you stay and listen to me complain about your tactical generosity.”

Rendell huffed another small laugh, then pointed his saber toward the bench. “Hydrate. You are starting to look like someone who forgot that sleep exists.”

“Noted.”

Rendell holstered his practice blade and headed for the door. At the threshold he glanced back once, the way people did when they were deciding whether to ask something they knew they should not. Ben kept his expression blank. Rendell hesitated, then shrugged and went out into the corridor traffic.

Ben waited until the hiss of the door closed behind him before he let his shoulders drop.

The salle felt larger with only him in it.

He stood there for a moment, the practice hilt still warm in his hand, and listened. The distant corridor noise seeped through the arch again: footsteps, a faint exchange of voices, the clean electronic note of a turbolift arriving somewhere above and to the left. The Temple never really slept. It only changed shifts.

Ben looked down at the blade in his hand. He had not missed the hit because he was tired. He was tired, yes. Everyone in the Order had been tired for months. That was not new. This was different. This was the third time this week he had gone one beat late for no reason he could name, and each time the lag had felt the same — not in him, around him.

He exhaled through his nose, irritated enough to be precise about it. That was useful. Irritation he could work with. Vagueness was an insult.

He moved to the center mat and put the practice hilt aside. Then he drew his hands up into guard and began the first form without igniting the blade.

Bare fists. Empty air. The sequence lived in his muscles as much as his memory. Step. Turn. Angle. Recover. He let his body go through it once at full speed, then again slower, watching his own feet, feeling for the hitch.

Nothing.

He tried again, and this time he let himself settle into the calm he used before a real drill. Not meditation. Just enough stillness to hear what the room was saying back.

Step.

Turn.

The lag came on the next transition.

It was tiny. Infuriatingly tiny. The room did not move wrong, not exactly. His body did not move wrong. The problem was the fraction between them. The moment when the Force usually met motion and made it feel inevitable came a hair late, like a chord resolved after the beat had already passed.

Ben stopped in the middle of the form.

For a second he just stood there with his hands half raised, listening to the silence under the sounds of the Temple. He could feel his pulse in his shoulder where the training blade had struck. He could feel the soles of his boots against the floor. He could feel the room, the air, the old familiar presence that ran through the Temple as naturally as durasteel through a hull.

And under it, that hitch.

Not in his reflexes. Not in his focus.

There.

The thought came without words at first, only the hard little drop in his stomach that told him he had been asking the wrong question all week. He was not missing because he had gone soft. He was not missing because he was distracted. The rhythm itself was off by half a beat, and he had been trying to force his body through it as if enough discipline could bully reality into behaving.

That was almost funny. In a bleak, deeply annoying sort of way.

Ben let his arms fall and gave the empty mat a flat look. “Wonderful,” he muttered. “So it is not me being incompetent. It is the universe.”

The room did not argue.

He paced once across the floor, then turned and came back, barefoot in spirit if not in fact. His shoulder still ached. Sweat had cooled on his palms. He flexed his fingers once, twice, and made himself go through the form again, slower now, feeling the delay at the point where his movement and the Force should have locked together.

Again. There. Half a beat late.

He stopped dead.

No amount of wishing would make that feeling his imagination. He knew his own timing. He knew the difference between a tired body and one that had started to lie to him. This was not lying. This was a flinch in the room itself, a tiny refusal in the flow that he could feel in his bones.

Ben dragged a hand over his face and looked down the length of the salle. The benches were empty. The other drills had moved on. Somewhere beyond the arch, the Temple went about its business, indifferent to his private problem.

Good. Private problem. He could work with private. Private meant he could keep pretending this was nothing, right up until it stopped being convenient.

He gave a short, humorless huff and rolled his shoulder again. “All right,” he told the empty room. “I admit it. I am not just being dramatic.”

That had less dignity than he preferred, but at least it was honest.

He set his feet and started the form a third time. This pass he stripped it down to the bones, checking every turn, every shift of balance. The lag appeared in the same place, exact as a bad habit. His body expected the field to be there under the next motion, and it arrived a fraction late, like someone stepping up to catch a weight and missing the first grip.

Ben closed his hand hard enough to creak his glove. His chest tightened once, sharply, then settled into something harder and colder than irritation.

Not me.

The thought landed without ceremony. No speech. No revelation. Just the clean edge of it.

Not fatigue. Not distraction. Not some private failure he could outwork by being stubborn enough.

Something in the room — in the field beneath the room, in the thing he felt when he reached for balance — was wrong.

That should have relieved him. Instead it left him standing very still, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to somebody else.

If it was out there, then he was not losing his edge. That was the good news.

The bad news was that he had no idea what was doing this, and that meant whatever had started costing him beats was still out there, still moving, still waiting for someone else to notice.

The door chime sounded once.

Ben did not turn at first. He was too aware of the half-breath silence that followed it, the one he had learned to associate with people stopping where they were not supposed to stop. He knew that pause. He knew the weight of it.

Then he looked up.

Luke appeared in the doorway with a datapad and an expression Ben knew meant a mission.
