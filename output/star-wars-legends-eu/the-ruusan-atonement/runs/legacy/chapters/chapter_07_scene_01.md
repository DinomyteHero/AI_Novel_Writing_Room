The planet appeared to have healed.

From the boarding ramp of the *Steady Returns*, Ben scanned rolling green hills that caught the morning light at low angles, casting long shadows across gentle dips and rises. Grassland stretched as far as the sensor sweep reached—no settlements, no roads, no structures visible above ground. He might have been viewing a nature preserve on Alderaan, if Alderaan still existed.

He reached out with the Force to read the perimeter.

His hand struck empty air.

Not metaphorically. He had reached for the ramp's side rail—muscle memory placing it eighteen inches from his hip—and his fingers closed on nothing. He glanced down. The rail was there, but farther than he’d perceived. He gripped it. His Force-sense projected a slightly different geometry than his eyes confirmed, the two images refusing to resolve into a single picture.

The wrongness settled in his chest like a stone. This was the parallax error he had sensed as they approached the node, but grounded now in physical space. Real. Not theoretical.

He released the rail and stepped onto the grass.

"Standard perimeter check," he said. "Stay in visual range until we know the ground."

Desh followed, scanning with a hand unit. Sera emerged into the light and stopped. Her posture stiffened, one hand dropping to her lightsaber without drawing—a reflex, not a decision. Torin stepped past her, his shoulders easing and his chin lifting. The compressed alertness he carried on the ship evaporated. He surveyed the hills, looking comfortable for the first time since Ben had met him, at home in the thinning fabric of the Force.

Kael walked to the edge of the ramp and stood still. He didn't focus on any specific point. He stared through the horizon, his expression going blank as the sensory displacement hit him.

Ben filed the three reactions and returned to the problem of the rail. His instinct was to solve it—to understand the parallax, compensate, and regain control of his perceptions. He tried the Force again, carefully—not a full reach, just a light touch to sense the nearest terrain. The Force was there. He could feel Desh's warm absence at his left, Sera's tight coil of attention, and Torin's odd ease. The planet possessed mass and life. But his depth perception remained skewed. When he tried to gauge the distance to the tree line, the number his instincts provided and the number his eyes gave him differed enough to matter if he needed to move fast.

He stopped trying and relied on his eyes.

"The dips," Desh said, eyes fixed on his scanner.

"I see them."

The gentle valleys weren't erosion channels or dry creek beds. They ran in parallel lines, curving around high ground in patterns erosion didn't create. A thousand years of rain and root systems had softened the edges, but the geometry remained deliberate. Military earthworks. Trenches, covered positions, kill corridors—all of it overgrown and beautiful now. The grass did not care what had soaked into the soil.

"New Sith Wars battlefield," Ben said. He pulled up his datapad and checked the archive notation for the system. *Minor skirmish, Army of Light forces, no permanent installations recorded.* "The Jedi archives have this as a minor engagement. No mention of a Corridor node or a facility."

Torin continued to watch the hills. "We held this ridge for eleven days."

The flatness of the statement caught Ben's attention. Not nostalgia. Not trauma. Just fact, delivered like a weather report.

Sera turned toward Torin. Ben watched her decide not to ask and turn away.

The bunker entrance sat half a kilometer in, set into a hillside so deliberately it resembled a natural rock outcropping until they were close enough to see the durasteel framing corroded into the stone. The door was massive—two meters thick, sealed with a locking mechanism designed for permanence. There was no exterior panel. No handle. Ben studied the slab for a moment, then pressed his palm flat against the metal.

The mechanism recognized the signature of a Force-sensitive and disengaged.

Inside, the air changed—dry, cold, and preserved. The scrubbers were still running, cycling air that had been trapped for a millennium. The scent was the smell of nothing: no dust, no organic decay, no moisture. Pure, dead atmosphere. Ben's nose registered the absence more than any presence.

Emergency lighting flickered on. Low, red-tinted, enough to navigate by.

The construction bore no resemblance to a Jedi temple. The walls were thick poured ferrocrete, reinforced with functional ribbing. Everything was built at a scale meant to last centuries rather than impress. No carvings. No inscriptions. The Jedi Lords hadn't built this to be found. They'd built it to hold.

"Jedi archives don't have this structure," Ben said, reading the datapad as they moved through the entry corridor. "Not under any designation."

Sera made a sound between a scoff and a sigh. "Gavran didn't report everything to the Council. The Reformations were supposed to be a fresh start. This was the part they wanted to forget."

The stasis chamber sat at the facility's center, behind two more security doors that opened to the same palm contact. Ben stepped through and stopped.

The chamber was sized for one. The pod was open—not forced, but deliberately unsealed, the lid raised to its full extent and locked. Inside, the surfaces were coated in a fine grey accumulation: dust that had settled so slowly it had formed a continuous film without a single disturbance. The pod had been empty for a very long time.

Ben read the installation records on the wall terminal. One anchor. Confirmed deceased. The death notation was dated 412 ABR—Old Republic dating, which his datapad translated to roughly seven hundred years ago. The anchor had died in place, the seal continuing to draw from a dead point until the load redistributed.

Kael stood in the doorway, staring at the empty pod with a hollow expression.

Ben crouched and examined the floor around the base of the unit.

The dust there was different.

It wasn't disturbed or scuffed, but marked. The silt that had settled over a thousand years remained intact in most of the chamber. Around the terminal and the base of the pod, it had been compressed into distinct shapes: the narrow-edged impressions of boot soles, pressed clean into the ancient accumulation. The edges were sharp. Crisp. No secondary settling had softened the lines.

He took a precise image with his datapad. The tread pattern wasn't military standard—nothing in the current GAG or NJO catalogs—but it was consistent. One person, moving with purpose. They'd gone from the pod to the terminal, from the terminal to the secondary access panel on the far wall, and then back out.

Ben stood. Three weeks, maybe four. The dust compression was still at its deepest point; nothing had crept back into the impressions yet. Whoever had walked this floor hadn't been here long ago in any geological sense. They'd been here recently enough that the timeline matched Darth Veraine's escape.

"She accessed the records," Sera said. He hadn't heard her cross the room. She was looking at the terminal, her hand resting on her lightsaber. "She knows which anchors survived."

"She knows which ones she has to worry about," Ben said.

The terminal screen still displayed the last query logged. The timestamp used Old Republic dating, but the query itself used a format Ben recognized from his father's descriptions of ancient Sith techniques: a systems diagnostic pull. Someone had asked the facility what was still functional.

Someone had come here not to grieve, but to take inventory.

Ben looked at the boot prints again. Precise. Efficient. No lingering.

He glanced at Sera. She stared at the terminal screen, her throat working once before she regained control. Torin had moved to the secondary access panel, his ease from the surface replaced by calculation. Kael hadn't moved from the doorway.

"She's not chasing a thousand-year-old ghost through a failing system," Ben said quietly.

Sera's eyes met his.

"She's already three moves ahead."
