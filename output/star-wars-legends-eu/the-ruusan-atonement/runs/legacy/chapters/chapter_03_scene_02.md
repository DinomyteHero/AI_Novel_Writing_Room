The holographic projector stuttered, spitting blue-white static before resolving into a star map ravaged by bit-rot. Ben kept his distance from the terminal cluster, watching Desh work.

Desh had located a floor panel within thirty seconds of the red emergency lights activating. Now he crouched beside the exposed console internals, a scomp link in one hand and a data-spike clenched between his teeth. He traced conduit runs with his free hand. He wasn't reading the room the way Ben did—no sense of the Force threading through old stone, no attention to the bas-relief iconography. He read load distribution, cable routing, and signal flow. His eyes moved in the deliberate pattern of a surgeon mapping a nervous system.

"Relay station," Desh said, pulling the data-spike to inspect the tip. "Not a temple, not a garrison. Look at the cable runs—they're all inbound. Everything here is designed to receive signals, aggregate them, and rebroadcast on a hardened frequency." He tapped the conduit with a knuckle. "Whoever built this was listening."

Ben crouched beside him. The exposed internals smelled of mineral oil and centuries of vacuum-sealed preservation—the dry scent of a machine kept but never used. His knees protested the deep squat. "Can you get the map functional?"

"Working on it."

The projection sputtered. The star pattern was wrong in ways that took Ben a moment to identify. The relative positions were skewed—not by corrupted data, but by stellar drift. A thousand years of it. Stars moved. The shift was negligible over a human lifetime, but across ten centuries, the accumulated motion was measurable. Whoever had programmed this system hadn't accounted for the galaxy aging after they sealed the vault.

Ben closed his eyes and reached out. 

The Force here felt thin. It wasn't absent, like the hollow void he’d touched at the first survey site, but stretched, like a cloak worn through at the elbows. He could still use it. He let his internal sense of the galaxy's current shape overlay the ancient projection. The stars slid into alignment in his mind, compensating for the drift. When he opened his eyes, he recognized the smears of light.

"The map's accurate," he said. "The coordinates are just old."

Desh glanced at him. "You're doing the Force thing."

"I'm doing the Force thing."

"Right." Desh returned to the conduit.

Cold crept into Ben's fingers. The facility's environmental systems ran at a minimum—enough to keep the electronics from seizing, but not enough for comfort. He exhaled, watching the mist dissipate in the red-lit air. The warning message cycled again from the central column, patient and indifferent.

*SEAL FAILING.*

He needed to call his father. The thought arrived with the same insistence it had maintained for the past hour. He pushed it down. Not yet. Not until he knew what he was reporting. Calling Luke meant calling the Council. Calling the Council meant committees, consultation, and the institutional process that treated urgency as a symptom of poor judgment. By the time the Order authorized a response team, the window this facility's distress signal was screaming about might be closed.

The reasoning was sound. He held onto it.

"Got something," Desh said.

The display lurched, flickered twice, and expanded. The map tripled in scale, pulling back to encompass a wider sector. Across the Outer Rim, a series of points ignited in sequence—red nodes pulsing at irregular intervals like a heart skipping beats.

Ben stood.

He counted them. Seven. Then three more, further along the same bearing. Then a final cluster that pulsed slower than the rest.

"Command and control architecture," Desh said, rising to stand beside him. He pointed at the pulsing points without touching the light. "These outer nodes report to a central source. The slow pulse at the end of the chain—that's not a relay. That's a hub." He paused. "This is a perimeter alarm. Everything we've been finding—the carved sites, this facility—they're sensors. Sensors for something they built a fence around."

The points formed a line. Not a random scatter or a patrol route, but a direct bearing Ben recognized. He'd been following this path for days.

"The Ruusan Corridor," he said.

"Following it to where?"

Ben studied the display. The line extended past the last charted navigation point, past the survey data his father had flagged, and past the edge of the Galactic Alliance's records. At its terminus, the map simply stopped. No stars catalogued. No survey data. A void in the records matched the dead zone Desh had identified on the *Steady Returns*.

Something had been hidden there. Deliberately. Systematically.

The Ruusan Reformations. He'd studied them as history—the transition from the Jedi Lords' military apparatus to the peacetime Order his grandfather's generation had inherited. A philosophical shift, the texts claimed. Reorganization. He'd accepted that framing without examination, the way a student accepts any lesson taught before they are old enough to ask better questions.

Looking at a thousand-year-old alarm broadcasting into an empty room, that framing cracked.

"Ben." Desh's voice was level, but his tone had shifted. "This is the part where I ask the professional question."

Ben waited. Desh had managed field agents for too long not to recognize the moment a mission became personal.

"We report this and wait for backup."

It wasn't a question, but it carried the deliberate pause Desh used when giving Ben room to arrive at the obvious answer. It was a habit from their GAG days, when Desh managed agents who needed to feel they'd made their own decisions. Now, Desh wasn't managing. He was warning.

Ben's teeth ground together. "How long does a hyperspace route to that terminus take to calculate?"

"Ben—"

"How long?"

Desh looked at the display, then back at Ben. "From here? Uncharted space, no lane data—the nav-computer needs time. Two hours, maybe."

"Start the calculation."

"That's not what I asked you to do."

"I know what you asked." Ben moved to the primary console. His hands were steady. He maintained the practiced calm of the competent son, the one who could be trusted with the hard call. "The relay stations have been failing for centuries. That's what the anchor degradation pattern means—this system was designed with redundancy, and that redundancy has been eroding one node at a time. The cascade that tripped this alert didn't start today."

He pointed at the slow-pulsing cluster at the end of the line. "Whatever's at the terminus, it's been losing structural integrity for a long time. Reporting this to the Council and waiting for authorization adds days we don't have."

Desh didn't move. He stood with his arms crossed, reading Ben the way he'd read the facility's cable runs—mapping the architecture underneath. "And if you're wrong about the timeline?"

"Then we report it and wait for backup." Ben kept his voice flat. "But I'm not wrong."

"You don't know that."

"No," Ben said. "I don't."

The admission hung between them. Desh's expression remained fixed, but his shoulders eased—not in acceptance, but in recognition. He'd asked the question. Ben had answered honestly. That was the contract.

Desh moved to the secondary console and began interfacing his equipment with the ancient system. "I'm logging this conversation," he said. "For the record."

"Noted."

"And I want it on the record that I asked."

"Also noted."

The nav-computer on the *Steady Returns* was still slaved to Ben's comlink. He pulled up the interface and watched the calculation begin—slow, methodical work through an absence of data. The display showed an estimated completion time and a projected entry vector into the dark.

The terminus coordinates resolved on screen. The point sat in the heart of the scrubbed sector. No stars named. No survey flags. No hyperspace buoys. Nothing.

The green light of the calculation display reflected off the console surface, steady and patient, pointing toward the void.

The facility cycled its warning again. The red light strobed once. Ben stood in the cold and the silence of the ancient alarm, and did not call his father.
