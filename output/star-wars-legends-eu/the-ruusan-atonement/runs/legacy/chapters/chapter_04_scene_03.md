The stairs ended in a ring.

Ben stepped off the last tread. The chamber opened outward in a perfect circle, thirty meters across, the walls curving into a ceiling that dissolved into shadow. Twelve alcoves cut into the perimeter at even intervals, each sealed behind a viewport of clouded transparisteel. Emergency lighting bled from the stasis fields—a blue so pale it was nearly colorless, pulsing with the irregular rhythm of a failing lung.

He held his lightsaber low. The blade's gold glow failed to reach the far wall.

The air pressed against his face, thick and stale. It carried the weight of oxygen processed and reprocessed for a millennium until it had lost everything but the bare minimum required for life. Ben didn't name the smell. No word existed for what a thousand years of sealed breath became.

Desh descended behind him, stopping at his shoulder. Neither spoke.

The Force here felt dense, like accumulated grief. It wasn't loud or violent, just heavy. It pushed against Ben's perception as if he were standing at the bottom of a deep sea. He could feel the lattice within it—the structure beneath the structure—and it felt like cloth pulled too tight over a warping frame. One hard tug and the fabric would tear.

He began walking the perimeter. Desh fell in on his left, three steps back, covering the angles Ben left open.

The first dark chamber sat four meters from the stairs. Ben knew it was empty before he reached it. The Force simply ended at that point in the ring—not a hollow, but a dead stop. He peered through the viewport anyway. Frost on the inside of the transparisteel had crystallized into abstract patterns over the centuries, but he could discern the shape behind the glass. Robes. A face collapsed inward. Hands folded at the waist.

He moved to the next. Then the next.

Nine of them. Nine stops where the Force vanished. He didn't try to identify the remains—Jedi or Sith, Human or alien. The specifics didn't matter; they would only anchor the horror. He registered the shape of each absence and kept walking.

"They were people," Desh said at the sixth chamber.

"Yes."

"And they put them in here."

Ben kept walking.

"Who does this?" Desh’s voice remained level, but a particular flatness crept in—the tone of a man who had seen too much and was choosing not to react. "Institutions do this. People with enough authority and enough fear."

Ben offered no answer. Desh wasn't asking for an explanation. He was naming the threat so he could track it.

The three living chambers were grouped together in the far arc of the ring. Ben felt them before he arrived—warmth where everything else was cold, three points of presence against nine of absence. His pace slowed.

He stopped in front of the first active viewport. The stasis field pulsed blue-white, strong and even. Through the frost, he saw a woman in Jedi robes from an era he’d only seen in archive images. Her hands rested at her sides. Her face was calm with the specific, controlled discipline of the highly trained. In the Force, she felt like the edge of a blade: precise, cold, and committed to a direction chosen long before she was sealed away.

The discipline was too tight. Someone had told her this was necessary. She had decided to survive it.

He moved to the second chamber. The Force presence here felt slower, heavier—a rhythm like a drum buried in the earth. A man sat in armor Ben recognized as Battle Master configuration, hands on his knees, head slightly bowed. The weight in his signature was specific. Not damage. Not corruption. It was the burden of someone who had done something irreversible and had been carrying the consequence for a thousand years.

The third chamber flickered at the edges, the stasis tech straining. The occupant was younger, perhaps in his mid-twenties when the field took him. His body language was wrong. While the others had arranged themselves for the long wait, this man had his arms crossed, his posture turned away from the viewport as if he’d been in the middle of a refusal. His Force presence came in irregular bursts, like a signal from a malfunctioning transmitter. Not Jedi. Not quite Sith. Something that had learned from both and belonged to neither.

Ben pulled back and surveyed the ring.

Three alive. Nine dead. One—

His count was wrong.

He turned in a slow circle, checking each alcove against his mental map. Twelve positions. He’d passed eight dark chambers and three active ones. That was eleven. The geometry refused to resolve. There was a space between the third active chamber and the stairwell that should have held another alcove.

He walked toward the gap. The wall looked smooth. No viewport, no frame, no stasis housing. Just bare stone and metal—until he got close enough to see the mounting brackets. They were bent outward. The housing had been removed from the inside. The transparisteel was gone. What remained of the door mechanism lay on the floor in pieces, the metal buckled in patterns that had nothing to do with mechanical failure.

Desh crouched over the wreckage, his helmet light cutting across the damage. He didn't touch the debris. He moved the beam slowly, systematically. "Force," he said.

"Yes."

"From inside."

"Yes."

Ben’s breath grew shallow. Implications assembled themselves in his mind. Desh stood and moved to a monitoring console recessed into the wall beside the breach. It was dark, save for a single indicator light blinking amber at the base. He flipped the emergency power toggle. The console hummed into a low-power mode, displaying a single screen of status data.

Ben read it over Desh’s shoulder.

*ANCHOR SEVEN. STATUS: ABSENT. BREACH DATE—*

The notation used an Old Republic calendar. Ben did the math twice. The first result seemed impossible.

"Six weeks," he said. His voice sounded strange—too controlled.

Desh didn't move. His shoulders were rigid. "Whatever was in there has had six weeks."

Ben looked at the buckled metal. Someone had spent time on this. It wasn't panicked or frenzied; it was methodical. Patient. The work of someone who understood the mechanism holding them and had planned its defeat.

The lattice in the Force near the empty chamber felt different. It wasn't just stretched; it felt like a rope that had been cut and knotted back together. Functional, but the splice would always be a weak spot.

"It was a Sith," Ben said. The residue had a texture he recognized from his training—old, certain, and cold. "High-ranking. One of Kaan’s people, maybe. An alchemist." He turned away, his motion sharp. "They’ve been loose in the galaxy for six weeks and we didn't know they existed until twenty minutes ago."

Desh looked at the three active chambers. "There’s a fourth level."

It wasn't a question.

***

The shaft at the center of the ring held a ladder. Ben went down first.

Level four was a single circular room, five meters across. The ceiling was low. Every surface was choked with conduit—thick bundles of cable and tubing that converged on a central chair. The conduit connected to the seat from twelve directions, one for each position in the ring above.

The man in the chair had been dead for a long time.

He still wore his robes and his rank insignia: Lord’s markings, Army of Light configuration. His hands rested on the control surfaces. The connection wasn't metaphorical; the Force had fused the interface points over a millennium of operation. The man’s hands had become part of the system, the way old trees eventually grow into the walls they lean against.

His face was peaceful.

Ben stopped. He had expected the face of someone who had made a terrible choice and knew it. Instead, Lord Gavran looked like a man who had sat down to work he understood and had not stopped until he couldn't continue. The monitoring logs on the console trailed off mid-entry. No final words. Just a sentence that ended where his life did.

Ben’s throat tightened. He traced the conduit running from Gavran’s hands into the walls, into the floor, and up through the shaft. Twelve connections. Three still humming. Three people still breathing because this man had stayed at his post for a thousand years and died here instead of somewhere else.

Desh descended the ladder and stood beside him. He looked at Gavran, then at the conduit, then up at the three chambers visible through the shaft.

"We can't leave them like this," Ben said.

Desh didn't argue. He looked at the lines running from the chair to the walls. His jaw was set.

"No," he said. "We can't."
