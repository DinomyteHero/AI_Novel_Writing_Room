The air inside tasted of nothing.

Not sterile, not stale—nothing. A millennium of sealed circulation had exhausted every volatile compound, every particle that gave the atmosphere character, until what remained was pure molecular function stripped of identity. Ben’s lungs processed it without complaint. His hindbrain registered it as a void.

Level 1 stretched ahead: a long, low-ceilinged chamber carved from the mountain’s marrow. Alloy paneling lined the walls, contracted by centuries of cold into a web of hairline fractures. The residual power Desh had spotted from the exterior translated here into a vibration in the floor. Ben felt it travel through his boots and into his teeth before he heard a sound. Then came the subsonic hum, patient and constant, like the respiration of a massive, sleeping beast.

Desh moved left along the wall, checking the first alcove. Ben tracked right.

The workstations were wrong in a way that took him a moment to name. No haptic surfaces. No gesture-controls or bioscan panels. Every console was physical—switches, manual toggles, indicator lights recessed behind thick glass. The GAG had used equipment like this in field stations a century ago, and it had been considered obsolete even then. This was older by orders of magnitude, built this way by design. Not for lack of technology; the relay station on WR-7741 had proven their computational sophistication. They chose this because physical controls failed predictably. Because predictable failure could be managed.

The engineers who built this had not expected to return.

Ben brushed dust from the nearest console and studied the layout. The toggle labels were etched in Old Basic, the pre-Reformation script his father had insisted he master. He translated the characters slowly: *Perimeter Contact. Status Uplink. Node Relay. Lattice Structural Integrity.*

That last one stopped him. He read it twice, then dismissed it—archaic terminology for Force-sensitivity readings, likely. The Jedi Lords had used structural metaphors for everything. He moved on.

What he couldn't move past were the personal items.

Someone had left a meal tray on the workstation beside the primary uplink terminal. The contents had long since desiccated into geometric husks, but the tray remained. A folded garment draped over a chair back—heavy fabric, military cut, the rank insignia worn smooth by a thumb. A child’s drawing, vacuum-sealed in a preservation sleeve, propped against a terminal housing. Stick figures. A sun. The crude optimism of a child who had never seen this room.

Ben picked up the drawing. The sleeve felt warm, the image inside impossibly bright against the dust-grey of the tomb. He stared at it until the silence grew heavy, then set it back exactly where he had found it.

"Clean evacuation?" Desh asked from across the chamber. His voice was flat, muffled by the dead air.

Ben didn't answer immediately. He was still looking at the drawing.

"No," he said finally.

"That's what I thought." Desh paused at an alcove. "No remains, though. Where are the bodies?"

Ben turned away from the workstation. "I don't know."

Desh didn't push. He moved deeper into the chamber, cataloguing exits with the methodical attention of a man planning a fast exit. Ben watched him work—the way Desh’s hand trailed along the wall, the way his eyes tracked the ceiling for structural weaknesses. He wasn't searching for knowledge. He was searching for escape.

The stairwell to Level 2 sat at the far end, marked by a recessed light strip that had failed decades ago. Ben extended his perception down the shaft. No movement. No heat signatures. Only the same dead air, colder.

He went first.

***

The archive was organized the way a drowning person organizes things—the most vital materials nearest the door, complexity increasing with distance, the far end a chaos of unsorted media that someone had intended to return to.

They never had.

Physical media lined the shelves: vacuum-sealed scroll cases in polymer that had held their atmosphere for a thousand years, primitive holocrons in protective housings, data tablets with depleted charge cells. Ben lifted one scroll case and examined the seal. Military cipher, Army of Light authentication marks. He set it down. The preservation seals were irreplaceable, and the secrets weren't going anywhere.

He found a fragment that had already been breached. Someone had cracked the seal before the evacuation and left the scroll half-unrolled on a reading table. Ben read the exposed portion.

The vocabulary was Old Basic military technical, dense and context-dependent, but fragments resolved: *...the Lattice Inversion proceeding beyond projected parameters... anchoring protocols insufficient to contain... Lord Gavran has authorized escalation to full...*

The scroll ended where it had been rolled, the rest shielded from his curiosity.

*Lattice Inversion.* The same word from upstairs. This time it wasn't a metaphor. It was an event. A procedure that had exceeded its parameters. Ben’s breath hitched. He set the scroll down carefully, as if a sudden movement might disturb something that had slept for centuries. His fingers lingered on the edge of the table before he pulled them back.

Across the room, Desh had found a chest mounted to the wall—alloy housing, no visible lock. "This one's different," he said.

Ben crossed to it and pressed his palm against the metal. The seal inside pushed back, not with hostility, but with the patience of something waiting for authorization that would never come. A Jedi seal, built with the heavy signature of the Lords era. He could feel the architecture of it: redundant, aggressive, designed by people who trusted nothing.

He could break it. The knowledge was immediate. It would take ten seconds and a surge of will, and it would be like putting a fist through a wall someone had spent months building.

He stepped back. The archive held enough fragmentary horror without forcing more. They could return with proper codes if they survived the next hour.

"Leave it," he said.

Desh nodded once. He didn't argue, but he positioned himself between Ben and the chest, a deliberate choice that made Ben’s skin prickle. Desh left nothing at his back.

The monitoring hub occupied the center of the archive—a cluster of consoles arranged around a central projection plate. Ben moved to the primary console and examined the controls. Physical toggles, same as Level 1. He found the power routing switch and flipped it.

The projection plate flickered. Dust burned off the emitter array in a brief, acrid flash. Then the image resolved.

It was a map of the Outer Rim. It was wrong in the specific way old maps were wrong—sector borders drawn before the Ruusan Reformations, hyperspace lanes marked by routes abandoned for centuries. But the geography underneath was correct, and across it, twelve points of light burned in a loose constellation following the curve of the Corridor.

Eight were dark.

Three pulsed amber, slow and irregular, like a failing heartbeat.

One—the point at the center of the display, positioned precisely where they stood—flashed crimson. The label beneath it scrolled through several status strings before Ben’s translation caught up: *CONTAINMENT CRITICAL. SEAL INTEGRITY: 0%. BREACH CONFIRMED.*

Ben gripped the edge of the console.

Not a tomb. A machine, still running on whatever power source the architects had buried in the mountain’s core, still reporting the status of a system that had been failing for centuries. Eight nodes gone dark as their anchors died. Three still holding, barely.

And this one—the ground beneath his feet—already gone.

The wrongness he’d been tracking across the Rim, the structural displacement, the Force that detoured around this place rather than flowing through it—it wasn't a symptom. It was the breach. He was standing in the hole.

"Ben."

Desh’s voice cut through the spiral. Ben looked up. The soldier stood at the stairwell entrance, one hand braced against the doorframe, his body angled back toward the room. His other hand rested on his blaster—not drawn, but ready.

The lighting below was blue. Emergency registers, ancient and dim, cast the descending stairs in the color of deep water.

Ben crossed to him. His legs felt distant, as if they belonged to someone else. He looked down.

The Force hit him before the visual did—not the structured absence of the upper levels, but a presence. Compressed, slow, and rhythmic.

Three rhythms. Distinct. Separate. Alive.

His hand moved to his lightsaber. He didn't draw it, just touched the grip to ground himself. "Desh," he whispered. "There are three hearts beating down there."

Desh didn't move. His jaw set. He looked at Ben, then at the stairs, then back at Ben. When he spoke, his voice had the particular flatness of a man who had just revised his threat assessment from bad to unknown.

"How long have they been down there?" Desh asked.

Ben didn't answer. He was still listening to the three distinct pulses in the Force. The blue light waited below, patient as the humming in the walls.
