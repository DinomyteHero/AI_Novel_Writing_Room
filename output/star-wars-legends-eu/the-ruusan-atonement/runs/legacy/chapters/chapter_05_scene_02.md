Sera Varik approached the archive terminal as if returning home.

It wasn't the hesitant homecoming of a traveler long removed from familiar halls. Her body remembered the path before her mind could engage. Without looking, her fingers found the activation panel, tapping three specific contacts in sequence. The room hummed in response.

Ben had spent twenty minutes down here with Desh, coaxing corrupted files and squinting at partial displays. Sera needed three seconds. The archive ignited.

Holographic projections bloomed from the consoles. Maps and schematics filled the air. A dense web of twelve nodes spanned the Outer Rim, connected by lines pulsing with a rhythmic, measured light. It reminded Ben of a heart monitor—a thought he immediately tried to suppress. At the center of the web, where the lines converged, a mass of structured geometry rotated. It was beautiful in the way a predator is beautiful. It was also wrong. He felt the distortion even through the projection, a directional pull he’d been tracking since the Rimma corridor, finally given a shape.

Veranthos.

"There," Sera said. She didn't need to point.

The wound throbbed at the center of the web. It wasn't red or dark; it possessed a quality that defied color. The software struggled to render the data, producing a jagged shimmer where the model failed. It looked like a visual representation of a sound pitched beyond human hearing.

"That's what I've been feeling," Ben said. "Since the Rimma. I thought—" He trailed off. What had he believed? That the Council's explanation held weight. Post-Abeloth redistribution. Natural thinning. He’d been skeptical enough to file a mission request, yet not bold enough to push when they flagged it as low-priority.

"You thought your Council had an answer." Torin Hal moved to Ben’s shoulder. He rested his hand on the console’s edge with the ease of a man sitting at his own desk. The gesture was casual, yet it grated on Ben.

"They always do," Torin added.

"The Reformations were penance," Sera said. She summoned a secondary layer of text—dense, archaic script scrolling beside the map. "I want you to understand that word. Not reform. Not evolution. Penance."

"Penance implies guilt," Ben said.

"Yes."

"For what?"

Sera looked at Torin. A silent agreement passed between them, a hand-off of the narrative weight. Torin’s expression shifted. He became warm and deliberate, adopting the tone of a man accustomed to explaining complexities. The tone of a man used to being believed.

"The Brotherhood of Darkness utilized a technique called a lattice inversion," Torin said. "They developed it at Veranthos for years. The theory was that the Force's organizational structure—the distinction between light and dark, the moral feedback loop—could be suspended. Remove the framework, and you remove the conscience. They wanted soldiers who could commit any atrocity without the Force signaling that it was wrong."

Ben’s hands turned cold. He kept them still. "They didn't succeed."

"No. They didn't." Torin paused, letting the silence stretch. "We did."

The room fell still. The display continued its slow, indifferent rotation.

Ben forced a breath out, keeping his voice level. "Gavran's technique."

"Derived from studying Nathema and Malachor V. Anywhere the Force had been structurally damaged, he searched for the mechanism." Sera spoke with the cadence of someone reciting a long-held secret. "When he found it, he used it. At Veranthos, during the final push, the Brotherhood's lines held. We were losing. Gavran deployed the technique on a scale that turned the tide. It worked. The Brotherhood collapsed. The war ended."

"And the wound opened," Ben said.

"The wound was already there. We made it permanent." Sera swiped her fingers across the display, rewinding the timeline. The web of nodes brightened. Twelve lights, steady and active. "The Reformations were announced eighteen months after Ruusan. The official record cited philosophical renewal. The Jedi Lords had grown too militant, the Order needed to be humbled. That is a lie."

Ben stared at the map. The math was simple and devastating. "The seal."

"The seal. Gavran built it because the wound was expanding. Without containment, it would have spread along the Force's pathways until the lattice failed in every connected system." She spoke clinically, which made the reality sharper. "Every Force-sensitive in the galaxy would have lost their moral intuition. The Reformations provided cover for the construction. Disbanding the Houses ensured no one would ask why Gavran needed twelve Jedi Knights for a classified, long-term project."

Twelve. Ben looked at the map. Nine dark nodes. Three lit. One—

"Kael," Ben said.

Kael Drenn leaned against the far wall, arms crossed. He had been quiet since they descended, but it wasn't the silence of processing. It was the silence of a man waiting for his cue.

"The Brotherhood's version didn't work," Kael said. "But they understood the lattice better than the Jedi. Gavran needed someone to translate the inversion mechanism from theory into application." He straightened. "I was that translation."

The pieces shifted in Ben's mind. Kael's presence. His knowledge. His careful distance.

"I was conscripted," Kael added. "Since we're being precise."

Sera remained motionless. Torin’s grip tightened on the console. Ben noted the reactions but kept his focus on Kael—on the flat, hollow way he spoke, as if reporting a fact that had long ago stopped hurting.

"So you were anchors," Ben said. "Structural components. Not volunteers."

"I was told it was for my protection," Sera said. "That the seal required Force-sensitive stabilization and my role was essential and temporary." She spaced her words evenly, the rhythm of someone who had spent centuries deciding how to describe a betrayal. "Gavran did not mention that stabilization would take a millennium. Perhaps he didn't know. He stayed at his post."

"We saw him," Desh said from the doorway. His voice was careful, stripped of inflection. "Level 4. He's still there."

The archive held the silence.

Ben thought of Jacen. He hadn't intended to, but looking at the shimmering wound, he couldn't help it. Soldiers who could do anything without the Force telling them it was wrong. Remove the framework, remove the conscience.

Jacen had approached it from the opposite side. He’d convinced himself the framework was the obstacle—that if he could operate beyond moral constraints, he could save the galaxy. He had been wrong about the method, the goal, and the cost. Ben had watched that descent from close enough to feel the heat of the fire.

The Jedi hadn't evolved after Ruusan. They had panicked. They built a myth of renewal over a cover-up and taught it to every initiate for a thousand years. All while a wound in the Force expanded in the dark because the seal was failing and the anchors were dying.

The Council's file on the Rimma anomaly was three pages of dismissive jargon. Low priority.

Ben looked at the display. Twelve nodes. Nine dark. Three lit.

"The twelfth," he said.

Sera tapped the screen. The empty node pulsed with a different quality—absent rather than dead. A log entry appeared in Gavran's own hand: *High-risk. Brotherhood-trained. Inversion specialist. Do not release without full containment protocol.*

"Darth Veraine," Sera said. "The Brotherhood's lead alchemist. Gavran captured her after Veranthos. She understood the technique better than anyone."

"She's been out for six weeks," Ben said.

"Yes."

"She knows the seal is failing because she was part of it. She can feel the cracks."

"Yes."

Desh spoke from the shadows of the doorway. "And she's had six weeks to act."

The wound at Veranthos throbbed, slow and rhythmic. The nine dead lights remained dark. Ben felt the foundation of his history give way. The Council had no file on Veraine. They had no file on any of this. The home he knew was built on a silence he had mistaken for peace.
