The plateau felt wrong before they were close enough to see why.

Ben sensed it from the ridge. The mountain range spread beneath them like a geological failure—fractured shale and permafrost under a thin, bruised sky. His breath came short. The atmosphere was technically breathable, but each inhale carried a metallic bite that suggested old pipes and sealed rooms. Beside him, Desh adjusted his rebreather filter. He didn't comment on the altitude. He simply acted.

The fortress sat directly below.

From this height, it resembled a fault line or a scar in the mountain's face where tectonic plates had collided. The stone gave way to unnaturally straight lines. Ben stared for ten seconds before his brain accepted the shape as a building. No crests. No columns. No ornamentation. The Army of Light had decided that whoever needed to find this place would find it; everyone else could keep walking.

"No approach cover," Desh said. He scanned the descent with a field scope, his movements unhurried and professional. "Switchback trail on the eastern face. One choke point. Two, if you count the lip of that ledge."

"Cover isn't our problem."

"Cover is always our problem." Desh lowered the scope to look at him. "We don't have an extraction plan yet, so we aren't calling it a problem. Which means we should be."

Ben didn't respond. He began the descent.

The trail was worse than the view suggested. Permafrost had wedged into every crack, turning the surface into a shifting negotiation of loose stone. Ben used the Force to stabilize his footing. He kept his attention forward, watching the fortress and the way the wrongness concentrated as they dropped.

On the ridge, the sensation had been ambient, like a dull headache. Down here, it turned directional. The Force didn't flow through the fortress; it diverted around the structure like water hitting a stone pier. The wake created something cold and structured at the center. Ben reached out and immediately recoiled. The feedback felt like pressing an ear against a pipe and hearing something heavy moving inside—channeled, concentrated, and indifferent to his presence.

It wasn't dark. He waited for the familiar pressure of the dark side, that greasy static preceding every Sith artifact he had ever encountered. This was different. This was absence organized into a shape.

They reached the fortress face.

Up close, the stone revealed itself as alloy beneath a millennium of mineral accretion. The mountain had swallowed the structure, or perhaps the builders had encouraged the growth. Permafrost painted a white crust along every horizontal surface. Ben pressed his palm against the wall. Cold bit through his glove.

"Look at the patina depth." Desh crouched, running a finger along the join between alloy and bedrock. "That isn't surface weathering. The mineral growth is structural. The mountain has been integrating this thing for centuries." He stood, meeting Ben’s eyes. "Whatever is inside wasn't built to be opened. It was built to stay."

The words landed with unexpected weight. Ben turned away before Desh could read his expression.

He moved to the main seal.

The slab stood the height of four men—a mass of alloy and compressed stone fitted into the mountain with a precision that made the word "door" feel inadequate. No handles. No visible mechanism. No markings. There was only the hair-thin join line and the sourceless pressure emanating from within.

He tried a telekinetic probe first. He felt for a locking mechanism’s center of mass, the way he would with a standard blast door. Nothing. The Force slid around the slab, finding no purchase or resonance. He pushed harder. The slab remained inert.

He stepped back and tried a meditative probe. He extended his awareness into the metal, searching for the mechanism’s logic. He found it—old, heavy, and built with an aggressive redundancy. The engineers had designed this to resist anyone who approached it the wrong way.

The problem was he didn't know the right way.

The required signature wasn't a technique; it was a weight. The Jedi Lords had carried a density of presence, a rawness his training had spent years refining away. His signature was clean. Precise. The mechanism didn't care. It looked for something heavier.

"It isn't working," he said.

"I noticed," Desh replied. "You've been standing there for four minutes."

Ben’s jaw set. He turned back to the slab and pushed again. He didn't use more power, but he allowed frustration to bleed into the gesture. The metal ignored the effort. It ignored the precision. It ignored a decade of disciplined learning.

His palm struck the slab harder than intended. The metal remained indifferent.

He breathed, though the thin air offered little satisfaction. He stopped trying to read the mechanism and simply stood there, hand flat against the alloy. Cold seeped through his glove into his marrow. The wrongness pressed against the back of his skull. His arm ached. His lungs burned. He was exhausted, the Force was behaving in ways he couldn't name, and the door wouldn't budge. The "SEAL FAILING" warning from the relay station felt like a promise he was about to break.

The mechanism moved.

It wasn't a click or a release of pressure. The mountain shifted. A groan began deep in the bedrock, traveling through the slab into his palm and bones. The sound bypassed his ears entirely. He yanked his hand back, but the slab didn't stop. It receded, drawing into the mountain face with the inexorable weight of something that hadn't moved in a thousand years.

Desh moved to his side, weapon raised.

Ben’s hand hung in the empty air.

He hadn't done anything. He hadn't pushed or probed. He had simply been tired and let his guard down, and the mechanism had recognized him anyway. Not his training. Not his technique. It recognized something older—the resonance his family carried like a frequency he could never quite modulate.

The fortress hadn't seen a Jedi. It had seen a Skywalker.

His stomach tightened. Relief didn't come. Instead, he felt the cold certainty that he had crossed an irreversible threshold. Something in this mountain had been waiting for a Skywalker to press a hand against the lock. He looked at Desh. The soldier was scanning the darkness beyond the gap, his face set in the hard lines he wore when calculating bad odds.

The gap was wide enough to enter. The air spilling out was ancient—dry, cold, and tasting of sealed stone. Ben stood at the threshold. The wrongness was no longer ambient. It was specific. Concentrated. 

It was waiting.

"Defensive formation?" Desh asked.

Ben gripped his lightsaber without igniting the blade. His hand remained cold from the alloy. "Two meters back and right."

He stepped into the dark.
