The Force-lock sat at the back of the records room like a knot tied by someone who expected it to hold forever.

Ben studied the raised panel. It sat flush against the wall with no visible interface, the metal itself feeling wrong in a way he couldn't categorize. He reached out, sensing the mechanism through the Force, but the sensation was like pressing a thumb against a bruise. The thin place made concentration slippery. Every time he tried to grasp the lock's internal structure, his perception of the physical panel shifted a few inches to the left. Parallax error made his hands feel disconnected from his intent.

He was calculating a new approach when Sera stepped past him. She pressed both palms flat against the panel and exhaled.

The lock didn't click. It released—a slow, settling pressure, like a fist unclenching. Ben felt the change in texture. Something dense became something open.

Sera pulled her hands back as the panel swung inward.

"How long would that have taken you?" she asked. It wasn't a taunt, but a genuine assessment.

Ben's first instinct was to deflect—to cite training protocols or frame it as a matter of methodology. He caught himself. "Hours. Maybe a day. I would have had to map the lattice structure first. My training assumes a certain baseline coherence in the Force."

"The baseline doesn't exist here." Sera moved through the doorway. "So you can't assume it."

He followed her. Recognition stirred in his chest. It was the specific, sharp realization that comes from watching someone perform the impossible.

***

The records room was smaller than the main chamber, its walls lined with archival terminals in a format Ben didn't recognize. There was no flowing Jedi architecture here, no calligraphic labels. These were military specification: numbered, indexed, and utilitarian. Desh stayed near the entrance, back against the wall, watching the corridor. Ben sensed him without looking; the man was a steady, quiet absence in the Force, a signal Ben had learned to interpret as safety.

Sera activated the primary terminal. Dim holographics flickered to life, rendered in an archaic display format. The projections were thinner than modern holos, slightly translucent, and the text used a High Galactic dialect Ben had to translate word by word.

"These are operational logs," he said. "Not histories."

"They were never meant to be histories." Sera scrolled through the initial index, her eyes darting across the lines. "The Council wouldn't have authorized what Gavran did. So Gavran didn't ask."

The logs were terse. Dates used Old Republic notation. Personnel were listed by designation rather than name. Resource allocation. Casualty projections. Ben read one entry and then read it again, certain he'd mistranslated.

"This says 'acceptable loss threshold: thirty percent of anchor cohort.' That's—"

"Twelve people." Sera didn't look away from the screen. "Yes."

Ben’s hands went cold. "He planned for a third of the anchors to die in the first decade."

"He planned for it, budgeted for it, and moved on." She tapped a key, pulling up a secondary file. "Gavran was fighting a war. He made war decisions."

The words hung in the stale air. Ben wanted to argue—to say that war didn't excuse that kind of calculus, that there had to be another way to read the data. But the numbers were clear. "The Reformations erased this."

"The Reformations decided it was a problem to erase rather than reckon with." Sera's voice carried no judgment, which made the revelation heavier. "Easier to rewrite the history than to live with what the Jedi Lords actually were."

The thin place pressed at the edges of Ben's concentration like static. He kept losing the thread of the text. It wasn't confusion; it was as if the part of his mind that monitored his surroundings had gone out of alignment. He realized he'd stopped tracking the room's exits and forced himself to scan the perimeter.

"The lattice inversion technique," he said, pointing to a line of text. "The records reference it as Jedi doctrine."

Sera's hands stilled.

"Not originally Sith doctrine," Ben continued, reading carefully. "This log attributes development to the Combined Lore Division of the Army of Light. They built it. The Brotherhood of Darkness adapted it."

He looked up. Sera was staring at the screen with an expression between vindication and grief.

"The modern Order believes the Brotherhood developed it," he said.

"The modern Order believes what made the story cleaner." She resumed scrolling, her movements precise. "We found the technique by studying the Force wounds. Nathema. Malachor. We wanted to understand how the lattice broke so we could repair it. Somewhere in that research, someone worked out that it could be applied deliberately." She paused on a file. "Torin was part of that division."

Ben felt the ground shift. Torin—the man Sera had trusted, had watched use a technique he'd helped develop. 

"He volunteered," Sera said, her voice dropping to a whisper. "That's what matters. He volunteered to use it, he used it wrong, and then Gavran built the seal to contain what was left."

She turned to him. Ben saw an exhaustion in her eyes that went deeper than physical fatigue.

"And then the Reformations happened," she continued, "and the whole thing got filed under 'necessary sacrifice' and closed. Everyone who knew the truth either died or learned to keep quiet." She gestured at the logs. "You wanted to know why I'm not impressed by your version of history."

Ben looked at the operational log entry. *Acceptable loss threshold.* His throat tightened. The comfortable narrative of his youth—the Jedi as keepers of peace, the Reformations as a necessary correction—was cracking. Underneath lay something far more damning. "I'm starting to understand."

***

He reached for the data drive port. They needed to move, and he'd seen astromechs pull entire archive systems in minutes. 

"Wait," Sera said.

She was staring at the primary terminal with an intensity that stopped his hand mid-motion. She wasn't looking at the data, but at something adjacent to it.

"There's a residue," she said. "Someone accessed this terminal and pulled a query through the Force rather than the interface. The mechanism still holds the shape of the intent."

"Veraine."

"Her signature is—" Sera hesitated. Ben heard uncertainty in her voice for the first time. "Distinct. Corroded. A thousand years in stasis changes a Force presence. I can read what she looked for."

Sera pressed her palm to the console.

The holographics didn't flicker. They bled—the existing display dissolving at the edges and reforming around a single point of data. The terminal had been holding something beneath the surface, and Sera had simply asked it to reveal itself. Ben watched her, recognizing that this wasn't a trick from a training hall. This was the work of someone who had spent decades mastering Force-mechanics his entire Order had let atrophy.

The reformed display held a single record: a facility index from the early years of the seal. At the top sat a location designation. Coordinates in Old Republic notation.

"Veranthos," Sera said. The name carried weight—a destination, a reckoning, a wound finally given a name.

Ben cross-referenced the coordinates on his datapad. The name matched the tactical data Kael Drenn had flagged aboard the *Steady Returns*.

"This is where they ran the first lattice inversion test," Sera said. "Before Ruusan. Veranthos was the proof that the technique worked." She pulled her hand from the console. Her fingers trembled before she curled them into a fist. "And it's where the wound is deepest. The original breach, sealed over and buried."

Ben overlaid the coordinates against the map Desh had built. The point sat at the southwestern terminus—the end of the path. His pulse quickened. "She's not dismantling the seal at random. She's following the lineage of the technique back to its source."

"No." Sera's expression cleared into a frightening clarity. "She's going home."

Desh appeared in the doorway, unhurried. "We good?"

Ben looked at the coordinates. Veranthos. A thousand-year-old wound with a location, and a Sith alchemist already moving toward it. The distance between them had collapsed. "We have a destination," he said. "She has a head start, but we know where the line ends."

Desh nodded, already calculating logistics. "Then we should stop standing in dead rooms."

Ben pocketed the datapad and followed him out. Sera moved past them both without a word. He could feel her in the Force—no longer the stern authority, but something unsettled. She was a woman who had just watched her own history rewritten in real time.
