The interior wall of the chamber was smooth.

Ben ran his fingers along the curve. He found no gouges, no frantic scratching. A thousand years of pressure had worn the composite material into slow, overlapping arcs—deliberate, rhythmic, each pass no deeper than the last. He traced a groove with his thumb. The Force felt thin here, not dark, but drained. It was the sensation of a room where the air had been breathed down to nothing.

"She didn't panic," he said.

Desh stood at the edge of the cell, scanning the monitoring lead with a handheld sensor. "Whoever she was."

"She worked the same section. Over and over." Ben stepped back. The pattern emerged from a distance—concentric rings worn into the inner lining, centered on the single stress point where the containment field anchored to the wall. "She was looking for the load-bearing joint. She wasn't trying to break out. She was mapping the structure."

Desh lowered the sensor and studied the wall. 

"That's not a prisoner," Desh said. "That's an engineer."

The Level 1 monitoring station smelled of scorched metal and dead circuits. Emergency lighting painted the consoles amber. Desh pried the access panel off a terminal that predated the Republic by a margin that made the distinction meaningless. He spent four minutes coaxing it into life with a bypass kit and a string of curses Ben didn't recognize.

"Old Imperial relay logic," Desh muttered. "Same principles. Different era." 

The screen flickered. Data scrolled in a format Ben couldn't parse, but the underlying architecture remained familiar. A log. A timestamp. A prisoner designation.

*ANCHOR PRIME — DESIGNATION: DARTH VERAINE. CLASSIFICATION: BROTHERHOOD ALCHEMIST / INVERSION SPECIALIST. CONTAINMENT PRIORITY: MAXIMUM. DO NOT RELEASE WITHOUT FULL PROTOCOL.*

Below the header: six weeks of unanswered alerts.

Ben read the file twice. Veraine had entered the system as a captured combatant. Gavran’s own notation, clipped and precise, described her as the Brotherhood’s lead theorist on lattice mechanics. She had understood the technique better than anyone alive. She had been conscripted as Anchor Prime because her connection to the inversion mechanism ran deeper than any of the others. She was the keystone.

Gavran had known exactly what he was doing.

Ben pulled his attention to the room. Sera read over his shoulder, her expression locked. Torin stood to the left, arms folded, eyes fixed on the floor. Kael Drenn lingered near the doorway. When the name appeared on the screen, Ben caught it: a stillness that lasted a heartbeat too long before Kael’s posture reset to a careful neutrality.

Ben’s pulse spiked. He forced his breathing to remain steady. He filed the reaction away and said nothing.

"Anchor Prime," Sera said. Her voice was flat. "He gave her the structural role with the highest load-bearing weight."

"Because she was the most connected to the technique," Ben said.

"Because she was the most dangerous." Sera turned to face him. A hardness had settled into her gaze. "If Gavran needed to ensure the seal held, he put his highest-risk anchor where a failure would be most contained. She was load-bearing because losing her would be catastrophic."

"And he sealed her in anyway."

"Yes." The word landed like a verdict.

Desh scrolled through the entries. The logs were sparse—mostly automated system checks—but anomaly flags began clustering four hundred years back. Small deviations in containment pressure. Within tolerance. Then, gradually, they drifted.

"She was eroding the anchor point from the inside," Desh said. "The system flagged it. Nobody answered."

Nobody had been left to answer. Gavran had died at his post on Level 4, and after him, the station had fallen silent.

Ben looked at the timestamps. Veraine had been degrading the seal for centuries. There was no spike in the readings, no frantic surge of power. It was steady. Measured. The same slow arcs worn into the chamber wall had been translated into the Force itself.

"She knew she was structural," Ben said. "She knew what she was holding together. And she did it anyway."

"She didn't break it to escape." Desh found a secondary log, buried under a partially corrupted system cache. He turned the screen. "She broke it because she wanted to."

The secondary log was in her handwriting—or the digital equivalent of a Force-imprint annotation. The notation was dated two weeks before her escape.

*The lattice is not a property. It is a constraint. Gavran built his seal around a wound he thought needed closing. The wound is not the disease. The seal is.*

Ben gripped the edge of the terminal. 

The Force signature in the empty chamber hadn't felt like Maul or Vader. Those presences were heat—anger compressed into a point. What lingered in Veraine’s chamber was colder. It was the absence of structure. The Force was present but stripped of its organizing principle, the way one might have hydrogen and oxygen without water.

He thought about Jacen. Jacen had believed the constraints were the problem, too. He had been wrong about the method, the goal, and the cost.

But Jacen had been driven by grief and ambition, twisting himself into shapes he couldn't sustain. This room held no grief. No ambition. Only certainty.

That was worse.

"She stayed," Desh said. He cross-referenced the main log. "Six days after the pod opened. She stayed in the facility."

"She was studying," Ben said.

"The archive. She accessed it repeatedly. And then—" Desh paused. "Here. A system purge. Initiated from this terminal."

The log showed a targeted deletion. It was clean and precise, stripping the archive of three specific datasets. The purge had been thorough; Ben wouldn't have known what was missing if the deletion record hadn't been preserved in the backup cache.

The Corridor. The node locations. The seal’s infrastructure, mapped in full.

She had taken a copy and destroyed the original.

Ben’s chest tightened. He leaned forward, searching the timestamps as if they might rearrange themselves into something less damning.

"She mapped the whole system," he said. "Then she made sure no one else could." 

He looked at the Corridor display. A line of nodes stretched from the Unknown Regions into the modern galaxy. Twelve lights—one for each anchor. Most of them were amber, flagging a slow degradation. 

"She knows every weak point. Every node close to failure. She could accelerate the collapse wherever she wanted."

Sera moved closer to the screen. "Or she already has."

"Six weeks," Torin said. His voice startled the room. "We don't know where she's been."

Six weeks. Ben calculated the time. She would have needed to reorient—a thousand years of stasis, a galaxy that had moved entirely past her frame of reference. She needed resources. Transport. She would have had nothing when she walked out of that cell.

She had used those six weeks to solve every problem. She was a strategist who had been granted a millennium to think.

At the corner of the terminal, a single indicator light blinked in slow amber pulses. Ben traced it back to its source: a distress signal Gavran had initiated before his death. It had been routed to a Jedi frequency that no longer existed, blinking unanswered into the void for a thousand years.

It had been answered now. By them. Too late.

Ben looked at the Corridor map. Twelve nodes. A line of failing infrastructure pointing toward the wound like an arrow. Veraine was already moving.

They hadn't even started.

Kael was watching him—not the screen, but Ben’s face. When their eyes met, Kael looked away, his shoulders rigid.

Ben filed that, too. But the filing felt incomplete. There was something in Kael’s reaction that didn't fit the shape of simple recognition. It looked like confirmation.
