The question cut through the *Steady Returns*’ background hum like a vibroblade through plasteel.

"The Reformations, Skywalker." Sera Varik stood in the hatchway between the cockpit and the common area. Her arms were crossed, her voice carrying the flatness of someone who already suspected the answer. "Tell me what the history books say they were for."

Ben set down the datapad.

The sanitized version waited on his tongue. He had recited variations of it a hundred times in temple classrooms and casual conversation—the easy shorthand of people who grew up knowing the story. *The Jedi disbanded their military structure. Submitted to civilian oversight. Ended the Dark Age.*

He looked at her. She had fought in that Dark Age.

"The official account," he said, "is that the Jedi Lords recognized the dangers of unchecked military power and chose to reform voluntarily. They submitted to the Galactic Republic and surrendered independent command authority." He paused. "The histories frame it as wisdom."

Sera didn't move, but her posture shifted. She looked like a commander watching the monument she’d bled for being rebranded as a gift that cost nothing.

"Wisdom," she said.

"That's the word they use."

"The word they use." She repeated it as if testing the shape of a lie. "Not the word for what happened at Ruusan. Not the word for the hundred thousand soldiers who didn't come home. Not the word for the seal." Her voice stayed level, but her fingers curled against her biceps. "Wisdom is what you call it when you're the one writing the history."

Desh emerged from the galley with a tray of field rations—modern, foil-sealed packets the GAG had used for years. He dropped them on the common table. "Eat. All of you. We've got twenty-eight hours before the Meridian approach."

Torin Hal reached for a packet with the automatic efficiency of a man accustomed to eating when food was available, not when he was hungry. He peeled it open and took a bite. His expression went blank—the look of a palate that expected one thing and received something else entirely. He chewed anyway.

Kael remained in his corner. He took the ration Desh extended, turned it over once, and set it on his knee.

Sera hadn't moved from the hatchway. She watched Ben with a heavy, silent intensity.

"The Ruusan campaign ended the war," she said finally. "Hundreds of thousands dead. The Army of Light—" She stopped. "You're telling me the Republic called that a *reorganization*."

"They called it a reformation." Ben kept his voice even, though he felt the inadequacy of the words. "It's in the archives as a turning point. The moment the Jedi chose peace over power."

"Peace." Sera's laugh was sharp. "Is that what they called it?"

"The seal, the wound, the technique Gavran used—none of that made it into the surviving records."

"Of course it didn't." She stepped into the common area. It wasn't a relaxation of her guard; she simply needed to move against the tension. "They couldn't write down what they did. They had to pretend it was a choice."

Torin looked up from his meal. "How complete are the current historical records? Regarding the New Sith Wars specifically."

The question was tactical. Ben noted the angle—not *what happened to us*, but *what do you know*. Torin was managing the room’s temperature.

"Partial," Ben said. "The Brotherhood of Darkness, the Army of Light, the major campaigns—those survived. The personal records, the correspondence, anything deemed strategically insignificant—" He made a small gesture. "A thousand years is a long time."

"What about the Jedi Lords?" Torin asked. "Their individual records?"

"Some. The major figures."

Torin nodded. "Hyperdrive classifications. What's the standard transit time from the Core to the Outer Rim?"

Ben provided the Class 1 and Class 2 specifications. Torin followed up with three more questions in quick succession: jump accuracy, fleet coordination protocols, and the current reach of the Galactic Alliance military. Each answer was filed away. He was building a tactical picture of the modern galaxy with the same efficiency he'd used to map the *Steady Returns*.

Desh watched him from the galley hatch, wearing the expression he reserved for situations that hadn't gone wrong yet.

Kael still hadn't opened his ration.

Ben picked up the datapad, needing something to do with his hands. The Ruusan records were still active. He scrolled past campaign histories, lists of ceded planets, and annotated Sith strongholds.

"Kael." He hadn't planned to speak. "Where were you from? Before Ruusan."

Kael looked at him. The dry humor usually in his eyes had vanished, leaving nothing behind. "Veshar's moon. Second moon. Sith held it as a training facility." He paused. "Small place. Maybe forty thousand people in the settlements."

Ben typed the name.

He searched three different ways. He cross-referenced coordinate regions, pulled archived Ruusan-era maps, and ran the search against the planetary registry.

Nothing.

Not a destroyed record or a footnote. The galaxy had moved on without ever writing the name down. Ben stared at the screen a moment longer than necessary.

"I'm sorry, Kael." He looked up. "It's not here."

Kael didn't move. The ration packet stayed on his knee, unopened. His face remained watchful and detached, but something behind it stopped. A signal that had been transmitting on a low frequency, so constant it was almost silent, had finally gone dead.

He nodded once.

Ben had no follow-up. No Jedi reassurance about the Force, no tactical redirection—nothing that wouldn't be worse than silence. He had a datapad with a gap where forty thousand people used to be.

Sera watched Kael from the table's edge. Her rigid posture softened. She recognized the wound.

Desh set a cup of hot caf near Kael's elbow and retreated to the galley without a word. No performance of sympathy. Just the cup.

Kael looked at it. He didn't pick it up, but his hand moved to the ration packet and tore it open.

Ben put the datapad down.

The common area held only the sound of eating and the hum of recycled air. Sera finally sat at the far end of the table. She didn't eat or speak, but her presence felt deliberate.

Ben felt a gaze on him.

Torin Hal was watching from across the table. His expression had nothing to do with grief. It was measured—the look of a commander who had watched a subordinate handle an ugly situation and was adjusting his assessment. He gave a short, precise nod.

The acknowledgment of a junior officer.

Ben held the look for a moment, then returned to the screen. The gap in the records. The sanitized history that would keep Sera awake for days. The cup of caf.

Twenty-eight hours to Meridian.

He picked the datapad back up and kept working.
