The cursor blinked.

Ben deleted the sentence and started again.

*Priority report, Jedi Temple Coruscant, for Master Skywalker: the perimeter facility on WR-7741 has confirmed the anomaly is—*

He stopped. Read it back. Scrapped it.

The cockpit of the *Steady Returns* was dim, lit only by the amber glow of running lights. The nav-console’s blue radiance pooled across the viewport, making the stars beyond look like pinpricks printed on glass. Desh was aft, running an equipment check that didn't need doing—his way of granting Ben space without having to say so.

Ben tried again.

*Priority report. Classification: urgent. Jedi Knight Ben Skywalker to Grand Master Luke Skywalker. Have identified a network of containment relay stations along the Ruusan Corridor consistent with pre-Reform Army of Light construction. Evidence suggests an active seal failure of unknown origin. Requesting—*

Requesting what? A briefing? Three weeks of committee sessions while the slow pulse at the end of that chain continued to falter? He could script the next six months in his sleep: the report would go to the Council, the Council would convene a working group, the working group would recommend a survey team, and the survey team would arrive at WR-7741 to find a facility that had been broadcasting a distress signal into an empty room for a millennium. They would take excellent notes.

He cleared the screen. The datapad went blank.

The nav-computer had been grinding through its calculation for eleven minutes. The terminus coordinates sat in the corner of the display, patient and unnamed, a point in space that existed in no chart Ben had ever studied. Forty-three minutes until they could jump.

*If* they jumped.

He flipped the datapad face-down on the console.

The seal was failing in real time. The anchor degradation pattern made that clear—one node going dark every few decades until the cascade became inevitable. The facility's alarm hadn't tripped because something had changed recently; it had tripped because the last redundancy finally gave out. Whatever sat at the terminus had been losing containment for centuries. Every hour spent waiting for committee approval was an hour the structure spent collapsing.

That was true. He was reasonably confident it was true.

The part where he was the only one who could do anything about it—he felt less confident about that. But the conclusion pointed the same direction regardless.

Footsteps echoed in the corridor. Desh ducked through the hatch carrying two cups of caf and the unhurried posture of a man who had decided not to rush. He handed one to Ben without comment, settled into the co-pilot seat, and studied the nav-display.

"Forty-one minutes," he said.

"Forty-three when I last checked."

"Nav-computer's running hot. It wants to be done." Desh sipped his caf. "Fuel consumption's going to be ugly. No lane data, no gravitational surveys—the computer's threading between projected masses."

"I know."

"And there's no beacon in that sector. If we have a problem on the far side, we're not getting a signal back to anyone for—"

"I know, Desh."

Desh nodded. He looked at the blank datapad on the console, then back at the display. He remained silent.

Ben watched the nav-computer work. The calculation filled in piece by piece, the route assembling itself from extrapolated data and calculated risk. Each segment was a best guess dressed up as certainty. That was all hyperspace navigation was, when you stripped the math away. Confidence formalized.

Jacen had also believed he was the only one who understood the shape of the problem. He had also done the math and reached a conclusion no one else could see. He had also decided that the cost of waiting for others to catch up was too high.

Ben wasn't doing that. He was certain he wasn't, with the specific conviction of someone who has examined the comparison and rejected it. Jacen had pursued power. Ben was trying to keep something from breaking. The difference mattered.

The Force disagreed with him in a mild, persistent way—not a warning, exactly, but a pressure in his back teeth. The structure of the galaxy felt thin here, thinner than the surface of the anomaly sites, thinner than the facility's cold corridors. It felt like standing in a room where someone had left a window open that he couldn't find.

"What's the ETA on the Council's response?" Desh asked.

Ben picked up his caf. Drank. Set it down.

The silence stretched until Desh turned to look at him.

"You're not reporting this," Desh said.

It wasn't an accusation. It was an observation, delivered with the flat precision of a man who had spent fifteen years in intelligence and knew the difference between a person deciding something and a person who had already decided. Ben had the clean datapad. Ben had the active nav-calculation. Ben had the look—Desh had likely seen it before on analysts who went off-book because they were certain the book was wrong.

Ben’s jaw set. He placed the caf down harder than necessary.

"Not yet," he said.

"Not yet." Desh repeated it without inflection. He leaned back, his body language unchanged, but the air in the cockpit suddenly felt heavy. "That's the phrase. That's always the phrase."

"The seal is failing right now," Ben said, his words gaining speed. "If I send that report, we spend two weeks getting authorization to do what we can do in the next six hours. You saw the facility. You heard the alarm."

"I did."

"Then you understand the timeline."

"I understand *a* timeline," Desh said. His voice stayed level, but something colder than anger surfaced. Recognition. "I understand the one where we jump to an unmapped sector with no backup and no beacon because you've decided the math works out." He turned to face Ben fully. "I ran operations for the GAG, Ben. I know what 'not yet' looks like from the inside. It looks exactly like this."

Ben's hands curled against the armrest. He wanted to argue. He wanted to explain the difference between Jacen's certainty and his own. Instead, he held Desh's gaze and found no judgment there—just the flat acknowledgment of someone who had watched this pattern before and was choosing to walk into it anyway.

"I'll report everything when we have something concrete," Ben said. "Right now all I have is a relay station and a distress signal. The Council needs more than that."

"The Council needs more than a thousand-year-old alarm saying the seal is failing." It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"That's not why you're not reporting."

Ben didn't answer. He couldn't. The silence between them crystallized. Desh knew. Desh had always known. And Desh was going anyway.

Desh picked up his caf and looked at the nav-display. "Thirty-eight minutes," he said. The words carried the weight of a line crossed. Not by Desh. By Ben. And Desh was following him across it.

They sat in the blue-lit quiet while the computer threaded a route through unmapped dark. Ben's hands stayed on the controls even though there was nothing to steer. The calculation crept toward completion, each segment a commitment to the next. The Force pressed against the back of his skull with that persistent, sourceless pressure—the lattice of reality felt strained, as if something at the end of this route was pulling the shape of things wrong.

When the computer chimed, Ben reached for the controls without looking at his co-pilot. His fingers moved through the sequence. Committed. Locked in.

The stars stretched. The tunnel opened. Hyperspace swallowed them.

It didn't help. Ben had expected the familiar quality of hyperspace—the sealed-off stillness, the sense of existing between places—but the wrongness came through the translation intact. It was more present now. Like a frequency that had been at the edge of his hearing was now closer, not because it had moved, but because everything else had gone quiet around it.

His hands stayed on the controls. There was nothing to steer. He kept them there anyway, gripping the handles until his knuckles whitened.

The blue light held them. The terminus waited at the other end of a route that existed only because the computer had decided to believe in it. Ben watched the hyperspace tunnel and felt the wrongness intensify. It wasn't a warning anymore; it was a presence. Something at the end of this route was warping the shape of things, and with each second, Ben moved closer to whatever was waiting. The structure of the universe pressed against him, steady and patient, like something that had been waiting a very long time for someone to finally arrive.
