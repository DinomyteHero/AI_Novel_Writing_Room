The training blade caught him across the shoulder.

It shouldn’t have. Ben’s guard was solid—Soresu opening, weight balanced, blade where it needed to be for the third exchange of the evening. But the ambient field stuttered. The field arrived a half-second late, like a musician coming in flat, and his parry hit empty air a beat too late. Rendell’s saber cracked against his shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise through his tunic.

Ben stepped back, deactivated his blade, and kept his face even. “Good hit.”

Rendell lowered his weapon, frowning. “You sure you’re—”

“Long week.” Ben clipped his training saber to his belt and reached for his water flask. “Mind’s not in it. Taungsday I blamed the late shift. Zhellday I blamed the meditation chamber. Figured I’d just push through today, but—” He gave a small shrug.

Rendell nodded, and Ben let him. Rendell didn’t feel it. None of them felt it. The field was half a beat off, and Ben was the only one in the salle who could hear the difference.

“Another round?” Rendell asked.

“Think I’ll call it.” Ben took a long pull from the flask, letting the delay fill with casual hydration. “You know how it is. Some days the field just doesn’t answer when you reach for it.”

He smiled. It wasn’t a lie—exactly. Just a carefully shaped truth.

Rendell gathered his gear. “See you at morning drill.”

“Yeah. Good spar.”

The door hissed shut behind him. Ben set the flask down and picked up a hand towel, mopping sweat from his face and neck. His hands had gone slick again. He’d noticed that Tuesday, the first time the lag stole a beat during forms. Wednesday he blamed the humidity in the meditation chamber. Thursday he told himself he was simply tired.

Friday had run out of excuses.

Ben crossed the empty salle and let his body fall into the Soresu opening sequence without igniting the blade. Advance. Parry. Pivot. Return. His muscles knew the pattern the way his lungs knew breathing—involuntary, absolute. What they didn’t know was the way the field kept arriving a half-beat behind the rhythm, the way it had been doing all week, as if someone had shifted the tempo and neglected to tell him.

He’d been telling himself it was fatigue. That was the easiest answer, and easy answers had the advantage of requiring no difficult conversations. No sitting across from Master Katarn and explaining that the Temple’s presence—the gathered echo of every Knight who had ever meditated in these halls, every Padawan who had fumbled their first forms here—had picked up a stutter.

Easy answers were also, occasionally, wrong.

Ben finished the sequence and held the final position, blade lowered, weight settled. The salle was quiet now. Rendell’s footsteps had faded down the west corridor, joining the thin stream of late-shift Knights heading toward quarters. Somewhere in the eastern wing a cleaning droid hummed through its rounds. Ben could hear the turbolift chimes from the main corridor—someone heading up to the archives, probably, or down to the refectory.

The Temple was never truly empty. Twelve thousand Jedi, Padawans, and support staff, plus the bureaucrats, plus the diplomatic staff, plus the families. Coruscant in miniature, a city inside a city, and at this hour it sounded like one: the murmur of voices, the distant thrum of machinery, the particular quality of recycled air Ben had stopped noticing years ago and was noticing now because the field kept arriving late.

He moved to the weapons rack and clipped his training saber into its slot. Then he stood there, hands at his sides, and let himself feel it.

The lag. That was the only word for it. Not an absence—the field was present, warm, steady, the way it was supposed to be in a place like this. But it was arriving a half-second behind where his body expected it. Like the difference between a live musician and a recording played a fraction too slowly. His reflexes had been built on the live version. They didn’t know what to do with the delay.

All week Ben had been telling himself it was nothing. Residual echo from the Abeloth crisis, maybe—the analysts had a name for it, residual echo, as if the field had taken a blow and was still wobbling from it. He’d seen the reports. Other Knights had felt it too, the analysts said. Background noise. Nothing to investigate.

But other Knights weren’t losing sparring exchanges to training blades.

Ben exhaled slowly and rolled his shoulders, working out the stiffness from the bout. The hit hadn’t hurt. That was the annoying thing. It had been a clean strike, technically sound, and it hadn’t hurt at all. What it had done was prove, for the third time in a week, that something was off in the way the field felt in the Temple.

He could feel it now. Standing still, breathing evenly, the presence settling around him like it always had—and lagging. A half-beat. Like a song he knew by heart suddenly playing just behind the metronome.

Ben closed his eyes and ran through the Soresu sequence again. Slowly this time. Watching for the lag.

There. Mid-pivot, when his weight shifted and his arm came up to cover the opening— field arriving half a second late, as if someone had tapped the beat on a different surface than the one Ben was listening to.

His body finished the movement without him. Muscle memory carried the sequence while his senses registered the gap. That was the problem, right there. His body knew what to do. His body had known since he was seventeen and first cleared for live sparring. But the field wasn’t supporting the way it was supposed to, and without that support, the reflexes were running on their own momentum.

No wonder he’d taken the hit.

Ben opened his eyes. The salle was still empty. The cleaning droid had moved on to the eastern corridor. He could hear it bumping gently against the wall as it navigated the turn.

He stood there for a long moment, breathing, feeling the problem and not naming it. Because naming it meant admitting he’d been feeling this for days and telling everyone he was fine. Naming it meant sitting across from someone and saying the words out loud: something is wrong with the field.

The knot in his chest tightened. Not frustration anymore—something colder. Sharper.

He wasn’t failing. Something was wrong.

The knowledge sat in him without edges. Ben let it sit there, felt it settle into his bones the way the Temple’s presence had always settled—warm, steady, present. Except wrong now. Structural wrong, the way a cracked foundation was structural, not something you could ignore until the walls shifted.

He was still standing there when he heard the footsteps.

They came from the main corridor—measured, unhurried, familiar in a way that made Ben straighten before he consciously recognized why. Luke appeared in the doorway of the salle, datapad in hand, expression carrying the particular weight that meant a mission was coming.

Ben’s chest tightened again. Not the cold knot of the field’s delay—something closer to hope, and just as reluctant. Luke was here, which meant Ben wasn’t going to be alone with this after all.

He wasn’t sure yet whether that was a relief.
