Luke didn't sit. He rarely did in the office; there was always somewhere to be, someone waiting, the particular restlessness of a man who carried an institution on his shoulders. He stood by the desk with the datapad loose in his hand, and the blue glow of the screen caught the lines of his face.

"Ben."

"Grand Master." Ben stepped inside and let the door hiss shut behind him. The room held the smell of old datapads and recycled air, the same stale, worked-in air every office in the Temple seemed to share. Through the window, Coruscant traffic murmured like a distant sea — the endless low wash of a world that never stopped moving.

Luke set the datapad on the desk and slid it across the surface. "Seventeen reports. Survey teams in the Outer Rim flagged the same anomaly over the past six weeks."

Ben reached for it. His fingers closed around the edge before his mind caught up with the motion, and the wrongness came at him at once — sour beneath the Temple's usual harmonics, a faint wrong note that had been following him since the salle. It was here too. Fainter than in the salle, but present. Like hearing a song played slightly flat in the next room.

He drew the datapad closer and scrolled. The flagged reports clustered along a bearing — Outer Rim, deep past the civilized sectors, a region where the Jedi survey network thinned to nothing. Three reports showed the same signature: Force readings that didn't match anything in the classification system. Not dark side contamination. Not a residual echo from Abeloth. Something else.

Ben zoomed in on the third one. The data matched what he'd been carrying for weeks — the same quality of wrongness, the same sourness in the Force-weave. He'd carried it alone. He'd told himself it was nothing, or fatigue, or the sort of problem a Jedi learned to swallow without bringing it to anyone else.

The Order had noticed.

"Skywalkers and a few Knights from the Corellia posting," Luke said. "None of them could classify it. The analysts put it in the queue for review, and it sat there until the pattern was obvious enough to flag."

Ben scrolled to another report. The bearing held steady. Whatever produced the signal was sitting in the same general region of space, broadcasting the same wrong note into the Force like a pulse that refused to settle.

He looked up. Luke was watching him — not with the formal weight of a Grand Master delivering a difficult assignment, but with something closer. Something Ben felt before he could name it.

"You've been reading it too," Ben said.

Not a question. Luke nodded once. "For about the same length of time you have."

The words settled in the room between them. Ben felt the brief connection like a pressure change — the sudden, vertiginous jolt of being truly seen by someone who understood exactly what he was feeling. He'd carried this alone for weeks, telling himself it was nothing, telling himself he didn't need to mention it. And his father had been reading the same signals, sitting on the data, waiting.

Waiting for what?

Luke set both hands flat on the desk. Not a gesture of authority — just steadying. "The survey route runs along that bearing. I'm assigning it to you."

Ben's jaw tightened. A survey assignment. A Knight and a skilled pilot, routine Outer Rim coverage, nothing that would require explanation or draw attention. The institutional cover for what was really a search for something that had no classification and no protocol.

"You want me to look at it," Ben said.

"I want you to tell me what you find." Luke's voice stayed even, calm, the Grand Master speaking to a Knight, mission parameters delivered without extra weight. But underneath it, Ben heard something else — the register he recognized from years of sitting across from this man, the tone that belonged to his father and not to the office. "I've read it from here. I need someone closer to the bearing."

Closer. Not alone — but the word carried the implication. Luke wasn't assigning him a partner. Ben wasn't asking for one. The silence around that choice stretched between them.

Ben set the datapad down on the desk. He could still sense the sourness in the Force, fainter here but present, the same wrong note he'd been tracking since Tuesday. His body knew what to do with it — move toward, assess, report back. The instinct that had driven him through the GAG years, through the Lost Tribe, through everything after. Action instead of explanation. Keep moving until the problem broke or he did.

He stepped away from the desk and moved to the window. Coruscant's traffic ran beneath them, a river of lights sliding through the dark like blood through veins. Somewhere down there, people were living their lives, arguing over contracts, falling in love, making the small choices that added up to a galaxy going about its business. None of them carried that wrongness. None of them would.

"Can I have a name for the pilot?" Ben asked. His back was to Luke now, but he could still feel his father's presence in the room — warm, steady, familiar in a way that made his chest ache if he let it.

"Desh Rolan. Former GA Guard. He's cleared for survey work and available for a two-week run."

Desh. Ben felt something ease in his chest — not quite relief, but close enough to sting. Someone who wouldn't ask questions about the Force readings. Someone who understood operational silence the way Ben did, who knew what it meant when a man didn't call his own Order for backup.

"I know Desh," Ben said.

"I know you do."

The words hung there. Luke wasn't asking why Ben wanted a specific pilot. He wasn't opening the roster, wasn't suggesting other options. He'd offered a name and waited, and Ben had taken it, and now neither of them was saying what that choice meant.

Ben turned from the window. Luke was still standing by the desk, hands at his sides, expression carrying the particular calm that meant he was holding something back. The warmth was there — Ben could feel it in the Force, the familiar signature of his father's presence — but it was wrapped in something more careful. Something that knew better than to push.

"Two weeks," Ben said. "I'll report what I find."

"You will." Luke nodded. "And Ben —"

He stopped. Ben waited, but the sentence didn't continue. Luke's mouth shifted once, like he was testing a word and deciding against it. Then he exhaled, slow and even, and the moment passed.

"Fly safe," he said instead.

It wasn't what he'd started to say. Ben knew that. He could feel the shape of the missing words in the room — the thing his father had nearly named and then left unspoken. Something about trust, maybe. Or danger. Or the particular weight of knowing your son was walking toward something you couldn't follow.

But Luke didn't say it. Ben didn't ask.

The distance between them was necessary. It always had been.

"I'll file the departure manifest tonight," Ben said.

"Do that."

Luke picked up the datapad. The blue light returned to his face, and the Grand Master was back in the room — the administrator, the leader, the man who carried twelve thousand Jedi on his shoulders and could not afford to let any of it show. He moved toward the door, and Ben stepped aside to let him pass.

The door hissed open. Luke paused in the frame.

"Ben."

Ben looked at him. Luke's expression was steady, unreadable, carrying the weight of a thousand conversations that had ended in silence rather than resolution. But his eyes — his eyes were warm. And tired. And present, in the way that only someone who had lost a great deal could be.

"I'll be here when you get back," he said. Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor, leaving Ben alone in the office with the sourness in the Force and the weight of what they hadn't said.

The silence settled around him like a held breath. Ben stood by the window and watched the traffic move, and he didn't let himself feel the relief of being trusted, because feeling it would mean naming what it cost, and he couldn't afford that. Not yet.

Two weeks. A bearing in the Outer Rim. A wrongness that pointed like a compass needle toward something he couldn't yet see.

He had work to do.
