Rendell’s training blade skimmed Ben’s shoulder guard and bit through the layer beneath with a sharp crackle.

Ben took the hit and kept moving on reflex, one step too late, one guard down, the yellow practice blade in his hand still half-raised where it should have been in time to meet the strike. The impact jolted through his arm and into his ribs. Not enough to bruise. Enough to make the point.

Rendell backed off at once, blade low, giving Ben the room to recover that a better sparring partner always did.

Ben rolled the shoulder once and ignored the sting. The salle’s air smelled of heated plastoid, sweat, and the faint metallic tang of saber emitters cycling down. Somewhere behind the open training arch, a cleaning droid hummed past in the corridor, and a turbolift chimed farther down the Temple wing. Late shift. People still moving through the building while he was in here getting schooled by a practice blade.

“Again?” Rendell asked.

Ben glanced at the scored edge of his guard, then at the younger Knight. No point in pretending he had not seen the opening. “You are enjoying this too much.”

Rendell’s mouth twitched. “You offered me a rematch.”

“Bad judgment on my part.” Ben killed the saber and clipped it to his belt before the glow could embarrass him any further. “I am calling fatigue before the Force decides to make a full evening of it.”

Rendell snorted. “That what we are calling that?”

Ben flexed his hand once, feeling the after-echo of the strike in his grip. He could still hear the timing of it. One beat late. The same beat he had missed twice already this week, both times in forms he could do in his sleep, both times on a feeling that had slid under his attention like a boot on a wet floor.

No. Not a feeling. That sounded too much like a problem with him, and problems with him tended to come with people asking questions he did not want to answer before he had answers himself.

He lifted one shoulder in what he hoped passed for easy. “If you tell anyone I got caught looking thoughtful, I will deny it.”

“Looking thoughtful?” Rendell said. “That is not what happened.”

Ben huffed a laugh despite himself. “Fine. Looking tired.”

That earned him a second, more skeptical look, but Rendell accepted it with the easy shrug of someone who had no particular reason to press. “Next time, then.”

“Sure.” Ben picked up his practice blade from the mat and turned it over in his hand. “Next time, I intend to be at least mildly annoying again.”

Rendell gave him a salute with two fingers and headed for the door, already moving on to whatever came next in his evening. Ben watched him go until the door slid shut behind him.

The silence that followed was worse.

Ben stood there a moment with the saber hilt warm in his palm and the shoulder that had been scored starting to remind him it had been scored. He could still feel the half-beat lag in the room, the wrong little drag on the edge of his awareness. The Force in the salle did not come up under his senses cleanly. It arrived just after he expected it, like someone stepping into a room half a second behind their own shadow.

That was a ridiculous thought. He should probably stop thinking in terms of shadows before somebody decided he had gone contemplative on them.

He set the hilt aside on the bench and crossed the mat.

The salle was empty now, all broad floor space and training stations and the thin seam of light from the corridor beyond. The durasteel beneath his boots gave back a firm, familiar answer. The air moved cool against the sweat on his forearms. Ben breathed in through his nose, out through his mouth, and tried to strip the spar down to mechanics.

Timing. Footwork. Reaction speed.

Nothing mystical. Nothing dramatic. Just one ugly little miss.

He rolled his shoulders, centered his weight, and moved into the opening stance of the form he had been using since he was old enough to resent being corrected by Masters twice his age. Slow first. No blade. Let the body remember before the mind started getting clever.

Left foot forward. Hands up. Shift. Turn.

The first sequence came clean. So did the second.

On the third, the lag slipped in again.

Not much. Just enough to snag the rhythm at the edge of his attention. Enough to make his next transition land a fraction after it should have, his body arriving where it was supposed to be and the rest of him following like he had taken the wrong turn in a familiar corridor.

Ben stopped and exhaled through his teeth.

All right. There it was. Not fatigue. Not plain distraction. Not a bad night, no matter how much he would have preferred one of those. The wrongness was still there, neat and awful, sitting inside the room with him and waiting for him to stop pretending not to notice.

He started again.

Step. Pivot. Drop. Recover.

The motion itself was fine. Better than fine. His body remembered every angle, every transfer of weight, every measure of pressure through his feet. But when he reached for the next beat in the Force, it came back late, just off the mark, like a partner missing the count by the width of a breath.

Ben came out of the sequence and stood very still.

There it was again. Clean enough to make him angry. Not because the sensation was hard to notice. Because it was easy.

His own reflexes had spent a week tripping over something that should not have been there, and he had been telling himself it was exhaustion. As though the Force had decided to get sloppy because he had had a few rough nights.

Very comforting. Very knightly. Very useful.

He rubbed a hand over his face, then let it drop. The salle’s silence had taken on a weight of its own, broken only by the faint hum of the building around him and the distant traffic in the corridor. The Temple never really slept. It just changed shifts.

Ben paced once across the mat, turned, and ran the form again.

The lag hit at the same point.

A note played just flat enough to make the whole melody feel wrong. Not a crash. Not a rupture. A hitch. A drag under the timing of his muscles and the pull of his senses, as if the floor itself had decided to answer a fraction behind his footfall.

He stopped on the edge of the mat and stared at nothing in particular.

No. Not nothing. The problem had a shape now, even if he did not like the shape. It was in the room with him. In the field. In the rhythm he had trusted since he was a kid and thought growing up meant the galaxy would stop moving out from under him for five minutes.

Ben gave a short, humorless breath that almost counted as a laugh. “Excellent,” he muttered to the empty salle. “I am being sabotaged by a half-beat.”

The words sounded ridiculous out loud. That helped. A little.

He turned back toward the center and kept going.

This time he stripped it down farther. No flourish. No force of habit. Just the skeleton of the form, step by step, hands and hips and balance. He let the movement get small enough that he could listen to it.

Still there.

Still there.

Not in his muscles. Not in the floor. In the space between what he expected and what answered. The gap was tiny. Infuriating. Repeatedly expensive.

Ben came out of the last turn and shut his eyes for one second, then opened them again before he could get sentimental about it. If the wrongness was in him, then he was out of alignment in a way that had already cost him a shoulder and a sparring match and probably his dignity if Rendell was the sort to gossip.

If it was not in him—

He stopped that thought before it could grow teeth.

No. Not yet. He would not build a theory off one ugly night and a week of irritation. He was not that reckless. He was merely annoyed enough to keep testing until the annoyance gave him something useful.

He crossed the mat again, this time with the hilt still clipped at his side, and ran the form without igniting it. The motions felt stripped down and almost honest. Boots on the floor. Breath in his lungs. Weight shifting. The body knowing before the mind had time to dress it up.

The lag came.

Ben’s chest tightened on instinct, not with pain, but with the hard little shock of a thing refusing to be explained away any longer. His focus sharpened so fast it nearly hurt. The wrongness was not a wobble in him. It was not nerves. Not the residue of a bad week. It sat out there in the flow around him and spoiled the timing of everything it touched.

He opened his eyes on the last step and stood there with his fists half-clenched.

There. That was the part he had not wanted to say even in his head. Not because it made him sound unstable. Because it made him sound like he was seeing patterns where none existed, and there were enough people in the Temple who would happily file that under exhaustion, stress, grief, whatever convenient label kept the day moving.

Ben rubbed the heel of his hand against his sternum once, hard enough to make himself pay attention to the sensation instead of the implications.

Whatever this was, it was not fatigue.

It was not him.

That thought should have been a relief. It was, a little. Enough to ease the edge off the frustration and replace it with something colder and less comfortable. If the problem was outside him, then he had not failed. If the problem was inside him, then he had.

He had no idea which answer he wanted more.

The corridor chime sounded again, faint through the salle doors. Footsteps passed, then faded. Someone laughed far off, the sound muffled by the Temple’s thick walls. Ordinary life. Other people. The galaxy continuing to revolve while he stood in a practice room trying to pin down a beat that should have stayed obedient.

Ben looked down at his hands, then flexed them once.

All right. Fine. So it was real. That did not make it useful. He could live with real. He had lived with worse. He just did not like having it happen where another Knight could see him get tagged because his own senses were running a fraction behind.

He was still deciding whether to be irritated or concerned when the door slid open.

Luke Skywalker stood in the doorway with a datapad in one hand and the particular kind of stillness Ben had learned to associate with an incoming problem. Not the prelude to panic. The prelude to work.

Ben straightened before he meant to.
