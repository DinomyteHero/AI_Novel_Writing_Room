Ben’s guard dropped a half-second late.

The training blade caught him across the shoulder. Not hard enough to bruise — Rendell had pulled the strike — but the contact was a clean hit. A sharp, electric sting that Ben’s reflexes should not have allowed.

He stepped back, deactivating his own training saber. The hum died in his hand. The salle’s cool air settled against his sweat-damp tunic.

“Again?” Rendell asked, lowering his blade.

Ben shook his head. He kept his voice light, the practiced Skywalker deflection. “I’m done. That’s three for the week.”

“Fatigue,” Rendell said, not quite a question.

“Something like that.” Ben thumbed sweat from his eyebrow. The wrongness tugged at the edge of his attention, a half-beat lag in the ambient field. Like a note played slightly flat. He’d felt it all week — a subtle drag on his timing, a hesitation where his body knew the move before his mind did. Now it had cost him a sparring exchange he shouldn’t have lost.

Rendell studied him a moment, then nodded. “Get some rest, Skywalker.”

He turned toward the equipment locker, his boots echoing on the polished floor. The salle was quiet this late, the only other sound the distant hum of a turbolift in the Temple corridors beyond. A cleaning droid whirred past the open doorway, its brushes whispering against the stone.

Ben watched Rendell go. The isolation settled over him, clean and sharp. Rendell didn’t feel it. None of the other Knights did. The Order had been treating the background disturbance as residue from the Abeloth crisis, a lingering atmospheric bleed. Ben had been treating it as a private problem. Now it was a private problem that had cost him in front of another Knight.

He ran a hand through his hair. The sting on his shoulder was fading, but the failure lingered. Not pain — the specific ache of a mistake his body shouldn’t have made. His instinct pushed toward action: rematch, push harder, prove it was a fluke. But the wrongness was still there, pulling at his attention. A missing handrail. Bad footing.

He paced the empty salle. The air smelled of ozone and polished durasteel, the familiar scent of a thousand training sessions. Through the tall transparisteel windows, Coruscant’s night sky glowed with orbital traffic and city lights. The Temple felt vast around him, a living structure of late-shift foot traffic, murmured conversations in distant halls, the quiet pulse of a place that never truly slept.

Ben stopped at the center of the floor. He ignited his training blade again, felt the familiar vibration in his palm, then shut it off. He didn’t need the light. He knew the forms by muscle memory, by the rhythm his body had carried since he was a boy.

He began the sequence slowly. Shii-Cho, basic opening stance. Step, pivot, blade arc — except there was no blade. Just his empty hands tracing the patterns, his feet finding the positions on the worn floor. He moved through the first three movements, his breath steady, his focus narrowing to the space between intention and action.

The wrongness stole the fourth beat.

It wasn’t in his muscles. His body moved with precision, each shift balanced, each turn exact. It was in the ambient Force field, arriving a half-second behind the rhythm he knew by reflex. Like trying to dance to music that kept skipping. The lag was subtle, barely there — but he felt it. A hesitation in the flow, a fractional delay between his movement and the field’s response.

He completed the form, his hands falling to his sides. The realization landed not as a thought, but as a physical sensation: a tightening in his chest, a sharpening of focus. This wasn’t fatigue. This wasn’t distraction. Something was structurally wrong with the Force itself, not with him.

He didn’t have words for it. He couldn’t articulate what the wrongness was, only what it did — it pulled, it lagged, it cost him beats he couldn’t spare. If it was external, he wasn’t failing. If it was internal — trauma, lingering Abeloth-bleed, some psychological fracture — then he was. The uncertainty settled deep, a cold weight beneath his ribs.

He ran the form again, faster this time. The lag was still there, a consistent half-beat delay. He tried a different sequence, then another. Each time, the same slight hesitation, the same note played flat. He stopped, breathing harder now, sweat cooling on his skin.

Frustration sharpened into something closer to dread. He didn’t name it. He just stood there in the empty salle, listening to the distant hum of Temple life, feeling the wrongness like a missing tooth in the universe’s smile.

A shadow fell across the doorway.

Ben turned.

Luke stood there, a datapad in one hand, his expression the particular calm Ben knew meant a mission. He wore his simple Jedi tunic, his face lined with years Ben was only beginning to understand. He didn’t speak. He just waited, his presence steady in the Force — vast and warm and utterly unaware of the lag.

Ben felt a surge of reluctant hope. Relief that he might not be alone with this, mixed with the cost of admitting it aloud. He braced for the handoff, his body already shifting from the form into readiness.
