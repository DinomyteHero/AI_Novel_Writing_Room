Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation, and Ben watched her fingers find the activation sequence from muscle memory—an old pattern, a thousand years old, and still quicker than his modern training.

The console hummed awake under her touch. Holographic displays rose in a register Ben had never seen: archaic Basic, the characters formal and a little off, the syntax carrying the weight of pre-Reformation administrative protocol. The archive air shifted with them, the fortress’s recycled tang turning sharper on his tongue.

Ben’s chest tightened. He didn’t reach for the Force. Not yet. That was the last thing he wanted under his skin.

Desh shifted beside him, his silence the efficient kind—watching, measuring, waiting for something useful. The Codex fragment sat in its sealed case on the console’s edge, faintly lit, still unopened.

Torin moved next. His hand found the display controls with the same ancient ease, and he drew an index into the holographic field. Veranthos unfolded in columns of text—containment terms, anchor designations, structural specifications—and Torin gave a breath that might almost have been a laugh.

“Gavran’s hand,” he said. “You can tell by the way he indexed the anchor bindings. Always the engineer. Even the penance had a schematic.”

The word penance hit Ben low and hard. Torin wasn’t explaining the later Reformations. He was remembering Veranthos—the place, the people, the choices made before the Ruusan aftermath rewrote everything. That difference mattered. Ben could feel it mattering before he could name why.

“Gavran,” Sera said, her voice all century-old discipline, “was not the architect of what came after. He was the keeper of what was buried.”

Kael reached past her.

The movement was so quiet, so spare, Ben nearly missed it. Kael’s hand slid across the display, and one designation changed—one word replaced by another. Architect became custodian. He didn’t explain. He didn’t look up. He simply made the correction and stepped back, his gaze settling on the amended text with something that might have been satisfaction, or grief, or both.

Ben caught the weight of it before he fully processed the words. Kael had broken his silence for this—his first voluntary contribution since the survivors woke. The monk’s brevity landed harder than any explanation would have. The survivors were not one voice. They were three separate people, each standing in a different place inside the same truth.

That separation was the truth.

“The Veranthos protocol,” Sera said, and her voice never wavered, “was private penance and containment. It predates the public settlement. The settlement was built to carry the wound.”

She did not call it the Reformations.

Ben did.

“The Ruusan Reformations,” he said, the words out before he could stop them. “The public demilitarization. The end of the Jedi Lords. Centralized training, Republic oversight. All of it real. All of it true. And underneath it—”

He stopped. The connection snapped into place before his head could catch up. His body took the blow first: a drop in the center of gravity, a sharp, cold clarity that said one of the histories Luke’s Order had handed down was missing exactly the part Ben now needed.

The public settlement after Ruusan had dissolved the Army of Light. It had ended Jedi Lord militarization. It had centralized training and bound the Order more closely to Republic and Judicial oversight. All of that was real. All of that was true.

And beneath it, Gavran’s concealed protocol—penance and containment—had been buried so thoroughly that a thousand years of Jedi history had never named it.

Ben’s mind raced ahead to the implications. If the private protocol was penance, what was it for? What had the old Order decided to hide?

The question settled into his shoulders. He didn’t ask it aloud. The room didn’t try to answer.

Sera’s face told him she knew the question was there. She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t dress the fact up with context or apology. The sentence had landed clean and complete, and she let it stand.

Torin’s hand still rested on the console, fingers brushing the edge of the display with a familiarity that made Ben’s skin prickle before his mind could catch up. The warmth in Torin’s posture was still there—the storyteller’s ease, the fatherly affection—but now Ben could see something else under it. A man touching the framework of his own waking. A man who had helped build the thing that held him.

Kael hadn’t moved. His gaze stayed on the corrected designation. Custodian. One word. A thousand years of silence broken for a correction that preserved a distinction most people would never know existed.

Ben looked at the three of them—Sera with her precision, Torin with his warm thread of story, Kael with his dry, quiet correction—and understood that the archive around them was no longer just a room full of records. It was testimony. Three people who had waited a thousand years to say what the Order had chosen to bury.

The weight settled in him. Not despair. Clarity.

He stood in a room with three witnesses to the Order’s hidden history, and the part of Jedi history he most needed to trust had just shifted under his feet.

Sera named the Veranthos protocol as containment and penance. Ben supplied the word Reformations himself. Sera did not say what the penance was for. She did not need to.

Ben could read the rest of it off her face.
