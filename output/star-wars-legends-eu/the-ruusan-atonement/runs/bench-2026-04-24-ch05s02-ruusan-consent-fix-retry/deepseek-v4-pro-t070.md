Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation, and Ben watched her fingers find the activation sequence from muscle memory — a thousand years old and still faster than his modern training.

The console hummed to life under her touch. Holographic displays flickered up in a register Ben had never seen: archaic Basic, the characters formal and slightly wrong, the syntax carrying the weight of pre-Reformation administrative protocol. The air in the archive changed, the recycled tang of the fortress suddenly sharper, more present.

Ben's chest tightened. He didn't reach for the Force. Not yet. The Force was the problem.

Desh shifted beside him, his silence the operational kind — the kind that meant he was reading the room and waiting for something he could use. The Codex fragment rested in its sealed case on the console's edge, faintly lit, unopened.

Torin moved next. His hand found the display controls with the same thousand-year-old familiarity, pulling an index into the holographic field. The Veranthos protocol unfolded in columns of text — containment terms, anchor designations, structural specifications — and Torin let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"Gavran's hand," he said. "You can tell by the way he indexed the anchor bindings. Always the engineer. Even the penance had a schematic."

The word *penance* landed in Ben's stomach like a stone. Torin wasn't explaining the later Reformations. He was remembering Veranthos — the place, the people, the choices made before the Ruusan aftermath rewrote everything. The distinction mattered. Ben could feel it mattering without yet understanding why.

"Gavran," Sera said, her voice carrying its century-of-discipline composure, "was not the architect of what came after. He was the keeper of what was buried."

Kael reached past her.

The motion was so quiet, so unassuming, that Ben almost missed it. Kael's hand moved across the display, and one designation shifted — a single word replaced. *Architect* became *custodian*. He did not explain the correction. He did not look at anyone. He simply made it and stepped back, his gaze settling on the amended text with something that might have been satisfaction or might have been grief.

Ben registered the significance before he processed the content. Kael had broken his silence for this — his first voluntary contribution since the survivors woke. The monk's brevity landed heavier than any explanation. The survivors were not unified in their testimony. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

"The Veranthos protocol," Sera said, and her voice did not waver, "was private penance and containment. It predates the public settlement. It was the wound the settlement was built to carry."

She did not call it the Reformations.

Ben did.

"The Ruusan Reformations," he said, and the word came out before he could stop it. "The public demilitarization. The end of the Jedi Lords. Centralized training, Republic oversight. All of it real. All of it true. And underneath it — "

He stopped. The connection landed before his intellect caught up. His body registered the impact: a vertigo-like drop in his center of gravity, a sudden clarity that came with the understanding that one of the histories Luke's Order had inherited was incomplete exactly where Ben now needed it whole.

The public settlement after Ruusan had dissolved the Army of Light. It had ended Jedi Lord militarization. It had centralized training and bound the Order more closely to Republic and Judicial oversight. All of that was real. All of that was true.

And beneath it, Gavran's concealed protocol — penance and containment — had been buried so thoroughly that a thousand years of Jedi history had never named it.

Ben's mind raced ahead to the implications. If the private protocol was penance, what was it penance for? What had the old Order chosen to bury?

The weight of the question settled into his shoulders. He did not ask it aloud. The room did not try to answer.

Sera's face told him she knew the question was there. She did not elaborate. She did not soften the institutional fact with context or apology. The sentence had landed clean and complete, and she let it stand.

Torin's hand still rested on the console, his fingers brushing the edge of the display with a familiarity that made Ben's skin register wrong before his mind could name why. The warmth in Torin's posture was still there — the storyteller's ease, the paternal affection — but Ben could see now that it was also something else. A man touching the architecture of his own waking. A man who had helped build the thing that held him.

Kael had not moved from where he stood, his gaze still on the amended designation. *Custodian*. One word. A thousand years of silence broken for a correction that preserved a distinction most people would never know existed.

Ben looked at the three of them — Sera with her composed precision, Torin with his narrative warmth, Kael with his quiet correction — and understood that the archive around them was no longer a room full of records. It was testimony. It was three people who had waited a thousand years to say what the Order had chosen to bury.

The weight settled. Not as despair. As clarity.

He was standing in a room with three witnesses to the Order's hidden history, and the part of Jedi history he most needed to trust had just changed.

Sera named the Veranthos protocol as containment and penance. Ben supplied the word Reformations himself. Sera did not say what the penance was for. She did not need to.

Ben could read the rest of it off her face.
