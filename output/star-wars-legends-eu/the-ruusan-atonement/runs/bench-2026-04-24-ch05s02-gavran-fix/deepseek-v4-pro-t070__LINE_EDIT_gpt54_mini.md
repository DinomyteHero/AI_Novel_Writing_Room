Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation, her fingers finding the activation sequence before Ben had finished mapping the room’s exits.

The console came alive under her touch with a low, clean hum that shivered through the deck plating under his boots. Holographic light climbed in pale blue sheets, resolving into glyphs Ben had spent twenty minutes failing to read. Sera moved through the interface like she was unsealing a familiar door. A thousand years old, and her hands were still faster than his modern training.

Ben’s chest tightened. He didn’t give the feeling a name. He held himself still and let his peripheral vision sharpen: the precise line of Sera’s shoulders, the way her left hand stayed braced on the console’s edge while her right worked, the sealed case holding the Codex fragment on a shelf three meters away—unopened, faintly luminous in the archive’s dim light.

“She knows the system,” Desh said quietly from Ben’s right.

“She built it,” Ben said.

The words came out flat. He heard them land and left them that way.

Torin stepped forward before the silence could settle. His hand found a secondary control surface—older than the main console, recessed into the wall like an afterthought—and a second display bloomed above the first. An institutional index, rendered in the same archaic register Ben had been fighting all morning. Dates. Designations. The careful language of public record.

“The Reformations,” Torin said. His voice carried the warmth of someone telling a story he’d told before, to people who had needed to hear it. “Demilitarization of the Army of Light. End of the Jedi Lords as a formal institution. Centralized training on Coruscant, under Republic oversight. The settlement everyone knows.”

The timeline scrolled under his touch—not the slow, halting movement of someone learning the system, but the easy rhythm of memory. He wasn’t searching. He was finding.

Ben watched Torin’s hand move and felt the vertigo start. Not the abstract wrongness he’d been tracking since they docked. Something sharper. The public timeline was accurate. He’d learned it from Luke’s Order, from the same histories every Jedi trainee absorbed in their first years of study. The dates matched. The terminology was correct.

But the private index running beneath it—the one Torin had brought up without breaking stride—changed what the timeline was for.

“You knew where to look,” Ben said.

Torin glanced at him. The warmth in his expression didn’t budge. “I helped write the index.”

Ben’s breathing steadied. His mind took the problem the way it took a combat drill—checking gaps, measuring what didn’t fit. The history he knew was true. The history they were showing him was also true. The space between them was where the answer lived.

Kael moved.

It was a small motion—a half-step forward, his hand reaching past Sera’s shoulder to touch one designation on the display. The glyph pulsed under his fingertip.

“Not architect,” Kael said.

His voice was dry, unhurried. He didn’t look at Sera. He didn’t look at Ben. He corrected the record and withdrew his hand, stepping back into the half-shadow at the edge of the console’s glow.

“Keeper,” he said. “Custodian of the Veranthos protocol. Gavran didn’t author the Reformations. He built the cage underneath them.”

The silence that followed had weight.

Ben caught the significance of the correction before he fully processed its content. Kael had spoken—his first voluntary contribution since the survivors woke, and he’d used it to protect a distinction that mattered to him. The monk’s brevity landed harder than explanation would have. Four sentences, and Ben understood something he hadn’t understood thirty seconds ago: the survivors were not one voice. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

Sera lifted her hand from the console. The motion drew Ben’s eye the way a blade being set down does—not because it was threatening, but because it was deliberate.

“The Ruusan Reformations served two purposes,” she said.

Her voice was composed, precise, each word set where it belonged. No contraction. No soft edges. She spoke like an archivist reading a finding she had spent a century preparing to deliver.

“The public reforms dissolved the Army of Light, ended Jedi Lord militarization, centralized Jedi training, and bound the Order to Republic oversight. That settlement was real. It was necessary. It was not the whole of what was agreed.”

She paused. Ben felt the pause in his spine.

“Beneath the public settlement,” Sera said, “Gavran constructed a private penance-and-containment protocol. The Veranthos seal. Twelve chamber-bound anchors, and himself as the central keystone. Thirteen lives to bind a wound the Reformations could not name.”

The sentence landed.

Ben felt it before his intellect caught up—a sudden drop in his center of gravity, the hard clarity that comes with realizing one of the histories Luke’s Order had inherited was incomplete exactly where Ben now needed it whole. His mind raced ahead. If the private protocol was penance, what was it penance for? What had the old Order chosen to bury beneath a thousand years of legitimate reform?

The question settled into his shoulders. He didn’t shake it off.

Sera did not elaborate. She did not say what the penance was for. She held her composure like a door kept closed, and Ben could read the rest of it off her face—the thing she wasn’t saying, the cost she had already counted and wasn’t offering to share.

Desh let out a slow breath. It was the only sound in the room.

Ben looked at the three of them—Sera at the console, Torin beside the secondary display, Kael in the half-shadow at the edge of the light—and saw what each had become to him.

Sera: the instrument of precision. The one who would name the thing, clean and complete, and let it stand without apology.

Torin: the keeper of narrative warmth. The one who remembered the index because he had helped write it, and who told the story like someone who had told it before.

Kael: the quiet correction. The one who had broken his silence to protect a distinction, then stepped back into shadow the moment the distinction was made.

The archive around them was no longer a room full of records. It was a testimony delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it.

Ben didn’t speak. There was nothing to say that wouldn’t be the wrong thing—a question he wasn’t ready to ask, an answer he wasn’t ready to hear. The post-Ruusan settlement had carried a concealed wound beneath its legitimate public reforms, and the three people standing in this room had been part of the concealment.

Now they were part of the telling.

He held himself ready for the next moment. Not in despair. In the hard clarity that comes when the history you need most turns out to have been edited at the source, and the people who did the editing are standing in the room with you, waiting to see what he would do.
