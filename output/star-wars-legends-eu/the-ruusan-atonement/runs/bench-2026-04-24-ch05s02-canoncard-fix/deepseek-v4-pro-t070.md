Sera crossed to the archive console without hesitation.

Ben watched her fingers find the activation sequence from muscle memory—a thousand years old and still faster than his modern training. The console hummed to life under her touch, holoscreens flickering through startup protocols in a register he didn't recognize. Archaic Basic, the characters formal and slightly wrong in their proportions, the kind of script that belonged in a museum archive rather than under someone's fingertips.

His chest tightened. The sensation wasn't the Force—it was simpler than that, a physical response to watching someone walk through a door she'd built herself and never expected to see again.

Desh stood near the entrance, arms crossed, watching Sera with the flat assessment of a man who'd learned to read people without the Force's help. He said nothing. The silence was the right call.

The first display resolved into an institutional timeline—dates, designations, the skeletal architecture of the post-Ruusan settlement. Ben recognized the shape of it. Public demilitarization. The dissolution of the Army of Light. Centralized training under a reformed Council. Republic oversight codified into law. The Reformations as every Jedi youngling learned them.

Torin stepped forward and pulled a secondary index into the display, his hand moving with the same thousand-year-old familiarity Sera had shown. The motion was casual, almost absent, the way a man might reach for a light switch in a room he'd lived in for decades.

"They got the dates right," he said. His voice carried something Ben couldn't name at first—not quite amusement, not quite grief. The tone of someone reading an obituary that had gotten the facts correct but missed the person entirely. "Republic Senate ratified the core provisions inside of six months. Remarkable, really. The negotiations took longer than the war."

Ben's focus tightened. The public timeline was what he'd been taught. The private index running beneath it—the one Torin had pulled up without asking permission, without seeming to think about it at all—was something else. A parallel record, cross-referenced against the public settlement but carrying entries the public record had never seen.

His breathing steadied as his mind engaged the problem. The gap between the two records wasn't an error. It was structural.

Kael moved.

Ben almost missed it—the man had been so still, so silent since they'd entered the archive, that he'd faded into the background of Ben's awareness. Now he reached past Sera, one finger touching a line of text on the secondary display. He replaced a single word. The correction was precise, unemphasized, the kind of edit a copyist might make without thinking.

He stepped back.

"Keeper," he said. "Not architect."

Sera glanced at the correction and nodded once. She did not thank him. The exchange had the feel of something that had happened before, in other rooms, a thousand years ago.

Ben registered the significance of the action before he processed the content of the correction. Kael had broken his silence for a single word. Not for an explanation. Not for a story. For a distinction that mattered to him.

The survivors were not unified in their testimony. They were three separate people with three separate relationships to the truth.

That separation was the truth.

Torin was still scrolling through the private index, his expression unreadable. "Gavran was the custodian of the Veranthos protocol. Not the public settlement's architect—that was the Council's work, and the Senate's. But the layer beneath it." He paused, his hand resting on the console's edge with an ease that made Ben's skin register wrong before his mind caught up. "He built the containment framework. Designed the seal geometry. Recruited the anchors." A beat. "Recruited us."

The warmth in his voice had thinned. Not gone—Torin's natural register seemed to be warmth, the way some people's natural register was sarcasm or silence—but stretched, attenuated, the way a recording degraded over too many playbacks.

Sera straightened. Her century-of-discipline composure held even now, even here, in a room full of records she had helped write.

"The Reformations were two things," she said. "Public reform and private penance. The public settlement was real—the demilitarization, the end of the Jedi Lords, the centralization of training, Republic oversight. All of it happened. All of it was necessary." She did not look at Ben when she said it. She was reading the display, or perhaps reading something behind it. "The private protocol was containment. A seal built at Veranthos, anchored by thirteen volunteers, designed to hold something the Order could not afford to name in the public record."

She did not elaborate. She did not soften the institutional fact with context or apology.

The sentence landed clean and complete.

Ben felt it before his intellect caught up. A vertigo-like drop in his center of gravity, the sudden clarity that came with understanding that one of the histories Luke's Order had inherited was incomplete exactly where Ben now needed it whole. The public settlement after Ruusan remained real. The concealed Veranthos layer beneath it was the wound the records had been built to hide.

His mind raced ahead. If the private protocol was penance, what was it penance for? What had the old Order chosen to bury?

The moral weight of the question settled into his shoulders. He did not ask it aloud. The room was not ready to answer, and he was not ready to hear it.

He looked at the three survivors—Sera still standing at the console, Torin's hand still resting on its edge, Kael still withdrawn into his monk's silence—and understood what they had just become for him.

Sera was the instrument. Precision, archival exactness, the century-of-discipline composure that delivered the truth without softening it because softening it would be a lie.

Torin was the keeper. Narrative warmth, the story-shaped inflection that made the institutional facts bearable without making them false. The man who remembered what the records meant, not just what they said.

Kael was the correction. The single word that mattered. The silence that had been broken only for a distinction worth making. The quiet observation that landed heavier than any explanation.

The archive around them was no longer a room full of records. It was a testimony delivered by three people who had waited a thousand years to say it.

Ben braced for what came next. The survivors' testimony had changed the part of Jedi history he most needed to trust, and he did not yet know what the penance was for.

Sera's face told him enough. She did not say it.

She did not need to.
