Luke's office smelled of old flimsiplast and recycled air, the same as it always had. The desk was a controlled accumulation — datapads in three stacks, a holocomm unit with a blinking standby indicator, a cup of something that had probably been caf an hour ago. Ben had sat across from that desk more times than he could count, and the room had always felt like an extension of the man: organized under apparent disorder, warm without announcing it.

His father slid the datapad across without preamble.

"Seventeen reports," Luke said. "Eight Knights, three Masters, six senior Padawans who were asked to log their perceptions as part of post-Abeloth monitoring. All flagged in the last fourteen months. All describing the same thing."

Ben picked up the datapad. The blue glow threw pale light across his fingers as he scrolled.

The reports were formatted differently — each Knight had their own habits — but the language converged. *Absent signal rather than corrupted signal.* That was Pellan's phrasing, which he recognized. Others had written *structural gap* or *tonal inconsistency* or, from one of the Masters, a blunt *something is wrong with the Force in this sector and I cannot be more specific than that.* Ben stopped on that one for a moment, then kept scrolling.

Three of the readings matched. Not in language — in the underlying data, the mapped coordinates, the bearing. He zoomed in.

"These three." He turned the datapad to show Luke the highlighted entries. "Draven's report from fourteen months ago. Pellan's from eight. And this one from Knight Arev, six weeks back." He set the datapad flat on the desk and tapped the coordinates. "Same bearing. Outer Rim, running along the Hydian corridor. Consistent position drift over time, which means it's not a fixed anomaly — it's moving, or growing, or both."

Luke was watching him with the particular quality of attention Ben had learned to read early: not passive, not evaluating. Listening for something specific.

"You've been tracking it," Luke said.

"Six weeks." No point in qualifying it. "My timing went off in sparring. I tracked the disruption upstream and found the source wasn't in me."

"I know." Luke leaned back in his chair. "I've felt it too."

Ben kept his expression neutral. *Of course you have.* His father's reach in the Force was something Ben had stopped trying to map when he was about fifteen — it was like trying to measure the depth of an ocean by dropping a string. Whatever Ben had felt as a faint sourness under the Temple's harmonics, Luke had almost certainly felt weeks before Ben noticed anything at all.

"How are you reading it?" Ben asked.

Luke was quiet for a moment. "Degradation. Old damage surfacing — the way a fault line doesn't announce itself until it does. Something buried for a long time, losing whatever was containing it."

"I don't think that's wrong," Ben said. "But I don't think it's the whole picture."

His father's expression didn't change, but something in the quality of his attention shifted. Ben kept going.

"Jacen didn't feel corrupted. Not the way the dark side usually presents — not Palpatine, not the Vong's absence. He felt *off.* Structurally. Like one load-bearing element had been quietly removed and everything else was compensating." He paused, aware he was talking about his cousin to his father, who had loved Jacen. He kept his voice even. "Abeloth was worse. She wasn't dark so much as *different* — the Force around her didn't follow the same rules. And Vestara —"

He stopped himself. That was a longer conversation.

"The point is," Ben said, "I've felt what damage to the Force actually feels like, not just what it looks like in the data. This isn't residue. It's not old trauma surfacing. It's active, and whatever's at the source of it isn't just broken — it's *absent.* Like the signal isn't distorted. It's missing."

Luke looked at him for a long moment. Ben resisted the urge to fill the silence.

"Pellan used that exact phrase," Luke said.

"I know. I read her report." Ben picked up the datapad again and set it back down. "She was right, and it got filed."

The corner of Luke's mouth moved. Not quite a smile. "I'm not filing this one."

He reached across the desk and pulled up a secondary display on the holocomm — a survey route, rendered in green lines across a sector map. The Outer Rim, running the Hydian corridor. The path overlaid almost exactly on the bearing Ben had been tracking.

"Survey mission," Luke said. "Officially a routine anomaly sweep — the Jedi Order does them quarterly along active trade corridors. Cover is genuine; the survey needs doing regardless. But the route runs directly through the three coordinates you flagged." He looked at Ben. "I'd like you to take it."

Ben stood up.

He moved to the window — not to look at anything in particular, just to put a few meters between himself and the desk. Coruscant's night skyline pressed against the transparisteel, a grid of light extending to the horizon. He'd grown up looking at this city. It still didn't feel like his.

The survey route. A solo assignment, officially routine, with real operational cover. His own ship, his own timeline, the space to follow the wrongness to its source without committee review or a senior Knight looking over his shoulder. The Outer Rim, where the Force was thin and the galaxy didn't crowd in on every perception.

He knew what his father was offering. He also knew it was genuine — the mission was real, the need was real, and his read on the anomaly was apparently sharp enough that Luke was treating it as actionable intelligence rather than post-crisis recalibration.

Both things were true at once. That was very like his father.

"The survey protocols require documentation at each waypoint?" Ben asked.

"Standard format. Cilghal wants copies of any anomalous readings logged in real time, not reconstructed after the fact."

"Understood." Ben turned back from the window. "I'll need a week to prep the ship and review the existing survey data."

"The Temple has everything you need. Pellan's raw readings are in the archive, not just her summary report." Luke paused. "Ben."

Ben waited.

"Your read on this — the structural quality you're describing — I want you to trust it. Don't let the official framing of the mission talk you out of what you're actually perceiving."

It was, Ben recognized, a careful thing to say. His father telling him to trust his own judgment, which was also his father telling him he thought Ben's judgment was sound, which was also his father declining to come along and verify it himself. Three things in one sentence. Luke had always been efficient that way.

"I will," Ben said.

Luke nodded once. The briefing was over.

Ben crossed to the desk, picked up the datapad with the survey route, and tucked it under his arm. His father was already reaching for another file — the next item on a Grand Master's evening, the machine of the Order turning without pause.

Neither of them mentioned a second seat on the mission. Ben didn't suggest one. Luke didn't ask why he hadn't.

The door hissed shut behind him, and the office's warmth dropped away, replaced by the Temple's cooler corridor air and the faint, persistent sourness underneath it, patient as ever.
