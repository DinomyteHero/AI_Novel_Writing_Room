The training blade caught him across the shoulder and Ben knew, before the sting registered, that he had no one to blame but himself.

He'd seen the opening. His body had already started the counter. The half-beat lag had taken the rest.

"That's three," his partner said.

Yara Fen said it the way she said most things — flat, observational, without the satisfaction that would have made it easier to dismiss. She was two years his senior in the Order and had never once scored three consecutive hits on him in any session he could remember. She lowered her training saber and looked at him with an expression that was worse than gloating: she was trying to figure out what was wrong.

Ben deactivated his blade. The hum cut out.

"I'm done," he said. "Early night."

"We've got twenty minutes left."

"I know." He clipped the training saber to his belt rather than returning it to the rack — something to do with his hands. "Fatigue. I'll make it up to you."

She held the look for a moment, the kind that said *I don't believe you but I won't push*, and then nodded once and headed for the door. Her boots were quiet on the salle floor. The door slid shut behind her with a soft hiss of recycled air, and Ben was alone under the overhead lights.

He stood there a moment, rotating his shoulder. It didn't hurt much. That wasn't the problem.

The problem was the half-beat lag, still sitting at the back of his skull like a note that had come in late and never quite resolved.

It had started four days ago. Not dramatically — nothing in the Force ever announced itself with drama when it wanted to be inconvenient. He'd been running a warm-up sequence alone in this same salle and the ambient presence of the place had arrived a fraction behind where his instincts expected it. Like reaching for a handhold in the dark and finding it an inch lower than your hand remembered. He'd shaken it off. He'd been tired. The Order had been tired. The post-Abeloth period had left everyone's Force-sense scraped thin, and the senior Knights kept saying the residue was clearing, give it time, the disturbance was settling.

He'd believed them. He'd wanted to believe them.

Ben walked to the center of the salle, where the floor was worn smooth from decades of footwork, and stood still. The Temple breathed around him — distant foot traffic in the corridors, the low mechanical rhythm of a cleaning droid somewhere down the hall, the faint chime of a lift. Normal sounds. Late-shift sounds. The kind of sounds that meant the building was alive and going about its business without him.

He unclipped the training saber and held it unlit at his side.

Then he moved.

The first form was muscle memory so deep it lived below thought, his body tracing Shien's opening sequence without any instruction from his conscious mind. Footwork, weight transfer, the pivot that brought the hilt up into a guard position his hands had found ten thousand times before. That part was fine. His body knew the form the way it knew how to breathe.

The Force was the problem.

Not absent — he wasn't reaching into a void. It was there, present, running through the room the way it always had. But the ambient field of the salle, the subtle orientation that had always arrived with his movement like an echo that anticipated the sound, was lagging. He'd reach into the rhythm and find it half a beat behind. Not enough to see with his eyes. Enough to feel in his gut, in the place where his instincts lived.

He ran the second sequence faster. The lag was still there.

His bare feet pressed into the floor, toes spreading against the worn durasteel. He tried to compensate, adjusting his timing, and that was the part that made his chest tighten — that he was compensating. He'd been compensating all week without naming it, and the compensation was getting harder.

He stopped in the middle of the third sequence, breathing through his nose, and let the unlit hilt hang at his side.

*Fatigue*, he'd told Yara. Which was technically true. He was tired. Every Knight was tired. That wasn't a lie, exactly. It was just the part of the truth that didn't require him to say the rest of it out loud: that the lag was there even when he was rested, that it had been there every morning when he woke up, that it had cost him a half-second in a sparring match he should have won cleanly, and that the Order's explanation — *post-Abeloth residue, settling disturbance, give it time* — had stopped feeling adequate somewhere around day two.

He hadn't said so to anyone. He was a Skywalker. People assumed everything came easy to a Skywalker, and correcting that assumption required a kind of conversation he didn't know how to start. *Actually, my Force-sense has been running late all week and I don't know why* wasn't a sentence that came with a comfortable follow-up.

The salle lights hummed above him. Somewhere in the corridor, the cleaning droid bumped against a wall and reversed.

Ben ran the fourth sequence.

The lag stole the pivot. Not catastrophically — his body saved the form through sheer repetition — but the wrongness was there, threading through the ambient field like a vibration in a hull plate that told you something was loose before anything had actually broken. His hands knew where to go. The Force arrived late to confirm it.

He stopped again. His palm was damp against the hilt.

*Fine*, he thought, with the particular quality of internal resignation that meant the opposite of fine. *It's not fatigue.*

He'd known that since day two. He'd just been waiting to see if it resolved on its own, the way the senior Knights kept saying it would. It hadn't. It was, if anything, getting worse — or he was getting less able to compensate, which amounted to the same thing in a sparring match. Either way, the wrongness was there, present as a second heartbeat, and tonight it had cost him in front of another Knight.

He was still standing there, hilt loose in his hand, when the door opened.

Luke Skywalker stepped into the salle with a datapad under one arm and the expression Ben had been seeing his whole life — the one that meant his father had already assessed the situation and was working backward from a conclusion. He glanced once at Ben's unlit training saber, once at the empty salle, and said nothing about either.

He held up the datapad.
