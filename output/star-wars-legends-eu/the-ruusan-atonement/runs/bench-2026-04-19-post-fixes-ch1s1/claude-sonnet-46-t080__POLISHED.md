The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and the sting of it was almost a relief.

Almost. Ben stepped back, lowering his guard, and the salle's ambient presence arrived a half-beat behind where it should have been — that lag again, like a musician who'd been keeping perfect time for years and had started coming in just late enough to notice. His sparring partner pulled up short, the blue training saber casting pale light across her face.

"That's three," she said. Not unkind. Just accurate.

"Three." He clicked his own blade off. The hum died and left the room quieter than it had any right to be.

She was watching him with the expression Knights got when they were trying to decide whether to say the thing or let it go. She let it go, which meant she'd already made a decision about what she was seeing. Ben had been a Jedi long enough to know what that decision looked like from the outside.

"Rematch?" she offered.

"I'm done." He managed a half-grin that probably didn't reach anywhere useful. "Bad week. I'm going to embarrass myself further in private."

She accepted this the way people accepted things that were technically plausible — nodded, ran a sleeve across her forehead, and crossed toward the bench where her robe was folded. The small sounds of her leaving filled the salle: boot buckles, the soft scrape of the door, the distant chime of a lift somewhere down the corridor. Then nothing.

Ben stood in the middle of the floor and stared at the far wall.

*Bad week.* Sure. That was one way to say it.

He'd been telling himself fatigue since Taungsday. Residue from the Abeloth crisis — that was the Temple's preferred framing, offered gently at every morning briefing like a blanket someone kept trying to press on him. The Force is settling. Background disturbance is expected. Give it time. He'd nodded along with everyone else because the alternative was explaining that what he was experiencing wasn't background noise, it was a gap in the rhythm, and he didn't have the vocabulary for that yet without sounding like a man who'd taken one too many hits to the head.

He walked to the weapons rack and set the training saber in its cradle. His shoulder ached where the blade had connected.

Three times this week. The first two, he'd had excuses good enough to believe. Tonight, his partner had been slower than him on every exchange until the moment she wasn't, and the reason she wasn't had nothing to do with her.

The salle settled around him. The overhead panels had dimmed to the evening setting — amber light, soft enough that the room felt later than it was. Somewhere beyond the corridor wall, a cleaning droid hummed past on its route. Ben listened to it go.

He unclipped his lightsaber from his belt and held it without igniting the blade.

The forms were muscle memory going back to before he could read. He knew Shii-Cho the way he knew his own name — it lived in his wrists, his weight shifts, the angle his back foot set against the floor. He didn't need the Force to run a sequence. That was the point.

He started moving.

The footwork was fine. The transitions were fine. His arms knew exactly where to be at each count and the sequence unfolded clean, no hesitation, nothing wrong. For about six counts it was almost convincing.

Then the lag hit.

Not in his body. That was the thing that made it hard to explain, the thing that would sound like trauma or exhaustion to anyone who hadn't felt it. His body was on time. His body had always been on time. But the ambient presence of the salle — the low-frequency sense of place and Force and the weight of a thousand training sessions absorbed into the floor — arrived just after his motion instead of with it, the way an echo comes back from a wall that's slightly farther than you thought.

He stopped in the middle of the form. Stood there with his unlit lightsaber angled toward the floor.

The lag pulsed once, faint and unmistakable, and then settled back to wherever it went between moments.

His bare feet pressed into the durasteel. He could feel the slight give of the grip pads under his toes, the cool metal at the edges. Real. Present. The salle smelled like recycled air and sweat and the faint metallic residue of training sabers run hot — everything exactly as it should be. The wrongness wasn't in the room. It was in the channel between him and the room, something off in the signal, a skip that happened in the transmission and not the source.

He'd been managing it. That was the honest word for what he'd been doing. Compensating a little here, adjusting his timing there, nothing that would show up in a standard spar until it did. He was good enough at this that managing it had looked, for almost a full week, like being fine.

His father would probably have a thought about that. His father had thoughts about most things that involved Skywalkers and the word *fine*.

Ben exhaled through his nose and let the lightsaber drop to his side. The salle was quiet. The cleaning droid had moved on to whatever corridor came next on its circuit. Down the hall, the late shift was wrapping up — he could feel the foot traffic thinning, the Temple settling into its overnight rhythm.

He'd run the form again. He'd run it until the lag stopped or until he understood it, whichever came first. That was the plan. Very solid plan. Very much the plan of a man who had his situation under control.

Except he'd run it twice more in his head while standing here and the lag was still there, patient as a fault line, and he was starting to understand that he wasn't going to outwork this one. The wrongness had cost him in front of another Knight. It had cost him three times this week. And standing alone in the salle with an unlit blade, he couldn't pretend anymore that what he was experiencing was fatigue or residue or the expected settling of the Force after crisis.

The door opened behind him.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway with a datapad and the expression that meant a mission.
