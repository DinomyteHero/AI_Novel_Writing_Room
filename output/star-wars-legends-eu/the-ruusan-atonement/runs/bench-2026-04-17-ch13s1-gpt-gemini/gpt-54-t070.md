The world filled the forward viewport in calm bands of blue and green.

If Ben had come in blind, he would have called it a good place to disappear for a week. Temperate seas. Broad cloud cover. Two visible continents stitched with river systems and dark forest belts. No obvious scars from bombardment, no ash plumes, no dead gray spread of industrial failure. From orbit, it looked restful.

Desh stood at the sensor pit with one hand braced on the console edge, reading a stream of numbers with the expression of a man being insulted by his own equipment.

“Surface atmosphere is stable,” he said. “Nitrogen-oxygen mix in family range. Pressure acceptable. Thermal bands normal. Settlement grid still there.” He tapped the display. “Chronometric interference across the old node perimeter. Spikes near the central valley. Biosign reads are wrong.”

Torin leaned forward from the copilot station. “Wrong how?”

Desh glanced back. “Vegetation density says thriving. Animal movement says almost none. Avian signatures are all over the place and nowhere useful.”

Ben kept his eyes on the world. “Meaning?”

“Meaning the scans say there are birds,” Desh said, “and I’d bet money there aren’t.”

Kael, strapped in behind him, let one corner of his mouth lift. “Always comforting when the non-mystic starts taking bets against the machine.”

Sera didn’t smile. She stood with both hands clasped behind her back, posture stiff enough to make the cockpit seem smaller. “The node failed completely?”

Desh shook his head once. “No. I’m saying the readings don’t act like damage. They act like physics stopped agreeing with itself in patches.”

That pulled Ben’s attention off the viewport. “That’s not ominous at all.”

Torin snorted. “You’ve inherited your mother’s talent for underreaction.”

Ben let that pass. His focus had narrowed to the seam between what he could see and what the instruments insisted on. Veraine had hit other nodes hard, but hard still left a shape a person could understand. Burnout. Collapse. Distortion around the edges. Cause, then effect.

This world looked healthy.

The contradiction sat under his ribs like bad food.

The *Steady Returns* broke through the upper haze in a slow descent. Sunlight flashed across the sea, then slid away as they banked over a broad inland basin. Ruined roads surfaced through the canopy in straight lines too precise for nature. Roofs showed through the green. Whole streets. A settlement preserved under growth.

Preserved wrong, Desh had called it.

Ben waited for movement below. Herds. Wingbeats. Anything.

Nothing crossed the clearings.

No flocks rose from the trees as the ship came down.

The landing struts touched old duracrete with a soft jolt. The ramp began to lower. Warm air entered the hold carrying wet soil, sap, and the mineral bite of standing water left too long in shade.

Ben unstrapped and rose with the others. “Standard sweep,” he said. “No heroics, no wandering off to contemplate reality alone.”

Kael pushed up from his seat. “You say that now.”

“I mean it now.”

“That’s less reassuring.”

Torin clapped Ben once on the shoulder as they moved for the ramp. “If reality starts contemplating us back, I nominate you to take point.”

“Because I’m a Jedi?”

“Because you volunteered first.”

Sera drew her lightsaber but left it inactive in her hand, ready without making a show of it. “Humor is not preparation.”

“No,” Torin said, still moving, “but it keeps preparation from getting preachy.”

She gave him a level look that might have cut metal. Torin answered with a grin broad enough to make the look useless. Ben would have enjoyed it more if the air outside didn’t already feel wrong against his skin.

He stepped down the ramp into warm daylight and the world missed a beat.

Not sound. Not vision. Something beneath both.

Instinct sent him reaching for the Force before his boots fully settled on the cracked landing pad.

He found it.

And nearly stumbled.

The Force was there in full measure—pressure, depth, endless reach—but his awareness slid through it without catching on anything. No current. No distinction. No familiar living texture. He reached again, harder this time, trying to orient himself the way he always could: life here, danger there, the subtle alignment that told him where to place his feet and how much of himself to trust.

Nothing answered in any language he knew.

Not absence. Not severance.

Light without surfaces.

He stopped at the foot of the ramp, one hand braced on the rail, pulse punching hard in his throat. For a second he had the ugly impression of stepping onto stairs and finding none there.

Torin came down behind him and halted at once. The warmth left his face. “Oh.”

Sera hit the ground next. Her breath caught short, the only outward crack in her control. “By the stars.”

Kael descended last among the Force-sensitives. He stood very still, eyes on nothing Ben could identify. “That’s bad,” he said. “That’s impressively bad.”

Desh came down with his blaster holstered and a compact field kit hanging from one shoulder. He looked from one Jedi to the next. “I’m taking from the chorus that we’ve moved past ordinary trouble.”

Ben swallowed once. “Stay sharp.”

“I was planning to.” Desh scanned the treeline. “You look like the floor moved.”

Ben wished it had been that simple.

Ahead of them, the settlement spread in low structures of stonecrete and weathered alloy half-swallowed by vegetation. Vines covered walls in thick green sheets—except where they didn’t. One building stood with its sunward face bare and clean while purple creepers climbed the shaded side in dense ropes, leaves turned away from daylight as if it burned. White flowers opened under an overhang where no direct sun reached, heavy-headed and waxy. On a shattered fountain near the plaza, moss had grown in a ring that stopped in the exact shape of an old waterline, though the basin was dry.

Ben watched a tendril hanging from a balcony.

It drew upward.

Not fast. Not enough to call movement if he hadn’t been staring straight at it. But it tightened against gravity, curling back toward the rail it had fallen from.

His skin prickled.

“No birds,” Desh said quietly.

Ben listened. Wind moved through leaves. Somewhere water dripped. The ship’s cooling systems ticked behind them.

No calls. No wing rustle. No insect drone. Even the quiet had a strained quality, as if the world were performing silence instead of living in it.

Sera turned in a slow circle, blade hilt clenched white in her fist. “The breach has expanded past the node perimeter.”

Torin’s voice had lost its usual warmth. “This was inhabited.”

“Was,” Kael said. “Nobody stays for this.”

Ben took three steps toward the plaza. Each one felt placed by memory rather than instinct. He knew how to move through danger. He had spent his life training body, mind, and that other layer beneath thought that let a Jedi act a fraction before choice became conscious.

That layer had gone flat.

He knelt near a crack in the pavement where a cluster of red grass pushed through. Half the blades bent toward the sun. Half bent away, sinking back into shadow, roots exposed and feeding in open air that should have dried them to threads. He held out his hand over them and reached again.

Energy flooded his awareness.

Potential. Life. Motion. Matter.

But no handhold. No moral grain. No tilt toward harmony or violation. The Force did not guide. It did not warn. It did not recoil from rot or brighten around growth. It simply was, immense and available, stripped of every contour by which he had known it since childhood.

His stomach turned over.

“Ben,” Torin said.

He straightened too fast and the horizon lurched. “I’m fine.”

Kael made a dry sound that might have been a laugh in better circumstances. “That line’s doing heavy labor today.”

Sera stepped nearer, though not too near. Her discipline held, but he saw the strain in the set of her mouth. “Describe it.”

He looked at her and had no words he trusted. “You know.”

“I need yours.”

Ben glanced across the plaza, at flowers blooming in shade and vines climbing the wrong way and a settlement waiting under broad daylight like a body that had forgotten how to be dead. “The Force is there,” he said. “All of it. But there’s nothing to read. No current. No shape.” He searched for the least-wrong phrasing. “I put my hand out and there’s no rail.”

Torin nodded once. Not agreement. Recognition.

Sera closed her eyes for a brief count, then opened them. “Yes.”

Desh set down his field kit and dug out a chrono the size of his palm. Old habit made Ben watch his hands. Practical work steadied people. Desh always had something to measure, secure, repair, or shoot. It gave the rest of them something solid to lean on.

Desh held the chrono up where they could all see.

The second hand advanced. Ticked. Advanced again. Then snapped ahead two marks. Held. Jumped back one.

Ben stared.

Desh rotated the device, checked it against the display on his wristcomp, then against the ship’s local feed. “That’s not my chrono failing. I’ve got drift across three time sources.”

Kael peered at it. “Comforting.”

“No,” Desh said. “Useful.”

He looked up, and for the first time since landing his calm had a crack in it. Not fear, exactly. Pressure. Something sitting on him from the inside.

“What?” Ben asked.

Desh worked his jaw once, irritated at the question because he hadn’t answered it for himself yet. “This place…” He exhaled through his nose. “It sits on you. Like I’ve lost someone and I can’t tell you who. Not memory. Just the hit after.”

Ben looked at him hard.

Desh tapped his own chest with two fingers. “I don’t do your thing, Jedi. This isn’t me borrowing your nerves. It’s in the air.”

Nobody spoke for a beat.

That was the moment the scale of it landed cleanly. Veraine hadn’t built a weapon against Jedi. She’d tested a break in the rules everyone lived inside, whether they knew it or not. If this could spread over a settlement world, then Veranthos wasn’t a target in the military sense. It was a proving ground with a population count.

Ben turned slowly, taking in the empty streets, the open doors, the vine-choked facades. “Field test,” he said.

Torin’s face hardened. “Yes.”

Sera’s reply came flat and cold. “She wanted to observe persistence under full breach conditions.”

Kael folded his arms. “And she got her data.”

Ben pictured Veraine walking these streets, seeing all this and taking notes.

His hand closed into a fist.

He let it go at once. Anger usually met resistance somewhere. A warning pressure. A familiar inward check. Not a command, not a leash—just a living awareness that reminded him where the edge lay before he stepped across it.

He reached for calm instead, for breath and posture and the forms drilled into him since he could stand in a training salle. Empty the noise. Let the Force move through you. Trust the current. Surrender the self that insists on steering.

The technique broke apart on contact.

There was nothing to surrender into. No answering presence. No quiet deepening beneath thought. His breathing steadied because he made it steady. His pulse slowed because his body obeyed training. That was all. The center he’d spent his life returning to had no echo here.

He had never noticed how often the Force met him halfway.

Ben opened his eyes. His hand had drifted toward his lightsaber without conscious choice. He pulled it back and curled his fingers hard enough to hurt.

“Ben?” Sera said.

“Don’t,” he said, sharper than he intended.

Silence dropped between them. Even Torin didn’t try to smooth it over.

He stared at the plaza stones. Fine roots had worked between the seams and lifted them into shallow ridges. One slab rocked under a breeze that should not have moved anything so heavy. Across the square, a strand of vine resumed its slow upward draw.

His thoughts had edges on them now.

Not wild. Not foreign. Worse than that. Familiar.

Torin is compromised.
Sera will hold to principle until it kills someone.
Kael will run if the angle turns bad.
Desh should never have come here.
Veraine needs to die before she touches another node.

Each judgment arrived clean, bare, without the usual softening pressure that asked for patience, context, mercy. Ben knew those thoughts were possible in him. Everybody had hard thoughts. Passing ones. Ugly ones. The difference was that he had never stood so close to them without the Force laying a hand on the scale.

He looked up at the ruined settlement under the bright sky, and the world offered no comfort at all.

Only himself.
