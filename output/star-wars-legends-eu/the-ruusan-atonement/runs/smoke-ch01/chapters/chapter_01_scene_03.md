The hangar bay echoed in the way large empty spaces did when they were trying to remind you how small you were. Ben sat in the cockpit of the *Jade Shadow II* — his ship, inherited name and all — with the repulsors idling and the preflight checklist green across every board. He'd been ready to leave for six minutes.

His datapad sat on his knee, open to the Jedi Knight roster.

Forty-three names. Most of them good people. Several of them genuinely skilled. He scrolled past Maren Sollis, who wrote careful reports and would ask careful questions. Past Tomas Weir, who'd been his sparring partner for two years and who had a habit of reaching for Ben's emotional state in the Force the way some people reached for their coffee first thing in the morning. Past Lira Dinn, who was excellent in the field and who would want to understand the assignment before she committed to it.

He told himself: *non-Force-sensitive makes sense here. The anomaly reads as an absence. Bringing more Force-users might make us more visible to whatever's causing it.*

That was true. It was also not the reason his thumb slid past the roster and opened his personal contacts.

Desh Rolan's entry was near the top — twelve years of being the person Ben called when he needed someone who would show up without a briefing packet. Ben hit the comm icon.

The connection negotiated through two relay stations before the channel opened with a crackle of long-range static. Then: "Rolan."

"It's Ben."

"I know it's Ben." A pause, and the particular quality of Desh's silence shifted — not surprise, not wariness, just a kind of recalibration. "You're calling from the Temple."

"Hangar bay."

"Ship fueled?"

"Preflight complete."

"So you're not calling to catch up." Another pause, shorter. "What's the job?"

Ben had worked with good Jedi who were also good people, and he still sometimes had to remind himself to stop bracing for what came next — the light pressure at the edge of his awareness, someone checking in on how he was doing whether he'd invited it or not. With Desh, there was none of that. Just words. Just listening. Ben's shoulders dropped a quarter-inch.

"Survey run, Outer Rim. Six systems along a specific corridor. Could be nothing. Probably isn't nothing." He watched a Temple maintenance droid trundle past the nose of the ship on the hangar floor below, oblivious. "I need someone who's good in the field and better at not asking questions I can't answer yet."

"Duration?"

"Unknown. Weeks, maybe. I'll cover expenses and standard rate."

"Standard rate." Desh said it the way you repeated something back when you were deciding whether to be offended. "I'm consulting for a Rishi shipping concern right now. Dull work. Mostly watching crates."

"This will be less dull."

"That's not necessarily a selling point." But the tone had already shifted — the same shift Ben had first heard during the GAG's last operational year, when Desh would answer a call at odd hours and ask only the questions that needed answering, because they'd both understood that some operations only worked if you didn't look too hard at the edges. That voice meant Desh already knew what Ben wasn't saying. "Send me the departure window. I'll meet you at Corellia — easier jump from Rishi than doubling back to Coruscant."

"Appreciated."

"Don't thank me yet. You said probably isn't nothing. That's a very specific kind of probably."

"It is," Ben agreed.

Desh made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Rolan out."

The comm cut. Ben sat with the silence for a moment, then set the datapad in the storage pocket beside the seat and reached for the departure request panel.

He filed the clearance under *standard survey mission, Outer Rim corridor, solo departure.* All of it accurate. He hit transmit before he could add anything else.

The Temple's traffic control confirmed in under a minute — fast, because the hangar bay was half-empty at this hour and because a single survey ship departing on a filed route didn't require committee review. Ben brought the repulsors up to full, felt the *Shadow* lift clear of the deck with the smooth solidity of a well-maintained ship, and angled her nose toward the hangar exit.

The bay doors opened on Coruscant's night side, and he flew out into it.

The city spread in every direction below him, the upper-atmosphere traffic lanes already pulling him into their organized flow. He let the autopilot handle the lane insertion and watched the Temple shrink in his aft display — the spires lit against the dark, a dozen lit windows visible from this altitude, people still working or reading or doing whatever Jedi did at this hour in rooms he'd spent his whole life walking through.

He looked at the display for three seconds, then set it to rear-camera off.

Orbital insertion was routine. The planet's curvature filled the viewport and then gave way to the star-scattered dark of open space, Coruscant's light washing across the hull at a shallow angle. Ben ran the navicomputer through the route his father had overlaid on the datapad — spinward approach, first waypoint in the Kessel sector, six systems to survey before he reached the corridor's center.

The wrongness was still there. It had been there since he'd walked out of his father's office, had ridden with him down the turbolift and through the hangar and into the ship. He'd half-expected the vacuum of space to thin it out the way cold air thinned smoke.

It hadn't.

He punched in the jump coordinates. The navicomputer chirped ready.

Ben pulled the hyperspace lever.

The stars stretched and collapsed and the *Shadow* slid sideways into the transit dimension, the familiar blue-white tunnel of hyperspace filling the viewport. His instruments held steady — all green, all nominal, the ship's systems reporting nothing unusual about a standard hyperspace transit.

The wrongness persisted. Sharper than it had been in the hangar. Sharper than it had been in orbit.

Ben went still in the pilot's seat.

He'd transited hyperspace thousands of times. The Force didn't behave the same way in hyperspace — presences muted, distances distorted, the whole thing slightly muffled in a way you stopped noticing after the first hundred jumps. But the wrongness wasn't muffled. It was clearer. More specific. It had, over the thirty seconds since the jump, stopped being a diffuse pressure from no particular direction and started being a pressure from one.

Ahead of him. Down the corridor his father had mapped. Pulling.

Ben stared at the hyperspace tunnel and let the realization settle in his chest without trying to name it. He'd told himself he was going to investigate an anomaly. He'd told himself he was choosing his partner based on operational logic.

The wrongness oriented toward the corridor's center like a needle finding north, patient and certain, as if it had been waiting for him to get moving.

He'd thought he was leaving Coruscant.

He was being collected.
