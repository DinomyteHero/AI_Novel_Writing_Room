The training blade caught him across the shoulder, and Ben stepped back.

Third time this week.

His sparring partner lowered her saber, the blue training beam cycling down to standby flicker. She was good — Knight Maris, two years his junior in rank, fast enough that Ben had to work for every exchange. He'd blocked that exact combination a hundred times. He'd felt it coming. And then, between intention and execution, something had gone slightly wrong in the way a room goes wrong when one instrument in an orchestra is tuned a quarter-step flat — not loud enough to name, just enough to make the whole thing feel off.

His guard had come up half a second late. That was all it took.

"You're telegraphing on the pivot," she said, because she was being generous.

"I know." He deactivated his training saber. The hum died. "I'm done for tonight."

She hesitated, the offer of a rematch visible in her posture, and he waved it off before she could voice it.

"Fatigue," he said. "Call it."

She accepted that. He watched her cross the salle, rack her equipment, and disappear through the corridor door. The sound of her boots faded down the hall. The door sealed with a soft pneumatic click.

Ben stood in the empty room and let out a breath.

The salle was standard Temple construction — high ceilings, durasteel floor panels worn smooth by decades of footwork, practice dummies racked along the far wall like witnesses. The overhead strips had dimmed to evening cycle, washing everything in pale amber. It smelled like recycled air and dried sweat and the faint ionized tang that training sabers left on fabric. Normal. Completely, boringly normal.

He rolled his shoulder where the training blade had connected. There'd be a bruise by morning. Not the shoulder's fault, not Maris's fault, not even really his fault in any technical sense. His mechanics had been fine. His footwork had been fine. The Force-read on her next move had been fine — accurate, even — and then for approximately one-half of one second, the signal had dropped out like a comm cutting through interference, and his body had responded to absence where there should have been presence.

He'd been dealing with it for three weeks.

Ben set his training saber on the rack without igniting it and started pacing the length of the salle. He kept his hands loose at his sides, walking through the first five positions of Shii-Cho from muscle memory, no blade, no Force extension. Just the body. Feet. Hips. Weight transfer. He'd learned this drill from Saba Sebatyne when he was fourteen, when she'd told him that the body remembered what the mind forgot, and that a Jedi who couldn't feel the Force should be able to fight on pure mechanics until the connection returned.

The mechanics were fine. He'd known they would be.

He stopped at the far end of the salle and stood still.

The wrongness was there. It was always there, lately — not constant, not overwhelming, but persistent, like tinnitus you stopped noticing until a quiet room brought it back. He'd grown up learning to listen to the Force the way a musician listened to a chord, feeling for the places where the resonance was clean and the places where it wasn't. He'd spent years around Jacen when Jacen was already gone somewhere dark, and he knew what corruption sounded like. He'd stood in Abeloth's wake and felt the Force scream.

This was neither of those things.

Corruption had a presence — weight, heat, the sense of something deliberately wrong. Abeloth had been noise, overwhelming and total. What he was feeling now was an absence shaped like a presence. A gap in the chord where a note should be. Not removed by violence, not twisted by use — just missing, the way a wall is missing when you reach for it in a familiar dark room and your hand closes on air.

He'd mentioned it to Master Cilghal two weeks ago, framing it as residual sensitivity from the Abeloth crisis. She'd nodded and told him post-crisis perceptual distortion was documented, common, and temporary. She hadn't been dismissive — she'd been thorough, actually, running three diagnostic meditations and finding nothing anomalous in his Force connection. He'd accepted her findings with a straight face and thanked her for her time.

He hadn't mentioned that the gap had gotten larger since then. Marginally, but measurably. He had no way to prove it wasn't him. That was the problem.

Ben walked back to the center of the salle and ran through Makashi's first sequence, still bladeless, letting his attention split between the physical movement and the Force around him. The Temple's background presence was steady — dozens of Knights and Masters in their quarters, in the meditation halls, in the archives. He could feel them the way you felt the hum of a building's systems, not intrusive, just present. That part was clean.

The gap was underneath it. Below the presences, below the normal background texture of a living space full of Force-sensitives, there was something that should have been there and wasn't. He'd been trying to find the right description for it for three weeks and kept coming back to the same inadequate image: a seam. Not a wound, not a tear — a seam, like two panels of material joined slightly wrong, smooth on the surface, but with a ridge you could feel if you ran your fingers over it.

He stopped mid-sequence.

He'd felt seams before. He'd felt them in the aftermath of Centerpoint's destruction, in the deep-space coordinates where Maw Installation had been, in the lingering wrongness around Myrkr that took fifteen years to fade. Places where the Force had been structurally damaged and hadn't fully healed. He'd been young for most of those, and he'd filed the sensation away as a property of specific locations, specific events.

The seam he was feeling now wasn't located. It wasn't coming from beneath the Temple floor or from some distant point in the city. It was directional, but the direction kept shifting — or rather, it was oriented the way north was oriented, as a property of space rather than a fixed address.

Something was wrong with the Force itself.

Not his perception of it. Not trauma residue. Not the lingering echo of Abeloth or Jacen or any of the other disasters he'd lived through. The Force, structurally, in some way he didn't have the vocabulary to describe precisely, was wrong.

He'd been handling that conclusion alone for two weeks because he had no evidence, and because the alternative — telling his father that something was wrong with the Force itself — was the kind of claim that required more than one Knight's subjective perception and a bruised shoulder.

He was still standing in the center of the salle, bladeless, when the door behind him opened.

Ben turned.

Luke stood in the doorway, a datapad in one hand, his posture carrying the particular quality of attention that meant he hadn't just wandered by. He looked at Ben, then at the empty salle, then back at Ben with an expression that didn't require translation.

He had a mission.
