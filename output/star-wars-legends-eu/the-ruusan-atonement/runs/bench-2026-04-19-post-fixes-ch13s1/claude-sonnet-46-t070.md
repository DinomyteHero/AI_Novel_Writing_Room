The planet looked fine.

That was the problem.

Kolven filled the viewport in greens and blues, cloud cover thin and scattered, the kind of temperate world the Outer Rim scattered through its habitable zones like loose change. Ben kept his hands loose on the co-pilot yoke and watched the terminator line slide past while Desh read from the scan display in the tone of voice he used when the numbers didn't make sense but he was going to report them anyway.

"Surface temperature variance across the northern continent is running about eleven degrees warmer than the albedo should allow," Desh said. "Wind patterns are contradicting themselves. The eastern weather system is moving southwest and northwest simultaneously." He paused. "The birds aren't registering."

"Define not registering," Ben said.

"No movement signatures. Not low — none." Desh set the scanner down on the console. "The vegetation density reads as healthy. Something should be eating something."

Through the viewport, Kolven kept looking fine.

Ben reached out, light and habitual, the way he'd done since he was old enough to know what the Force was — a quick orientation check, the kind that told him whether a place was warm or cold in the way that had nothing to do with temperature. He got something back. He always got something back.

He pulled his attention in and said nothing about it.

---

The *Steady Returns* set down in what had been a landing field on the settlement's outskirts. The duracrete was intact. The perimeter fencing had rusted in the standard patterns. Three cargo containers sat stacked near the eastern wall, and the paint on the nearest one had faded but not peeled.

Ben stepped down the ramp first and stood there breathing recycled-air habit for a moment before his lungs adjusted to the outside. The air tasted metallic, faint and persistent, like the back of a credit chip held too long. Nothing in the atmosphere reading explained it.

Sera came down beside him and stopped.

She was looking at the building line twenty meters ahead — a row of low prefab structures, the kind that went up fast on new settlement worlds and stayed up because nobody got around to replacing them. Intact walls, intact rooflines. And along the north-facing sides, where the shadows ran deep and the sun hadn't touched all morning, vines were climbing in dense curtains of green.

The south-facing walls were bare.

"That's not phototropism," Sera said.

"No," Ben agreed.

Torin came off the ramp behind them and stopped at Ben's shoulder. He looked at the vines, at the shadow-climbing green, and didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "The flowers."

Ben looked lower. Along the base of the north wall, in the full shadow, small pale flowers were open. Facing inward, toward the dark.

Kael came off the ramp last, moving without hurry, hands in his jacket pockets. He looked at the flowers the way he looked at most things — like he'd already considered several responses and discarded them.

"Right," he said. "So that's wrong."

Desh pulled his chrono from his breast pocket and held it face-up in his palm. The second hand moved in its steady sweep. Then it didn't. It skipped — a full second, gone, the hand jumping forward past the interval. He watched it happen again. His expression didn't change, but he didn't put the chrono away.

"Time drift," he said. "Measurable. That's new."

Ben walked toward the building line.

The pressure behind his sternum had been there since the ramp, low and steady, not pain exactly — more like the moment before pressure equalization on a fast descent, the body knowing something was wrong with the air before the ears confirmed it. His balance kept registering a drop that wasn't there. The duracrete under his boots was level. He checked twice.

He passed the first building and into the shadow of the gap between structures.

A vine was growing along the wall to his left, moving northwest up the shadowed face. He watched it for a moment. The tip was moving. Not fast — barely perceptible — but he stood still and watched and the tip was higher than it had been. Growing toward the dark, away from the light, in full daylight, in real time.

He kept walking.

The settlement's central square was fifty meters across, the kind of open space that served as market and meeting ground and emergency landing zone all at once. A fountain stood in the center, dry, the basin cracked. More of the wrong-climbing vines had reached the far buildings and were working their way up. A cluster of the pale flowers had taken hold in the shadow of the fountain's base.

Ben stopped in the center of the square.

He reached for the Force.

Not the habitual check he'd done on approach — this was deliberate, both hands, full attention, the way he'd been trained to open perception when a situation required clarity. He reached the way he'd reached ten thousand times, in training sabers and live combat and quiet meditation and the long dark hours of the war, the reach that had always returned something he could use.

The Force answered.

He felt it — energy, presence, the deep hum of something alive and vast and real. It was there. He wasn't cut off. He reached and the Force reached back and—

There was nowhere for the response to go.

No axis. No orientation. No pull toward anything or away from anything. The sense that usually told him where the weight of a situation lay, the quiet signal that had guided every moral choice he'd made since childhood — it wasn't degraded, wasn't distant. It was absent. The Force returned raw potential and nothing else, like a compass in a room where magnetic north had been removed from the concept of direction.

His trained instincts fired. Nothing answered them.

The hollowness hit his arms first — a physical thing, a sudden absence of weight in the bones from elbow to wrist, as if the limbs had been told they were no longer needed for anything that mattered. Then his own thoughts arrived, loud and unfiltered, without the subtle dampening he'd never known was there until it wasn't.

He'd made a decision three weeks ago, on Coruscant, before any of this started. He'd told himself it was tactical. He'd believed it. Now, standing in the center of a dead settlement with the Force returning nothing but noise, he heard himself think about that decision without the quiet softening that had made it bearable, and it was not tactical. It had been convenient. There was a difference, and he had known the difference, and he had chosen not to look at it.

He stood very still.

Behind him, Desh said: "The chrono's skipping every forty seconds now. Regular interval."

Kael's voice was quiet, almost conversational. "Does anyone else have grief they can't account for? I'm asking practically. I don't have grief. I'm not a grief person. But there's something in my chest that has no object."

"Yes," Sera said. Flat, immediate, no hesitation.

Torin said nothing. Ben didn't turn to look at him.

Ben's hands were at his sides. He kept them there, though the instinct was to reach again, to try again, to find some angle of approach that would return the familiar structure. He recognized the instinct for what it was. He'd been recognizing his instincts clearly for the last thirty seconds, without anything between the recognition and the thing recognized.

That was going to be a problem.

He turned back to the group. Desh was watching the chrono, face neutral. Kael was looking at the flowers by the fountain. Sera had her arms crossed, not defensively — structurally, the way she stood when she was holding herself in place. Torin was watching Ben with an expression that Ben didn't have the Force to read anymore, so he read it with his eyes instead: concern, or something that wore concern's face.

"Veraine ran a field test," Ben said. "The whole settlement. Not a localized pocket — the whole perimeter." He looked at the vine tips moving toward the dark, the flowers in the shadow, the dry fountain with its pale collar of wrong-blooming growth. "She wanted to know how large she could make it."

Nobody argued with him.

The Force was still there. He could feel it, vast and present and completely without direction, without the axis that had been as natural to him as knowing which way was down. He didn't reach for it again.

His own thoughts were very loud.

They were his. All of them. Including the ones that, until thirty seconds ago, the Force had always quietly softened before he had to hear them clearly.
