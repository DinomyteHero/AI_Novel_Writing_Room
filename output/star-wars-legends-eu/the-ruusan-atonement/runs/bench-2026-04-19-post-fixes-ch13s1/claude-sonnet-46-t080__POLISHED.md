Kolven looked like a vacation.

That was what Ben couldn't get past. Green hills terracing down to what had been a modest port town, blue sky with high clouds, the kind of afternoon light that made architecture look intentional. Through the *Steady Returns'* viewport it could have been a Core world weekend retreat, the sort of place his mother had occasionally threatened to take him before something more urgent always intervened.

"Atmospheric nitrogen at nominal," Desh said from the scan station. "Oxygen nominal. Temperature reading seventeen degrees." He paused. "Except the secondary sensor says eleven. Same location. Same timestamp."

"Instrument error?" Torin asked.

"Ran diagnostics twice. The instruments are fine." Desh pulled up the overlay without being asked. Two temperature curves, diverging by six degrees and widening. "They've been drifting apart since we crossed the outer marker."

Ben kept his hands loose on the co-pilot yoke. Below them, a bird wheeled over the settlement's eastern edge, and he tracked it for three seconds before his stomach registered what his eyes were already reporting. The bird was flying the wrong direction. Not against the wind — wrong, in some way that had nothing to do with wind. Its wingbeats were too slow for its altitude, its course a long arc that bent slightly away from where birds in that thermal should go.

He pulled his gaze back to the scans. "Take us down."

---

The duracrete of the main square was intact. That was almost worse — no rubble, no burn marks, no evidence of the kind of battle that left scars. The settlement had simply been abandoned. Planters still held soil. A child's comm unit lay in a doorway, screen dark, strap broken.

Sera was three steps ahead of him, turning slowly, cataloguing.

"There," she said.

The vine on the eastern wall of what had been a supply depot was climbing toward shadow. Not toward the afternoon sun on the building's south face — toward the overhang that blocked the light entirely. As Ben watched, a tendril at the leading edge moved. Not wind. Not settling. A slow, deliberate extension toward darkness.

"That's not growth response," Sera said. "Nothing in Kolven's botanical record grows photonegatively. The Ankhural survey was thorough."

"Maybe the survey missed something," Kael said. He was standing in the center of the square, hands in his jacket pockets, watching the vine with the flat attention of someone who has already decided the information is bad and is waiting to find out how bad. "Or maybe the survey was right and something changed the vine."

On the north face of the depot, flowers were open. White, small, the kind that needed direct sun. They were blooming in full shade, petals spread wide toward a wall.

Desh crouched by one of the planters and pressed two fingers against the soil. Then he stood, pulled out his chrono, and held it level.

The second hand skipped. Not a mechanical catch — a visible lurch, a quarter-second gap, then resumption. He watched it happen again.

"That's not the instrument," he said. He wasn't asking.

"No," Ben said. "It's not."

There was a low pressure behind his sternum that had been building since they crossed the outer marker, something that didn't correspond to anything he could point to. Not nausea. Not anxiety, exactly. His balance kept registing a drop that never arrived, a step down onto a surface that wasn't there.

He stopped cataloguing the wrongness and did what he should have done the moment they landed.

He reached for the Force.

---

It was there.

That was the first thing, and the most disorienting. He had half-braced for nothing, for the complete void of a Force-dead zone, and the Force wasn't dead. He could feel it — energy, presence, the immense living weight of it that had been the ground of his perception since he was four years old and his father had put a hand on his shoulder and told him to listen.

But there was no direction to it. No gradient. He reached and the Force responded and the response had nothing to measure itself against. No light, no dark, no pull toward the right choice or the quiet warning against the wrong one. He extended his senses and they moved through the Force the way a hand moves through water in the dark — present, surrounded, no surface to push against.

His trained instincts fired anyway. Every Jedi reflex he had spent a decade building — the threat assessment, the emotional read, the instinctive lean toward what the Force was asking — all of it activated and all of it returned nothing. Not static. Not interference. The absence of the thing the instincts were designed to find.

His arms felt hollow. The bones of them, specifically, in a way that made no anatomical sense and was entirely real.

"Ben." Sera's voice was careful.

He opened his eyes. He hadn't realized they'd closed.

"I know," he said.

"The Force is—"

"I know." He kept his voice even. "It's there. It just isn't — there's no axis. No register." He looked at her. Her face was very still, the kind of stillness she wore when she was holding something back with both hands. "You're getting the same thing."

"Yes." One word. She glanced at Torin.

Torin stood at the far edge of the square near the child's comm unit, and he was looking at his own hands as though they belonged to someone else. He was a man who had been commanding Jedi for forty years before the Ruusan Reformation sealed them both into stasis, and whatever he was feeling, he'd decided not to say it yet.

Kael said it instead.

"There's something in my chest," he said. "Grief. Except I have nothing to grieve. I've been trying to locate the object for the last ten minutes and there isn't one." He didn't sound frightened, exactly. He sounded like someone identifying an engine sound he'd never heard before. "Is that what you're all experiencing? Because I want to be clear that I'm not Force-sensitive, and whatever this is, it doesn't require Force sensitivity."

Desh's chrono skipped again. He watched it happen, put the chrono away, and said nothing.

Ben made himself stand still. His hands wanted to reach again — the reflex wasn't done with him — and he pressed them against his thighs instead and let the reflex fire into the same silence.

"She did this," Sera said. Not a question. "To a settlement world. As a demonstration." Her voice had gone flat in the way that meant the flatness was doing a lot of structural work. "There were people here."

"Looks like they left." Kael glanced at the child's comm unit. "Mostly."

The vine on the eastern wall extended another centimeter toward shadow while they stood there. The white flowers opened fractionally wider.

Ben looked at the vine, at the flowers, at Desh's face — calm, trained, operating on pure professional discipline while something in his chest grieved an object that no longer had a shape in the Force — and felt the full weight of what Veraine had done here. Not as an abstract. Not as a data point in a threat assessment. The weight of it settled into him the way cold settles into durasteel: slowly, then all at once.

"This is what she wants to do at Veranthos," Torin said. His voice was quiet. "At full scale."

"Yes," Ben said.

The Force moved around him, present and formless, neither asking anything of him nor offering anything to hold. He was standing in the middle of it and it was everywhere and he had nothing to compare it to, no way to take a bearing, no current to read.

And in that silence, he heard himself.

Not the voice he used when he was planning, when he was running threat assessments and calculating margins. The other thoughts. The ones the Force usually met before they finished forming — a gentle counterweight, a quiet re-orientation toward something larger than the thought itself. Those thoughts were his now, unmediated, arriving without the safety net he hadn't known he was using.

He heard them. All of them.

Including the ones he had been counting on the Force to soften.
