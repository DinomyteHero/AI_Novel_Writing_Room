Kolven filled the forward viewport in layers that should have meant comfort.

Blue water. Green continents. White cloud bands sliding in slow, clean curls over a temperate world that looked fit for orchard towns, schoolyards, and people who complained about weather because they had the luxury. From orbit, Veraine's field test looked like a tourism poster.

Desh ruined that in six words.

"Surface variance is getting worse."

He stood behind the cockpit seats, one hand braced on the bulkhead, datapad angled under the overhead glow. The ship's recycled air carried machine heat and old caf. Ben kept his eyes on the world anyway, as if staring hard enough might make the numbers surrender.

"Worse how?" Torin asked from the port station.

Desh flicked through the scan feed. "Temperature bands crossing each other. Groundwater signatures where there shouldn't be any. Plant density mapping changes every pass." He paused. "And the clock stamp on the sensor package is drifting."

Ben's hands tightened on the copilot yoke. Cold metal pressed into his palms. A faint coppery tang touched the back of his tongue, sharp and thin.

"Ship clock drift?" he asked.

"Sensor package only," Desh said. "For now."

"For now," Kael echoed from the jumpseat, dry enough to sand paint.

Sera leaned toward the viewport, posture rigid even in a crash webbing harness. "The world is outwardly stable."

"Outwardly," Ben said.

She gave him a brief look that might have been agreement if Sera Varik believed in wasting motion on obvious things.

The Steady Returns rolled into final descent. Cloud cover swallowed the world and gave them back a smear of white. The hull shivered. Repulsorlifts changed pitch. Ben reached, more from habit than intent, brushing at the Force for the easy situational map he'd used since he was old enough to know the difference between instinct and training.

He got enough to know they had crossed something.

Not a wall. Not a pressure line. Just a moment where every sense inside that reach slipped half a step and failed to settle. The reaction came back to him stripped bare, energy without bearings. No warning. No comfort. No shape he knew what to do with.

He let go of the yoke for a second, flexed his fingers, and put them back.

"You got that too," Torin said quietly.

Ben glanced over. Torin's usual warmth had gone careful around the edges.

"Yeah," Ben said.

Sera's gaze stayed forward. "So the reports were not exaggerations."

Kael snorted. "Nice when the nightmare turns out honest."

The ship broke through the cloud deck. A settlement spread below them near an inland sea, streets still visible in a tidy grid, buildings standing in pale blocks among trees and climbing green. No blast scars. No bombardment glass. No obvious ruin. Ben saw a central square, a tower with half its skin peeled back, rows of homes with roofs intact.

Then he noticed there were no movement trails. No speeders. No smoke. No birds lifting off the rooftops at the sound of an incoming ship.

No birds at all.

"You'd think something would scatter," he murmured.

Desh looked up from the datapad. "There's animal biomass on scan."

"Where?"

Desh's mouth tightened. "That's the question."

Ben brought them down on the cracked pad at the edge of the square. The landing struts kissed duracrete. Engines wound lower, then lower again, until the cockpit filled with the ship's small interior noises—the ventilation hiss, a relay click, the faint vibration that never quite left an old vessel.

Nobody moved right away.

Ben stared through the viewport at a line of vine-covered facades. One cluster of white flowers climbed the north face of a building sunk in shade. Another patch on the sunlit side had browned and folded in on itself. He watched one tendril on a lower wall uncurl, hesitate, and begin creeping downward, against the grain of every climbing plant he had ever seen.

"All right," he said, because silence was getting ideas. "Let's go make bad decisions in person."

Kael gave him a sidelong look. "That passes for Jedi optimism now?"

"It's premium-grade optimism," Ben said. "Comes with a robe surcharge."

Torin barked a short laugh. It helped for about two seconds.

They exited through the main hatch in a staggered line. The first breath outside tasted damp, mineral, and faintly metallic, like rainwater left too long in a pipe. Air touched warm across Ben's face and cold over his hands at the same time. The contrast prickled.

The landing pad's duracrete sat level under his boots, but his body argued with it. His inner ear kept expecting a drop that never came. Pressure gathered behind his sternum, low and steady, not pain but something near it.

Desh came down the ramp last with a field kit slung over one shoulder and turned in a slow circle, scanning roofs, alleys, windows. Pure habit. Human habit. Useful habit.

"Still no birds," Ben said.

"No insects either," Desh replied. "I should hear something."

Sera had already moved toward the nearest wall overgrown with dark green runners. She stopped with one gloved hand an inch from a flowering stem. "Observe."

Ben joined her. The blossoms opened from the shaded side of the wall, pale violet cups drinking darkness instead of light. A vine above them tightened, then eased in the opposite direction, reversing its curl while he watched.

Torin came up beside him and stared. "That's wrong enough to be impressive."

"Biology does not improvise," Sera said. "It follows structure."

Kael crouched near a crack in the pavement where a net of roots had shoved through. "Apparently not here."

Ben looked down the street. Buildings stood intact except for weathering and the stubborn takeover of plant life. Balconies sagged. Signage hung crooked in a script too worn to read at this distance. A child's repulsor toy lay half-swallowed in moss near the curb. The place had not been destroyed. It had been left behind by rules.

He started toward the square. The others followed, boots clicking over old pavement and scattering grit. The silence pressed harder there between the buildings. No wind moved the leaves, but a curtain of ivy kept inching along brickwork in patient, visible progress.

Desh muttered, "I hate this place."

"That makes two of us," Ben said.

"Five," Kael said.

Sera did not answer. For her, agreement was apparently too generous for the situation.

The central square opened around a dry fountain choked with ferns. Ben stopped near the basin. The old civic buildings around it had the same dead-still look as everything else, but the greenery refused to hold still. He watched a branch along the council hall's roofline bend toward shadow by fractions. Not fast. Fast would almost have been easier. This was slow enough to make his eyes doubt themselves.

He drew a breath and let it out.

"Ben," Torin said.

A warning. Gentle. He knew what Ben was about to do.

"We came here for this," Ben said.

"We came here to see," Sera corrected.

Ben gave her a quick look. "Right. And I'm planning to see very hard."

That got him the faintest twitch at the corner of Torin's mouth. Not Sera. She watched him the way a commander watched someone volunteer to test a minefield with his own boot.

He planted his feet on the cracked stones of the square and reached for the Force.

The answer came at once.

Power. Presence. Immensity close enough to drown in.

And nothing to stand on.

The ground of sense dropped out from under the reach. Every trained instinct in him fired and came back empty of direction. No lift toward the light. No recoil from shadow. No grain to follow. No current. No edge. The Force was there, fully there, but stripped of every line that had ever told him where he was inside it.

His breath caught hard enough to hurt.

He did not stagger. Barely. The hollowness hit first in his arms, deep in the bones from wrist to shoulder, as if the strength had been scooped out and only shape remained. Then his thoughts got loud.

Not clearer. Louder.

A quick ugly inventory flashed through him with no familiar cushioning around it: how easily he could take control of this group if he stopped asking; how tired he was of Sera questioning him; how much of Torin's warmth made people follow him without noticing; how fast fear could turn Kael into dead weight; how Desh was the only one here who had no choice but trust the rest of them and how dangerous that made all of this. The thoughts came clean and sharp and entirely his.

Ben cut the reach off like he'd grabbed live current.

The square lurched back into ordinary sight. Duracrete. Fountain. Vines. Air.

"Ben?" Torin said, closer now.

He held up one hand without looking. Wait.

The pressure behind his sternum had become a cavity. He stared at the fountain's dry basin until the stone edges stopped trying to tilt.

Sera's voice reached him from his left, controlled to the point of strain. "Describe."

"No." The word came rough. He swallowed and tried again. "Not yet."

That silenced even her.

He had spent his whole life with the Force as context, correction, friction when he started sliding where he shouldn't. Not a voice. Not a set of spoken rules. Something older and steadier than that, woven through choice and consequence until the difference between his judgment and its pressure had never needed naming.

Here, that armature was gone.

Raw power remained. Raw potential. But the part that told him what a thing meant, what it cost, what it leaned toward—that was absent. Not damaged. Absent.

His hands wanted to rise again, to test it, to make sure. He locked them at his sides.

Desh stepped up on his right, already pulling a chrono from his jacket pocket. "I've got something."

The analog second hand twitched, advanced two marks, froze, then jumped backward one.

All five of them watched it.

It skipped again.

Desh turned the chrono over, checked the casing, smacked it once against his palm, and held it out. The second hand crawled, hesitated, and snapped ahead.

"Well," Kael said into the quiet, "that's rude."

Desh did not take his eyes off the dial. "This chrono was calibrated three days ago."

"Mechanical failure?" Torin asked.

Desh shook his head once. "Not like that."

Kael had gone very still. Ben looked over and saw the other man pressing the heel of one hand against the center of his chest, more puzzled than alarmed.

"What?" Ben asked.

Kael glanced at him. "You ever wake up knowing somebody died, then spend an hour figuring out who?"

"No," Ben said.

"Lucky you." Kael lowered his hand, frowned at nothing. "This is that. Except there's no somebody."

The words landed harder than the drifting chrono.

Desh looked up at him. "You too?"

Kael nodded. "Started on descent. Worse here."

Desh exhaled through his nose. "Good. Not good. But good." He tapped the chrono face. "I've got measurable drift and a hole under my ribs for no reason. Means I'm not imagining either of them."

Sera turned a slow circle, studying the buildings, the vines, the dead fountain, each of them in turn. "The zone affects more than Force perception."

"No kidding," Ben said.

She ignored the tone. "Veraine did this to test scale."

Torin looked out across the square at the intact town wrapped in wrong-growing green. His voice had lost its easy warmth. "A settlement world. Not a battlefield. Not a sealed ruin. People lived here."

Ben followed his gaze to the rows of homes beyond the square. Someone had planted those ornamental trees along the street. Someone had chosen the tiles around the fountain. Someone had expected a future here long enough to build civic pride into stone.

Veraine had used it as a trial run.

His stomach clenched. He kept his hands flat against his thighs.

"If she can spread this," Desh said, "what happens in a population center?"

Nobody answered. They did not need to.

Ben knew what a Jedi usually did in a moment like this. Reach deeper. Trust the Force. Let it settle the noise and sort fear from fact.

He looked at the vine on the council hall wall as it slowly turned itself toward shade and did not reach.

Inside the silence where that reflex should have gone, his own mind stood up with nothing around it to blunt the edges.

Sera was right too often and enjoyed it too much. Torin would sacrifice everyone's choice if he could call it protection. Kael was already measuring exits. Desh did not belong in this and Ben had brought him anyway. Ben himself wanted command because command meant he would not have to admit he was scared.

The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
