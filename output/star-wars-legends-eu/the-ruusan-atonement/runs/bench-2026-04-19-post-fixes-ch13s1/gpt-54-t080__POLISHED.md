Kolven filled the forward viewport in bands of green and blue so clean they might have come out of a travel bureau holo. White cloud over broad water. Dark forests. A temperate world with room to breathe.

Desh, strapped into the jumpseat behind Ben, looked down at the scanner display in his hand and said, "Surface temperature at the primary settlement grid just dropped six degrees in twenty seconds."

Ben kept his eyes on the world. "Maybe Kolven's trying a new climate policy."

"No," Desh said. "No, this is the part where I tell you the humidity is rising at the same time, plant respiration is spiking in darkness, and the chrono sync with shipboard time just lost three-point-two seconds."

That took some of the humor out of it.

Ben tightened his grip on the co-pilot yoke as the Steady Returns rolled into descent. The metal bit cold against his palms. Recycled air moved through the cockpit with its usual dry tang, but a sharp taste touched the back of his tongue anyway, thin and metallic, like he'd put a credit chip in his mouth on a dare. He swallowed. It stayed.

Ahead, the settlement zone resolved from neat geometric marks on the display into actual streets and blocks. Buildings stood where they should. Rooflines unbroken. No scorch scars. No bombardment craters. From orbit it looked abandoned, not ruined.

Sera stood behind the pilot seats, one hand braced on the bulkhead. Her posture never seemed to change with turbulence. "Veraine wanted a controlled example," she said. "Not spectacle."

Torin glanced back at her from the port console. "You say that like it helps."

"It clarifies intent."

"Wonderful. I always enjoy having the enemy's intent clarified."

Kael, folded into the corner near the hatch as if the ship had grown around him, said, "I'm with Torin. I prefer my apocalyptic experiments less tidy."

That got the briefest curve from Desh's mouth. Ben took the landing vector lower.

As they crossed the upper atmosphere, the world on the viewport shifted from distant beautiful to immediate wrong. Not because the colors changed. Because the numbers kept refusing them.

"Wind shear just reversed," Desh said.

Ben frowned at the display. "At this altitude?"

"It reversed twice."

"Can weather panic?" Torin asked.

Desh flicked him a dry look. "If it can, we're flying through it."

The settlement spread beneath them on the edge of a shallow valley, streets laid in a practical grid around a central square. Trees crowded one side. Farmland, or what had been farmland, ringed the rest. Patches of green lay too thick in some places and bare in others, the pattern wrong in a way Ben couldn't file fast enough. His body caught it before his mind did. That low pressure behind his sternum settled in and stayed, not pain exactly, more like his chest had become aware of weight it couldn't identify.

He reached, lightly, toward the Force out of habit.

Something answered.

He pulled back at once.

Not fear. Reflex. His hand had gone near flame and returned before the burn.

"Ben?" Torin asked.

"Later," Ben said.

He hated how flat that sounded.

The Steady Returns touched down in the main square with a muted thump through the deck. Repulsors whined down. Outside the viewport, empty buildings watched them with blank windows and curtains of green spilling over balconies and walls. Nothing moved across the square. No people. No droids. No birds lifting out of the eaves.

That absence landed harder than it should have. On any abandoned world you expected scavengers, winged pests, something opportunistic enough to inherit what people left behind. Here there was only stillness and the soft patter of wind dragging leaves over duracrete.

Desh unstrapped first. "I'm going to enjoy being wrong about all of this."

"Optimism from you?" Ben asked.

"Professional aspiration."

They geared up fast. Ben clipped his lightsaber to his belt, checked the seal on his jacket, and followed the others down the ramp.

The air outside hit cooler than the viewport image had promised. Damp, too, with loam and green growth and the mineral smell of old concrete after rain, though the square looked dry. The sky overhead was bright, pale blue, harmless. A warm breeze touched the left side of his face while cold air brushed the right.

Ben stopped at the foot of the ramp.

The square had a fountain at its center, long dead and webbed with vines. Shops and apartment blocks ringed it in low rows of prefabricated permacrete and local stone. Most doors hung open. Plants had taken the gaps, but not the way plants should. Ivy climbed the shaded sides of buildings in thick braided ropes while the sunlit walls held only scattered leaves. White flowers bloomed under awnings and in the mouths of alleys where light barely reached. In the open planters, stems leaned away from the sun.

Sera crouched beside one of the planters, studying a spill of broad leaves with military severity, as if she expected them to confess. "Wrong side," she said.

Ben followed her gaze. The vine had wrapped itself around a support post and was moving.

Not fast. Not enough for anyone to call it a lunge. But while he watched, one tendril loosened, slid back on itself, and began to wind the opposite direction.

His balance dipped. The square stayed level. Duracrete remained under his boots, solid and flat, but his body insisted on a step that wasn't there. He caught it before he moved, annoyed at himself for almost staggering in front of everyone.

Torin saw anyway. He came up beside Ben, warm presence, easy voice pitched lower than usual. "You all right?"

"Ask me after I decide whether the plants are doing that for intimidation value."

Torin snorted. "If so, it's working."

Kael passed them on the way to the fountain. "Show-off vegetation. You hate to see it."

That should have eased some of the pressure. It barely dented it.

Desh had gone still near the ramp. He wasn't looking at the buildings. He was looking at nothing Ben could identify, head slightly tilted the way he did when listening to a distant transmission. Then he shook himself and unhooked a compact chrono from his belt.

Sera straightened. "No wildlife."

Ben listened. Wind. Leaves rubbing. The ship's metal cooling with soft ticks behind them.

"No insects, either," Desh said quietly.

That was worse.

Ben walked toward the center of the square, each step loud on the duracrete. His senses kept trying to sort the place into categories they knew. Abandoned colony. Environmental contamination. Dark side nexus. Some old bioweapon gone feral. Every possibility arrived with the expectation of a shape behind it. A direction. This felt like reaching for a railing in the dark and closing his hand on empty air.

No. Not railing. The whole image was wrong.

The ground of sense was gone.

He stopped beside the dead fountain.

Vines curtained half the basin. Water stains marked the stone where a spray had once arced. Someone had carved names into the lip long ago, settlers making sure they would be remembered by a world that had not asked for them. The letters were half-hidden by moss growing thickest on the side facing away from the sun.

Ben set his boots square. Breathed once. Deliberately reached for the Force.

It was there.

Not diminished. Not blocked. Not wounded in the way he had known wounds before.

Present. Vast. Immediate.

And without any axis.

His training moved first, every practiced instinct of a lifetime: locate, orient, read. The response came back with nothing to catch on. No grain. No pull. No answer to the simplest questions a Jedi never had to speak aloud because the Force had always carried them under everything else. Safe or unsafe. Help or harm. Step forward or stop. The current existed. The banks were gone.

For one jarring instant his mind kept searching for light and dark and found neither. Not balance. Not blur. The structure that made the distinction had been lifted clean away.

His arms hollowed out to the bone. Not numbness. More frightening than numbness. Space where certainty had always sat. His next breath scraped going in.

He opened his eyes. He hadn't noticed closing them.

The square remained the square. White flowers under shadow. A tendril unwinding itself. Torin three meters to his right, hand halfway toward him and not touching. Sera motionless near the planter, face gone harder, as if hardness could brace against this. Kael at the fountain's far side, watching Ben with the expression he wore when bad odds had finally stopped pretending to be decent. Desh near the ramp with the chrono in his hand.

No one had changed.

Everything had changed.

Ben let the connection snap back to the smallest thread he could manage and even that was wrong, the way stepping onto a stair that should exist and didn't leaves the body falling for a fraction too long. His stomach turned. He locked his hands at his sides because they wanted to reach again, to test, to force a better answer out of it, and that impulse had his own voice all over it.

"Ben," Sera said.

He looked at her.

She had gone pale under the bronze of her skin. "You touched it."

Not a question.

"Yes."

"What did you find?"

He searched for words and got none he trusted. Not because the thing lacked them. Because every phrase that came to mind tried to tame it into something familiar, and familiar was the one lie this place wouldn't allow.

"Nothing I know how to use," he said.

Torin exhaled through his nose. "That bad."

Ben gave him a look. "That clear."

Desh crouched and held up the chrono. The second hand ticked twice, jerked backward one mark, then jumped ahead three.

No one spoke for a beat.

"Well," Kael said, "that seems unhealthy."

Desh didn't look up from the dial. "Shipboard sync says I lost another second." He tapped the casing, then checked it against his wrist unit. "Both are doing it."

Torin moved closer to him. "Equipment fault?"

"Five pieces of equipment faulting in different ways at the same time, all inside a hundred meters of the square?" Desh shook his head. "I'd rather take the haunted-force-wound option. It's cleaner."

Ben almost told him not to say haunted. The word belonged to superstitious cargo crews and Outer Rim taverns, not to this. But the correction would have been for his own comfort, and comfort had stopped being useful the second he touched the Force here.

Kael had one hand pressed flat to the edge of the fountain. Not gripping. Anchoring. "Anybody else got a weight in the chest for no reason?"

Sera turned to him. "Describe it."

"No." He kept his eyes on the basin. "I'm not doing your formal-report voice right now. There's grief sitting under my ribs and I can't tell you whose funeral I'm meant to be attending."

The square seemed to contract around that sentence.

Desh looked up at last, his expression stripped down to professional plainness. "I thought it was just me."

Ben's stomach clenched hard enough to hurt.

Not just Jedi, then. Not some specialized failure of Force perception he could box up and claim as his problem. The place reached into people whether they had language for it or not. It took hold where moral shape ought to be and left the body searching for what had been removed.

Veraine had done this as a field test.

A colony world. Intact buildings. Empty streets. Plants learning the wrong lesson from sunlight while the people who had named the fountain were gone.

Sera stepped into the square proper now, slow and deliberate, as though she expected the ground to object. "She measured output here," she said. "Scale. Duration. Stability."

Torin's head came up. "You're sure?"

"She wanted to know whether a settled environment could hold after de-structuring." Sera looked at the flowers in the shade, the vine reversing itself on the post. "It held."

"That's your word for this?" Torin asked. "Held?"

"It remained."

"Comforting distinction."

Sera ignored the jab. Her focus had narrowed to the environment, to function, because function at least could still be named. Ben knew the move. He was making his own version of it by staying upright and breathing evenly and not letting anyone see how badly he wanted the old reflexive map of the Force back.

He listened for it anyway.

Nothing answered in the register he knew.

There was power here. Presence. Enough to drown in. But no moral contour rode beneath it. No instinctive warning. No quiet pressure steering his hand away from a choice before he made it. The space where that guidance had always lived was bare, and into that bare place his thoughts came through with a new hard edge.

He could leave now.

The thought arrived crisp and clean.

Get everyone back on the ship. Mark the coordinates. Call the Order. Hand it upward. Let older people with broader mandates and committees and very official worries deal with the impossible thing Veraine had built.

Another thought followed right behind it, just as clean.

And if they moved too slowly, Veraine would open Veranthos while the Council debated language.

Ben stood very still in the middle of the square, with the dead fountain at his back and the wrong-growing world around him, and heard the argument continue inside his own head without any softening pressure under it, no gentle correction, no quiet nudge toward the better version of himself. Only his own mind, sharp as cut glass, offering him options and costs and selfish relief alongside duty with exactly the same calm.

The thoughts were his. All of them. Including the ones the Force usually softened.
